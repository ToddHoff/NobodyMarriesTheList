## Chapter 11

**Jo**

Kevin texted on Friday night, and the next morning my son was in the white truck with me, on his way to a client's house.

*Graduation's tomorrow. I'm on the field at 6:30 and done around 2. He can sit in the bleachers or stay with you. Whatever works for you.*

It was my week. On my Sunnyvale Saturdays Kevin takes him Friday night, and that has worked since May.

Denise would have taken him. So would my father. I would have had to ask, and I had a truck with an empty seat.

I lay awake a while anyway. Ten days ago I had kissed that client on a curb in Los Gatos. Last Saturday I had sat on a bench in his mother's atrium with my knee against his and left it there through a whole sandwich. Now I was bringing my son.

Vili took the sixteen-footer and Manny. I followed them down 101.

“Dad says graduation's been on the calendar since August,” Owen said.

“It has.”

“He says he sends you the calendar every year.”

“He does. Say the rules.”

I have never opened it. It comes every August, and I file it. I know when Owen's school starts and when it lets out, so the rest of Kevin's year is Kevin's. This year the rest of it had a graduation in it. He had sent it like always. I'd had since August to find out. I found out last night.

“I stay where you can see me. Everything in the house is somebody's, so I touch what you hand me.” He turned nine in February, and he recites like his father. “And I don't ask the client for anything.”

“Not a glass of water.”

“Vili says the client is Cal Brennan.”

“The client is a man with a house in Sunnyvale.”

“He made Remy.”

“I know what he made. It answers my phone.”

“You don't even talk to it.”

“I don't. Put the phone away.”

“It's my phone. Dad gave it to me.”

“It was your father's phone, and now it's yours, and it stays in your pocket in that house.”

“What if he wants to see my app?”

“He won't.”

“He might. I made it with his thing.”

My son talks to that thing every day. He tells it what he wants and argues with it when it's wrong. I have never said one word to it.

“If he wants to see it, he'll ask you,” I said. “You don't ask him.”

“What if I just show him? Showing isn't asking.”

“Owen.”

“Vili says he has a dog, and it leaned on you.”

“Vili talks. The dog stays on his side of the glass, and you stay on yours.”

The app is dogs. It has every dog he has ever met, in order. I'm not good with dogs, and my son has a list you have to scroll.

“You swear at his billboard every time we drive under it,” he said.

“I quit.”

I had. On Monday I drove under forty-eight feet of that man and checked my hair in the mirror. Then I fixed it.

***

Cal Brennan was at the curb three minutes early on Saturday morning, with three paper bags.

His car was across the street, with the fender the way I had left it. Vili took the driveway in one pass, and he looked at me in his mirror while he did it.

He'd moved his car out of reach of my trucks. My carrier was paying for that fender. I'd filed on Monday. A man like that can have a headlight put in by lunch. He hadn't. I didn't know what that was about. I wasn't going to be the one who brought it up.

He had on the gray shirt, and the boots had a month on them now. The sun was low enough to come straight down Remington, and it was in his hair. He watched my truck all the way to the curb, and I had to look at the parking brake to get it set.

Two of the bags were folded over twice and creased. The third was rolled shut.

“Which one's yours?” I said.

He held up the rolled one.

“You made it.”

“I made it at 5:40. It took nineteen minutes.”

I opened it. I check his boxes, and I checked this. He had used about a yard of foil. Inside, everything was squared to the crust, and the tomato was up against the bread.

“By eleven that slice is soup,” I said. “Next time butter the bread. Both pieces, out to the edge. It keeps the wet off.”

“Butter is a barrier.”

“Butter's a barrier.”

“Nobody told me that.”

“My father told me. I was seven.”

He was watching the foil in my hands and waiting to hear how he had done. My stomach went over the hill, at a curb, with my son ten feet away.

“It's square,” I said, and gave it back.

Owen had come down on his own side. He was looking at the man and then up the street, which is what he does with anything he has been told not to stare at.

“This is Owen. His father has a graduation to run, so he's with me today. He stays in the atrium, and he touches what I hand him.” I had the rest ready. “I'm taking a hundred and fifty off the day.”

“What is that for?”

“You're paying for three of us. Part of me is going to be watching him.”

He opened his mouth and shut it.

“All right,” he said.

I'd done that number lying awake. That man brings three lunches when you tell him two. My son was going to be in his house for four hours. If Owen came home with anything, a glass of water or a turn with the dog, I wanted it paid for before it was given. I still hadn't found that man's piano. I didn't want my son to be the one standing in front of it.

He put out his hand to my son. Kevin taught Owen to shake, one pump, looking at the man.

“I am Cal Brennan.”

“I know.” Owen had to look a long way up. “You don't sell software. You sell an hour.”

I had heard that across a table in Los Gatos, and I had told the man he was presenting. My son had it by heart.

Cal's shoulders started down. I had watched them do that for a woman at a bar. He looked at me, and they came back up.

“That is the short version,” he said. “The whole of it runs four minutes.”

“Four minutes and ten seconds.”

“It runs four minutes and ten seconds,” Cal said.

He said it in the voice he uses on me, the one with the numbers in it.

I'd have bet the truck he would present to my son. Owen would have loved it. My face got warm, out there on the curb. Then I was scared. My son had known that man off a screen. Now he was going to know the real one. I had brought him here myself.

“My mom used to swear at your billboard.”

“She said it was easier when I was forty-eight feet wide.”

I'd said that five weeks ago, over a plate of shrimp. I'd been sure it went right by him. He had it word for word. Owen looked up at me to see whether it was true. I let him look and went up the walk.

The front door of that house opens on the atrium. Owen touched the frame twice going through. He does that at every house. Cal watched him do it. He didn't ask, and I never have.

From the atrium you can see through the front room and out the back glass. The dog was lying against the back door with his nose on the track.

Owen took the phone out, and he looked at me first.

“I'm not asking him anything,” he said. “I'm showing him.”

“Thirty seconds,” I said.

Showing isn't asking. He got that from me. I don't ask anybody for a job. I put the estimate on the hood and let them read it.

“What is it?” Cal said.

“Dogs I've met. In order. I made it with Remy.”

“What does it sort on?”

“What I say. He kept putting them in alphabetical.”

“What did you tell it?”

“That alphabetical isn't an order. It's just the alphabet.”

Cal looked at my son for a while.

“May I see it?”

Owen turned the phone so that he could, and kept hold of it. Cal went down the screen with one finger.

“Who is Truck Dog?”

“He's first. I saw him on a truck on 101. He was standing up on the load, and he wasn't even holding on.”

“Did you meet him?”

“I saw him.”

“It says dogs you have met.”

“He was before the rule.”

“I was going to do volcanoes first,” Owen said. “My grandpa got stuck in the ash from Mount St. Helens. But you can't meet a volcano around here.”

My father tells that one every Christmas.

“Lassen Peak is in California,” Cal said. “It last erupted in 1917.”

“That's asleep. It doesn't count.”

“And does that one count?” he said, and nodded at the glass.

Owen looked at the dog for about four seconds.

“He's second.”

“You have not met him either.”

“That's why I have to.” He turned around to me. “Can I ask him if I can go out there?”

“No. He's a client. And the dog stays on his side of the glass.”

Owen put the phone in his pocket. He didn't argue. He goes quiet when he's told no, and that part he gets from his father. I've watched for it in him since he could walk.

Cal looked at me, and then at his dog. He kept quiet too.

Any other client would have said it was fine. They always do. He's no trouble, let him come out, and then I'm saying no twice, to my own kid, in their house. Cal had his own dog on the other side of his own glass. He let my no stand. He wanted to say yes. It was all over his face.

I hate that quiet on Owen. I have never found one thing to say to it that works.

I gave Owen the tape gun and a stack of flat boxes, and I went to do the hall bathroom.

I do bathrooms by myself, with the door open. A medicine cabinet is nobody's business. The bottles go in a bag for the pharmacy drop box, and I don't read a label out loud. The family gets an empty shelf.

It's the quietest job in a house. Nobody comes in, because nobody wants to watch you empty a dead woman's medicine cabinet, and I like it for that. It was already warm in there. The sun comes through the high window over the tub, and there was dust on the sill thick enough to write in.

She'd had one toothbrush. The towel on the bar was worn thin enough to see my hand through. In the cupboard there were six good ones, folded in thirds with the fold facing out. Everybody has good towels. I have boxed a thousand of them, and not one had ever been wet.

I taped the box and wrote BATH on it and stood there a minute with the marker. I was thinking about a bench, and a knee I hadn't moved. I made myself think about towels.

Voices carry in that house. It's glass on a slab.

“Mind the teeth,” Cal said, out in the atrium. “I cut my thumb on that the first day.”

“I've been doing it since I was six.”

“I was forty-one.”

My son laughed out loud. He does not laugh for strangers.

I couldn't see Owen from the bathroom. That breaks rule one. I didn't go out. If I went out there they would stop. I wanted to hear what my son would say to that man when I wasn't standing there.

“You have looked at my dog nine times,” Cal said. “What is the ask?”

“I'm not allowed to ask you anything. You're a client.”

Cal took a second with that.

“If you were allowed, how many questions would there be?”

“Eleven. One is can I pet your dog.”

“I see. Then tell me what is wrong with this box.”

“There's no H on the bottom.”

“Your mother taught me the H.”

“She taught me first.”

I was standing in a dead woman's bathroom with a towel in each hand. I felt it low in my chest, and I stayed where I was until it let go.

The last thing in any bathroom is the back of the door. The family never thinks of it. I swung it shut to get the robe.

It was blue terry cloth on a brass hook. I went through the pockets, because I go through every pocket. The left had a tissue in it. The right had a blue ballpoint pen.

I lifted the robe off the hook, and there was writing on the door behind it.

It was two columns of short lines in blue ballpoint, each with a name and a number. The left ones said CALVIN, from 4 up to 12. The right ones said ABBY. They started at 3, down by my hip, and the last one said 14 and was over my head.

Abby was the daughter who named the dog. She was in six of the frames out in the hall. Nobody had to see this door but me. I could have hung the robe back on its hook and let the door go with the house. Whoever bought it would paint it.

I don't decide anything in that house without the owner in the room.

“Cal.”

He came down the hall with his cuffs turned back. Owen came behind him. I had told him to stay where I could see him, and he will argue that he did.

Cal looked at the left column first.

“Those are mine,” he said. “I stopped standing for it at twelve.”

“And the others?”

“I did not know about the others.”

He put a finger on the line that said ABBY 3 and went up them one at a time.

“There are twelve,” he said. “I was not here for any of them.”

He said it the way he gives a time, to the minute. His finger stayed on the 14.

Kevin was at every one of Owen's games. I have said that to anybody who asked, the way you say a man pays his child support. Standing in that bathroom I was glad of it.

“Who's Abby?” Owen said.

“She is my daughter.”

“How old is she?”

“She is eighteen.”

Owen found the line that said 9. He backed up against the door under it and stood up straight.

I laid my hand flat on the top of his head. My father used to do that to me against the frame of the garage door.

“Half an inch over,” I said.

He turned around and looked. Then he put his own hand up to the line that said 10. He couldn't reach it.

“Mark mine.”

“It's not our door.”

“It is mine,” Cal said. He held out his hand. “May I?”

He meant the pencil. I took it from behind my ear and gave it to him.

Owen got back under my hand. Cal drew the line along the edge of it, and the pencil went past the side of my finger, close enough that I felt it go. Then he wrote beside the line in capitals, slowly. He took a long time over the O. OWEN 9.

The last hand on my own head had been his. The first night, at the Pied Piper, with my own tape. I'd told myself it was nothing like my father's. This time my hand was on my son's head. The man had just run my pencil along my finger. My throat went tight. I kept my eyes on the door.

“That's carpenter's pencil,” I said. “It won't rub out.”

“I know. I have tried.”

“Green or blue?” I said.

“It is a door.”

“Doors come off.”

“Green.”

He tore the tape clean and put it on the door, up above everybody's head.

Green goes with the owner. Wherever he took that door, my son's name was going with it, in my pencil. It was the fifth Saturday of ten.

“Boxes,” I told Owen, and he went.

That left the two of us in the hall where he had held up his grandmother's hutch. He still had my pencil.

“He has looked at my dog fourteen times now,” Cal said. “He has eleven questions for me, and he informed me that he is not allowed to ask one, because I am a client.”

“That's my rule.”

“May he hold the leash? In the back yard. I will be on the other end. I am asking you first.”

I'd lain awake over what he would give my son, so I could pay for it ahead of time. He hadn't offered Owen a thing.

“The back yard,” I said. “You stay on the leash with him.”

“Yes.”

He held out the pencil. I didn't take it. I had stood in that hall with him once before, with a hutch for an excuse, and I had not kissed him since a curb in Los Gatos ten days ago. I had thought about it every day since, including at three in the morning in my own kitchen.

“It goes behind your ear,” he said. “May I?”

“Go ahead.”

He put it there. He was careful about it, and his fingers stayed in my hair after the pencil was in. I felt it all the way down my back.

My son was in the atrium with a tape gun, thirty feet away. I knew exactly where he was. I took hold of the front of the man's shirt anyway.

The top of my head comes to his collarbone, and I can't reach his mouth from the floor.

“Bend down,” I said.

“I have not asked whether I may kiss you,” he said.

“I know,” I said. “I'm telling you.”

He bent down.

I pulled him the rest of the way by his shirt, and I kissed him. His hand went into my hair, and the tie came half out. His other hand found the small of my back and stayed there. He kissed me slower than he had on the curb, and my knees went loose. I held on to the shirt. For about a minute I quit listening for my son. I don't do that.

“Mom.”

Owen was at the end of the hall, where Vili had stood with the dolly. He had the phone in one hand and the tape gun in the other.

“You said he was a client.”

“I did.”

“So is he a client or a friend? Because if he's a friend, I can ask him.”

My hand was still on the man's shirt, and his heart was going under it.

Vili had asked me nearly the same thing from that spot three weeks ago. Helping, or a client. I hadn't had an answer for him.

Owen was going to get one. He had eleven questions in him and a rule against every one of them.

“Friend,” I said. “Go ahead and ask him.”
