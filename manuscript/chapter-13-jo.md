## Chapter 13

**Jo**

I sat in my truck in Cal Brennan's parking garage for forty minutes, reading his texts.

I have sent men home for sitting in a truck on my time.

I got off his elevator, walked to my truck and shut the door. I waited to cry. I didn't. I wanted a dolly and a flight of stairs.

Upstairs I had stood at the edge of his floor and watched him stop a thing that was going out to forty million phones on Thursday. He did it in one sentence, at the volume I use to tell Vili which truck. It was about the boxes he'd sent his mother. The last one was still on her hearth with no tape on it.

I'd stood there with my clipboard and been proud of him, like he was one of my crew. Then I asked him Owen's other question like it was nothing. I had already told my son the answer.

After a while I took out my phone.

There were thirty-two. I went through them the way I go through a closet, every one, out loud.

*Thank you for today. At eight you read three labels I would rather nobody had read. You didn't look at me once.*

“Hers,” I said.

*Noted. That is five.*

“His.”

*Tell Denise nobody has ever announced me from a window. I liked it.*

“Hers.”

*You know a house is empty when it starts answering your footsteps.*

“Hers.”

I had read that one to Vili at a red light on Grand Avenue. I had thought about it in every empty house since.

The one about the dog I didn't read out loud. I had read it three times in my kitchen the first night. *He leans on the people he intends to keep.* I'd thought that was about me. A woman I met an hour ago wrote it.

I said “Hers” anyway. I made myself.

He had told me that nine were his. I counted nine. They were short, every one with a number or a time in it. Not one of them was nice. I read the nine again.

At *Noted. That is five* I laughed out loud. I was as mad as I've been at anybody since the divorce, alone in a parking garage with my hand over my mouth like somebody might hear.

A car came down the ramp from the level above me. It was going slow. It was gray, with its front fender pushed in and no headlight on that side. I knew that fender. I put it there.

I slid down in my seat.

I am forty-four years old. I own three trucks and sign seven paychecks. I slid down behind my own steering wheel until I was looking at the bottom of the dash. I stayed there until I heard him go out past the gate arm.

He didn't stop, though he has walked me to that truck twice and it has my father's name on the door. If he saw it, he was doing what I told him.

I sat back up. My hair had come out of its tie on the headrest.

Ten days ago I had backed the sixteen-footer into that fender because I was looking at him in my mirror. He never said one word about it. I had to put a form in front of him before he'd sign anything. I'd liked him for it ever since. Now I sat there and wondered whether there had been a card for that.

The phone rang in my hand. It was Dawn Pruitt.

“Joanna,” she said. “He left me nine minutes on my machine.”

“When?”

“At 10:41, from his car. He says you know who writes his messages.”

That was about when I was looking at the bottom of my dash.

“Did you know?” I said.

“I knew he had someone. At that level they all have someone.”

I heard her pour coffee.

She said it the way I'd say every kitchen has a junk drawer. At my level I sign the paychecks on Fridays with my sister standing over me. Nobody puts my name on anything but me.

“You did divorces for fifteen years,” I said.

“You know I did.”

“The men who sent the most flowers. How did they do in a divorce?”

“I had one bring a binder,” Dawn said. “Every bracelet. The night he drove four hours in the rain. Dates and prices.”

“For what?”

“To show what she owed him.”

“Did she?”

“She thought they were presents.”

Back then Dawn sent me one of those wives, out of a four-bedroom in Hillsborough. She packed her bracelets in a shoebox herself and wouldn't let the crew carry it to her car.

My father sits on a cushion on the dock now because one of Cal's messages said he should. I had read another one to Vili at a red light. I had turned my phone face down on my counter every night for a month, like I was sixteen.

“How did you know which ones would bring a binder?” I said. “Before it got that far.”

“You tell him no, and you watch what he does with it.” She poured again. “You told him no the first morning. I was on the phone with him at 6:20.”

“What did he say?”

“He said, ‘All right.’ In fifteen years I heard a man say that about four times.”

I remembered it. He'd been on the sidewalk in suede shoes with his back to his mother's house when he said he wouldn't ask again. He hadn't since. I had stood on that wet lawn wanting him to argue, so that I'd have something to push on.

“That doesn't make the messages his,” I said.

“No,” Dawn said. “It doesn't.”

I turned the key.

“He's driving to your yard,” she said. “He wanted you told, so that he wouldn't show up unannounced.”

“I'm not at my yard.”

“Where are you?”

“In his garage.”

She poured a third time.

“He won't hear that from me,” Dawn said, and hung up.

***

His car was in my yard when I pulled in, parked beside the dock. Same fender.

I had used the drive. By the airport I had what I would say and where I would stop. By my exit I had a shorter version.

He'd had Dawn call ahead to tell me he was coming. Even that, he'd had somebody else say. It went in the short version.

Denise was down the office steps before I had the brake set.

“He's been here half an hour,” she said. “Dad has him folding pads. Where WERE you?”

“Traffic.”

“HE didn't hit any.”

I got down. My father was in his chair on the dock, on the cushion. Cal Brennan was beside him in the gray shirt with a stack of furniture pads, every one of them folded square.

“What did he do?” Denise said.

She had two coffees and gave me one. She had her face ready to be on my side. She's been practicing it since grade school.

“He has a woman who writes his texts,” I said. “All the long ones.”

“The one with my name in it?”

“Hers.”

“The one about the dog?”

“Hers.”

Her face did what I had known it would.

“He PAYS somebody to remember my name.”

“Kevin wrote his own texts,” I said.

I don't know why I said it. Kevin's texts said what time he'd be home Thursday.

“Kevin's fine,” Denise said. “Kevin's been fine for eleven years. Ask anybody. Did Kevin ever notice that Dad needed a cushion?”

“The cushion was her idea.”

“She's never seen that chair,” Denise said. “Who told her about it?”

I didn't answer her. I went up the dock steps.

I knew who told her. Janice had said so herself, upstairs. He'd been on this dock on a Tuesday in May with the wrong sandwich. My father had made him pick up a ramp. He'd seen the chair. Then he'd gone home and told it to a woman who gets paid to write things down.

On the first night, the card he squared to the edge of the table said to ask about my father. It was in her writing, with the sevens crossed. He asked. I told him about the knees and the one line.

He stood up when he saw me. He always does. He had his cuffs turned back, pad lint all down the front of the gray shirt, and a crease in his forehead I hadn't seen before. I wanted to pick the lint off him anyway.

“I said Saturday,” I said.

“You did,” Cal said. “I came to fold pads.”

I looked at the pads. They were folded in quarters, every edge lined up, like something a hotel would do.

“Thirds,” I said. “Then in half. They don't go on the shelf square.”

He started to unfold the top one.

“Leave it.”

My father looked at the pads, at Cal, at me.

“Take the sixteen-footer,” he said. “It's got a door.”

***

The sixteen-footer was in the yard with its ramp out. I walked Cal up it and pulled the door down behind us to knee height.

It was dim in there and it smelled like old pads and the rubber on the floor. The light came in under the door in a stripe, about as high as his shins. The straps hung on the rail behind him. He stood up straight in there with his hands at his sides, like a man waiting to be told where to go.

He was four feet away. I knew that without measuring.

“I can still see your FEET,” Denise said, from the yard.

“You asked me how I was going to fix it,” Cal said. “I do not have a how.”

I had come ready for the fix. I had a version for a check and a version for an apology somebody else wrote and a version where he sent Janice. I had none for this.

“You drove up here to tell me you've got nothing.”

“Yes.”

Dawn had said to watch what a man does with a no. I hadn't told him no. I'd asked him how, in front of Janice. He'd driven up to my yard to say he didn't have one. When Kevin didn't have an answer, he came home and ate dinner and passed the salt. He didn't talk to me till Sunday.

I took the clipboard off the hook and turned to a clean found sheet. My hands needed something.

“Then we do it my way,” I said. “Same as a closet. I call it out, you say whose it is, and I write it down.”

I got my phone out and started at the top.

“‘Thank you for today. At eight you read three labels.’”

“It is Janice's,” he said.

“It says you owe me half a sandwich.”

“Janice wrote it. The debt was mine. I told her that I owed it.”

“I'm not doing halves,” I said. “‘Noted. That is five.’”

“It is mine.”

“‘He leans on the people he intends to keep.’”

I had told myself I'd read that one flat, like a packing slip. My voice went on the last word.

“It is Janice's,” he said. He said it quietly. He was looking at me and not at the phone.

If he'd said it was his, I would have written it down. I wanted him to. I'd have put it in his column and never asked him again.

I called out all thirty-two. He knew every one without looking at the screen and didn't claim one of hers. Not even the dog. I wrote 23 on the sheet and 9 under it, pressing hard enough that the numbers came through on the next two pages.

“Janice hoped my boots were off,” I said. “Janice told me to get my father a cushion. I bought it.”

“She likes you,” Cal said. “Nobody paid her to.”

That was true. It was the worst thing he could have said. I had stood in that office an hour before and watched her nod at me. I liked her too.

“You pay her for the rest,” I said. “Flowers at six in the morning. A taco truck.”

“I asked first.”

“Every house has a piano nobody put on the estimate. Where's yours?”

“There is no piano.”

“There's always a piano.”

He didn't answer that.

I was scared where he could see it.

“What else was you?” I said. “Not her. Not your robot. You.”

He took his time. He always does when it's a real question. I have watched him do it with a shrimp.

“I stood in a line in San Jose for fourteen minutes and bought you the wrong sandwich,” he said. “I called your sister to ask what Vili eats. In Los Gatos I left the cue card in my car. I asked you before I sent for the taco truck. I asked you before I kissed you.”

I was writing. When he got to that one, the pencil stopped. I could feel his hand on the side of my face in that parking lot. I made the pencil go again.

“On Saturday I made my own lunch,” he said. “I wrote your son's name on my mother's door.”

That was all. I had seven, in the order he said them.

“It's a short list,” I said.

“I want to make it longer.”

Seven. I have written longer found sheets for one coat. I read down the sheet while he stood there. Nothing on it had cost him more than a sandwich. The last line was my son's name. Owen would be home on Sunday. He'd want to know what the answer was. I wanted his signature under all seven.

I turned the clipboard around and held out the pencil.

“Sign it.”

He came the four feet to take it. For the length of the signature he was close enough that I could smell the clean shirt and the pad lint and the coffee Denise had made him. He signed under the seven and gave me back the pencil.

I did not step back. Neither did he. My heart was going, but I was not going to let it decide anything.

“One more,” I said. “Your list. Twelve lines. Who wrote it?”

“Remy wrote it,” he said. “In April I asked it to read fourteen years of my history and describe the woman I would choose. It took nine seconds. I read the twelve lines. I agreed with them.”

“You asked your phone who to marry.”

“It knows me best.”

“And it said five foot eight or taller.”

“It was off by a curb.”

I had stood on a curb in a dress for that line. I had made him get the number right.

Nine seconds. I can build a box in less. Dawn told me before the first night that I wasn't on that list. That was why I went. I figured a woman who isn't on the list can drink her beer and go home.

I hung the clipboard back on its hook with the sheet on it, facing out, where I would see it every time I pulled that door.

On the first morning he'd offered to take the job to another company. I could have taken him up on it now. Vili would have had his Saturdays back. My father's name was on that contract. I've never walked off a job with his name on it. I wanted to see what this man did with a Saturday, too. I wanted him at his mother's curb at seven, in those boots. I didn't care for that part.

“Saturday,” I said. “Seven. The contract's the same as it was.”

“I will butter the bread. Both pieces.”

“And if you've got something to say to me before then, you type it yourself. I don't care how short it is.”

“Understood.”
