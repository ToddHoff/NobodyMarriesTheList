## Chapter 18

**Cal**

On Saturday evening at 5:52 I was on Jo Silveira's front step in San Bruno with nothing in my hands, because she had told me to bring nothing.

On Thursday I had written to ask what time and what I should bring. She wrote back *6. Nothing.* I read it four times looking for a second meaning. There was not one. I had not walked into anyone's house empty-handed since I was twenty-four.

I had thought about wine on the drive up. I had thought about flowers. In May Janice had sent her sweet peas in my name. In June Jo had found out who chose them. She writes what she means. When I got out of the car my hands went to my pockets for something to carry.

It had been eighty-one degrees in Woodside when I left. On her street it was fifty-nine, with the fog coming over the ridge to the west. Her sixteen-footer was backed into the driveway. I had parked behind it at 5:44 and walked to the end of her street and back, twice.

A jet went over, climbing, low enough that I looked up to find it. A man across the street was watering his lawn. He did not look up.

A lap of her street took me four minutes. I had parked sixteen minutes early. Four laps would have put me on her step at six exactly. After the second lap I went up her walk anyway.

She opened the door before I knocked.

Her hair was down. She had on jeans and a soft blue shirt with the sleeves rolled. She was barefoot, with a dish towel over her shoulder. I had never been inside her house. My watch tapped.

I had never seen her at her own door with nowhere to be. I wanted to kiss her right there on the step, with the man across the street watering his lawn.

“You walked around the block,” she said.

“I walked to the end of your street and back. I did it twice.”

“I know. The lady across the street called me. She wanted to know if she should call the police.”

“What did you tell her?”

“To give you one more lap.” She stepped back out of the door. “Come in. Mind the shoes.”

Owen's shoes were inside the door, one of them on its side. The front room had a couch with a blanket folded over the arm and a television not much bigger than the screen in my car. She had vacuumed. The lines were still in the carpet. The hall closet door would not quite shut on whatever she had put in it in a hurry. I kept that to myself.

She had cleaned for me. On Tuesday she had sat in her truck in my driveway working up her nerve. She had told me so to my face. I put the odds at better than even that she had stood at her front window through my second lap doing the same thing. I kept that to myself. I was not going to make her answer for Tuesday in her own front room.

I followed her into the kitchen and stood in front of the drawer she wanted.

“My kitchen's eleven feet,” she said. “You're taking up six.”

She put both hands on my hips and moved me a foot to the left. Then I was in front of the refrigerator. She moved me again. The third time she put me in the doorway and kept her hands on my hips. My watch tapped again. She heard it. One corner of her mouth went up.

Nobody steers me. People who work for me ask where I would like to stand. Then they ask Janice. I could feel each of Jo's thumbs through my shirt, one on either side. My face went hot.

The doorway was the only place in the kitchen nobody needed. I leaned on the frame. There was pencil on it, beside my arm.

It was marked up one side in short flat lines, dark, with a name beside each in capitals. The first said OWEN 2 and the last said OWEN 9, which came to the middle of my chest. Every line was carpenter's pencil. Abby's were on my mother's bathroom door, in my mother's ballpoint. I saw them for the first time in June.

I counted Owen's. There were eight, one for every year from two to nine. None was missing. Every one of those years somebody had stood him against this frame and put a flat hand on his head. The capitals were hers. I was in my mother's house for none of Abby's twelve.

I moved my shoulder off the lines.

She had a foil packet out of the refrigerator with a strip of masking tape on it. The tape said *J FRI* in square capitals that were not hers. Her father had been in this kitchen last night, letting himself in. She slid six linguiças into a black pan and lifted it off the burner one-handed to shake it. Her forearm took the whole weight of it. I watched the forearm.

Her father had a key to this house. He had used it to bring her dinner. Everyone who has a key to my house and does not live in it is on my payroll.

Her phone buzzed three times on the counter.

“That's Denise,” she said, without looking at it. “She'll do four more.”

It buzzed four more times. I laughed out loud. She did not pick it up.

On the refrigerator, under one of her company's magnets, there was a calendar. Every other week said KEVIN across it in her capitals. On the square for Saturday the eighteenth she had written *CAL 6* in pen.

I am on a great many calendars. Janice puts me on them. I find out where I am supposed to be by looking. I could not remember the last time someone had written me on one by hand. The square was in a KEVIN week. I could have worked out what that meant in about a second. I did not let myself, in case I had it wrong.

We ate at her table, which had three chairs. One had a phone charger hanging off the back of it. She gave me one of the other two. The linguiça went on rolls with peppers and onions out of the same pan.

“Does this count against the half I owe you?” I said.

“It's my father's linguiça. You can't pay me back with my father's linguiça.”

“Then I still owe you.”

“You still owe me.”

I had owed her half a linguiça sandwich since May 16. On the Tuesday after, I had stood in a bakery line in San Jose and brought her a bifana, which was wrong and good and did not count. Anything else I owe, Janice has paid before I know about it. I had carried this one for nine weeks. I was in no hurry to put it down.

“What did you do all afternoon?” she said.

“I changed my shirt twice.”

She waited.

“At four I changed out of thirty-one and into thirty-two,” I said. “At four-twenty I changed back, because thirty-two is Sunday's.”

She laughed until she had to wipe her eyes with the dish towel.

“They're the same shirt,” she said.

“Yes.”

She ate with both hands and her elbows on the table. A plane went over. The glasses in her cupboard rang against each other. She did not look up.

“Who's got your dog?” she said.

“Rita has him. She is sleeping at the house tonight. She will walk him at five.”

Jo put her sandwich down.

“Tonight,” she said.

My ears went hot. I had arranged it on Thursday morning, before I wrote to ask what time.

I had asked Rita myself, in my own kitchen. On June 27 a car had waited at her curb with her name on it and the usual. I had watched her look at my face to see whether I had sent it. I had arranged my dog so that I could stay the night in her house. She had not asked me to stay.

“I wanted to be able to stay, if you asked me to,” I said.

“Did Rita ask why?”

“She asked whether I was ill.”

Jo laughed with her mouth full and had to put a hand over it. Then she looked at me over the hand for some time. She picked her sandwich back up and finished it.

I have sat across tables from that look since I was twenty-four. It belongs to people deciding whether they have been handled. Most of them take a week and send the answer through a lawyer. She did it in under a minute with her mouth full. I did not breathe much while she did.

Every evening on my calendar has an end time. This one had none. I did not ask her for one.

After dinner she ran water into the pan and turned the tap off. She left the pan in the sink. Since May I had not seen her leave anything where it did not go. She dried her hands on the towel and hung it on the oven door.

“Owen's at his dad's till four tomorrow,” she said.

I squared my plate to the edge of her table.

She took my hand and walked me down the hall, past a shut door with a dog drawn on it in marker. Her room was at the end. She turned on the lamp and not the overhead. There was a pair of drugstore reading glasses folded on a library book on her nightstand. I had not known she wore them. Her work boots stood by the closet with the laces tucked in. The fog was against the window.

Janice has a card on Jo in her file. I have never opened the drawer it is in. It would not say that she reads in bed in drugstore glasses. I had found that out by walking into her room. I wanted to know what the book was.

I had decided two things on the drive up 280. I would go slowly. I would not look at my watch.

I reached for the top button of her shirt. She had the other four undone before I had the first.

Then she undid mine. At the collar she turned the label to the light.

“Thirty-one,” she said.

“There are nine more.”

“I know how many there are.”

My hands were not steady. I noticed it the way I would notice it in a man across a table from me. I put them on her waist so they would have somewhere to be. I heard my voice go down and slow. It does that on a stage.

“There is no hurry,” I said. “We have all night. Tonight is about you.”

She stopped with her hands on my belt.

“Cal.”

“Yes.”

“You're presenting.”

I laughed. I could not stop. She laughed with her forehead against my chest. I felt it go through both of us.

She had said it to me once before, in June, across a table in Los Gatos. I had argued with her. I told her I was answering her question. In her room I had my face down in her hair and could not get my breath.

She put her hand flat on my chest, where she had put it on a curb in Los Gatos, and pushed. I went down onto her bed. She took my left wrist and turned my watch around on it, so the face was against the inside of my wrist where neither of us could read it.

After that I did not decide anything.

She did. She told me what she wanted in plain words, without any hurry. I did it. I stopped asking her whether anything was all right. Her skin was cooler than mine everywhere except her forearms and the back of her neck, where the sun gets her on the trucks. The callus from the tape gun ran across her palm. I felt it on my ribs and then all the way down my back. Her hair came down around both our faces and shut out the lamp. I had wanted her since she caught my arm on a wet ramp in May.

I found the scar on the back of her left calf with my hand, a ridge about as long as my thumb.

“Pacifica,” she said, before I asked. “Eleven stitches.”

I kept my hand there.

I had known since the first night that a dog bit her on a job when she was twenty-six. I had not known which leg. On the first Saturday my dog came through a door she had left open and leaned his whole side against her left shin. She ate the rest of her lunch without moving it. It was this leg.

She came down over me and took me in slowly. She watched my face the whole while. I let her see it. Her breath went short. Then she quit being careful with it. So did I. A jet went over, climbing. The window hummed in its frame. I did not look up. Once, low, against my neck, she laughed at something I never found out.

My watch tapped against the inside of my wrist more than once. I could not have told you the number. I did not want it.

Afterward she lay with her head on my shoulder and her hand flat on my chest. Her fingers moved a little, in time. I believe she was counting.

I lay as still as I could, so that she would get an accurate count.

“Vili wants next Saturday off,” she said, into my shoulder. “I told him if he gets Manny to take the…”

She was asleep. Her hand stayed where it was.

I never found out what Manny was supposed to take. Her breathing went long against my shoulder. Her hand on my chest got heavier by small amounts. Then it stopped changing. I did not move. I did not want to be the reason she woke.

The clock on her side of the bed said 11:14. I do not sleep in other people's houses. I lay on my back under her hand and waited for five o'clock, the way I do.

The clock on her side said 7:02. It was light. For about four seconds I did not know whose ceiling it was. Then I knew. I lay there a while and listened to her house. A cupboard shut. The coffee maker finished and clicked off.

The last time I had seen was 11:14. I had meant to be awake at five, when Rita would be walking my dog in Woodside. Somewhere between the two I had stopped waiting. I could not find the minute.

The bed was empty beside me. I could smell coffee. My watch was still turned around on my wrist. I left it that way.

My shirt was on the chair by her boots, folded in thirds and then in half, the way she folds a furniture pad. I put it on and went down the hall.

While I slept she had picked my shirt up off her floor.

She was at the kitchen table in a gray crew shirt with SILVEIRA across the shoulders and, as far as I could see, nothing else. She had the reading glasses on, and a pen, and her checkbook open. I stood in her kitchen doorway and wanted her again.

She looked at me over the glasses.

“You slept,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Coffee's on. Mugs over the stove.”

The mug said SILVEIRA MOVING & ESTATE and had a chip out of the rim. I stood at the end of her table with it, because there was still nowhere to put me.

Her mortgage statement was on the table under her pen. I read it upside down. I read everything with a number on it. The lender's name was at the top. The loan number was under it. The principal balance was $518,395.69. The interest rate was 7.000 percent. At the bottom was the coupon. In the box for additional principal she had written *1,900.00* in pen. Down the margin, circled twice, she had written *NOV 2036*.

I had read it to the bottom before I asked myself whether I should. The night before, I had read her custody calendar off her refrigerator without asking.

She saw me reading it. She turned it around on the table so it was right side up for me.

“If you're going to read it,” she said, “read it right.”

I read it right. I ran it, nineteen hundred a month extra at seven percent, from that balance. The last payment came out in November 2036. She had worked it out to the month. I have run companies that could not do that.

“It comes out in November 2036,” I said.

“I know. I did it on a spreadsheet in 2023.”

“I did it in my head. I got the same month.”

“You want to see the spreadsheet?”

“Yes.”

“Too bad.” She took the statement back. “I'll be fifty-five. Owen'll be nineteen. Party in the driveway.”

“Who is invited?”

“Not the bank.”

It was ten years and four months from that table to her driveway. I wanted to be standing in it in November 2036. I did not say so. She had done the spreadsheet by herself. It was her party.

She wrote the check for the payment and the nineteen hundred and signed it. She tore it out, folded the coupon around it and licked the envelope. She made a face at the glue. She put the statement on the counter by the coffee maker, with the envelope on top of it for the mail.

It was a number I could have sent from her kitchen in less time than it took her to lick that envelope. I had promised her on Wednesday. I put both hands around the mug.

On Wednesday at my sink she had told me about Kevin's mother and eleven years of Sundays. She had kept her eyes on the pan the whole time she told it.

At eight my watch tapped against the inside of my wrist. She heard it.

“What's it say?” she said.

Three times since June she had told me to keep that number to myself. It was the first time she had asked what it said.

I turned it over.

```
REMY · 8:00 AM
Sleep: 7 hr 40 min.
No prior instances since 2019.
```

“I slept seven hours and forty minutes,” I said.

“Is that good?”

“I average five hours and eleven minutes.”

She looked at me over her glasses for a while. Then she hooked the leg of the chair beside her with her bare foot and pulled it out from the table.

“Sit down,” she said.

I sat down.
