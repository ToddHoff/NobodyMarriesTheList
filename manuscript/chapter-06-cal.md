## Chapter 6

**Cal**

At 9:07 that Saturday night, Remy asked me to approve a message.

```
REMY · 9:07 PM
Draft to J. Silveira, from Janice.
"Thank you for today. You read three
labels this morning that I would..."
Approve?
```

I opened it and read all of it, which I don't usually do.

At 7:30 I had given Janice eleven minutes on the day, with times. I had told her about the labels, the dog and the half sandwich. I had told her that I have never been able to determine why Pancake leans on people.

Janice had an answer for that. It was in the draft, after a semicolon. *He leans on the people he intends to keep.*

It was a better message than I would have sent. It was warmer than I am, and it had my facts in it. That has been true of every message Janice has written for me since 2009. I approve about three hundred things a day, and I stopped reading them in 2019.

“Approve,” I said. It went at 9:14.

Her answer came at 9:21.

*Next Saturday bring a lunch.*

“Janice has signed off,” Remy said. “Draft a reply?”

I had already sent one. I typed it with my thumbs in nine seconds, and it contained the one word I have been told not to use.

She didn't answer it. I looked at the phone for eleven minutes, and my watch reported twice.

Then Remy had something else.

```
REMY · 9:33 PM
Pattern recognized: J. Silveira.
Filed as relationship 12.
Prior: 11. Average duration: 4.1 months.
Start the usual plan?
```

“Yes,” I said.

I didn't open it. I have said yes to that plan eleven times.

“Started. Fourteen items scheduled.”

I did not ask what they were.

There was one thing I did not hand to anyone. I owed her half a sandwich. I had been told on a bench what my job was on Saturdays, and I decided that the debt came under it.

On Monday I called the number on the door of her truck. I had read it once, on Minna Street, and kept it. I dialed it myself. A person answered, which I had not prepared for.

“Silveira Moving.”

“This is Cal Brennan. I'd like to bring Ms. Silveira something at her yard. I'm calling to ask when.”

There was a silence, and then the sound of a hand going over a telephone.

“Tuesday,” the woman said. “Eleven-thirty. She'll be on the dock. Don't tell her I said.”

“May I ask who this is?”

“This is the sister,” she said, and hung up.

On Tuesday I moved a product review. I had not moved one before.

I walked Pancake at five, and I was in San Jose by ten. There is a neighborhood there called Little Portugal. I did not ask Remy for the best bakery in it. I drove down the street until I found a door with nine people over seventy waiting outside, and I stood behind them for fourteen minutes.

At the counter I asked for sausage and egg on a sweet roll. I may have pointed. I paid with my phone, because I don't carry a wallet, and I drove north with a paper bag on the passenger seat. The car had 1,100 miles on it. I have owned it for three years.

Her yard is at the end of a street of warehouses, under the hill. The hill says SOUTH SAN FRANCISCO THE INDUSTRIAL CITY in white letters, and that morning I could read every one.

She was on the loading dock with her back to me, pulling a strap tight across a stack of pads. She put her whole weight into it. Her hair was coming out of its tie, and the pencil was behind her ear.

*Heart rate 101.* I let it report. I had decided the data was useful.

“He CALLED,” someone shouted from the office window. That was the sister.

Jo turned around. She saw me, and then the bag, and she did not come down off the dock.

“You called,” she said.

“You told me to call your office.”

“That was twelve days ago.”

“A person answered this time.”

“That person's fired.”

“You can't fire me,” the sister said from the window. “I do your taxes.”

I held up the bag.

“I owe you half a sandwich. I didn't know which half, so it's a whole one.”

She came down the steps then. She took the bag, opened it and looked in for some time.

“Where did you get this?”

“San Jose. A bakery in Little Portugal.”

“Who sent you there?”

“Nobody. I looked for the line with the oldest people in it.”

“You stood in a line.”

“Fourteen minutes.”

She unwrapped it on the edge of the dock and lifted the top of the roll.

“This is a bifana.”

“I asked for linguiça and egg.”

“What did you say? The words.”

“I said sausage. I may have pointed.”

“You pointed at a pork cutlet.” She took a bite. She chewed, and looked at the hill. “It's wrong.”

“I can go back.”

“Don't go back.” She took another bite. “It's wrong and it's good. That's a first, from you.”

“What were the others?”

“Right.”

She did not mean it as praise. I didn't follow, and she didn't explain. She ate the bifana standing up, all of it, and I watched her do it. Nothing in the product review I had moved would have held me as well. She wiped her hands on her jeans.

“That doesn't count,” she said. “You still owe me half a linguiça.”

“Then the debt stands.”

“It stands.”

I found that I preferred it standing.

She looked at my left hand.

“How's the thumb?”

“Healed.”

“Let me see.”

It was a Tuesday, in her yard, in front of her father, and she took my hand and turned it over. The cut was a thin line. She pressed beside it once with her own thumb, checking, and my watch tapped my other wrist. *Heart rate 108.*

“You'll live,” she said.

She let go sooner than she had on Saturday. I noticed, and I think she meant me to.

There was an old man in a kitchen chair on the dock, in the shade of the door. Somebody had carried that chair out for him once, and nobody had carried it back. He had a tide book the size of a deck of cards open on his knee, and he had been watching me over it since the gate.

“Jo. Who's this?”

“A client, Dad.”

“Clients bring lunch now?”

“This one did.”

He looked at me for a while. I am looked at a good deal, usually by people deciding whether I'm him. He was not deciding that. He looked at my shoulders and then at my hands, in that order.

“Can he carry?” he asked her.

“He builds a square box.”

“Didn't ask that.”

“I know what you asked.”

He turned to me. “Those your boots?”

“Yes, sir.”

“Who's breaking them in?”

“I am.”

He pointed the tide book at a ramp lying along the edge of the dock. It was aluminum, fourteen feet long, and it belonged in a slot under the white truck.

“That's been in my way since Thursday,” he said.

“Dad.”

“He's got hands.”

Jo looked at me. I went to the middle of the ramp and took hold of it there.

“Not there,” she said. “That's not how you carry one. You take an end. Palms up.”

I took an end, and she took the other. It was heavier than its dimensions suggested. I have lifted more than that in a gym four mornings a week for ten years, and I have never had to walk anywhere with it.

“On three,” she said. “I say three. You don't.”

I lifted on two.

“That was two.”

“Yes.”

“Put it down.”

We put it down. We did it again, on three, and walked it twenty feet with her going backward and telling me where the truck was. I slid my end into the slot. It took about a minute, and I was more pleased than the task warranted.

The old man had watched all of it.

“Hm,” he said, and went back to the tide book.

I have presented to sovereign wealth funds. I could not have told you how that had gone.

“That's my father,” Jo said. “That was friendly.”

The big man from Saturday had come out of the warehouse with a hand truck. That was Vili. He was looking at the empty wrapper on the dock.

“He brought lunch?”

“He brought a sandwich,” Jo said.

“One.”

“One.”

“There's eleven of us.”

“On Saturday he tried to buy lunch for everybody, and I told him to put his phone away.”

Vili gave her a look I knew from my own board.

“Why would you do that?” he said.

“Go eat, Vili.”

He went, and he took his time going.

She walked me toward my car and stopped short of it, at the fender of the white truck.

“Rowena Bautista called me yesterday.”

I knew the name. She was the daughter of the widow in Daly City, the one with the recliner. I had not expected to hear it from Jo.

“Her mother got an envelope. Alaska, fourteen days, a balcony, two tickets, paid in full. No name on it.”

“Yes.”

“That was you.”

“Yes.”

“I never told you her name.”

“Her daughter reviewed your company. Five stars, eleven sentences. The envelope in the coat is in the fourth.”

“You read my reviews.”

“Remy read them. I asked it who she was.”

She took that in and set it to one side. I could see her decide to come back for it.

“Did you ask her?”

“Ask her what?”

“If she wanted to go.”

“It was a gift. You don't ask.”

“Mrs. Bautista is eighty-one,” Jo said. “She thinks he sent it.”

“Who?”

“Her husband. She thinks he booked it before he died, and it took this long to come. She has told everybody at the new place. Forty-one years he said they couldn't afford it, and he got her the cruise after all.”

I had not specified that outcome.

“Rowena wants to send it back. She couldn't find who to send it to, and now she can't send it anywhere, because as far as her mother is concerned it's from her father.”

“That's on me,” I said. “I'll fix it.”

“How?”

“I can have someone call and explain.”

“Explain what? That it wasn't him?”

I ran it. I ran four versions. Every fix made it worse, and I don't often meet a problem with that property.

“So she goes,” I said.

“Maybe. With Rowena, who has a job and gets seasick. Or it sits in a drawer and she looks at it.” She pushed off the fender. “It was a kind thing. I'm not telling you it wasn't. Next time, ask.”

“Noted.”

“Six.”

She wasn't smiling this time. Then she was, a little, at one corner. I had been in her yard for twenty-six minutes. I had been wrong three times, and my watch had reported four. I did not want to go.

I opened the car door.

“Is this where you ask me to dinner?” she said.

“No.”

“No.”

“I told you I wouldn't ask again.”

She looked at me for three seconds. I counted. She took the pencil from behind her ear, for no reason I could see, and put it back.

“Joanna Marie,” the sister said from the window.

“Saturday,” Jo said. “Seven. Bring two lunches. Vili eats.”
