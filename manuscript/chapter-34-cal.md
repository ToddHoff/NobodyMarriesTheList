## Chapter 34

**Cal**

~~9. Good family~~

At 4:52 on Saturday morning I put two links of linguiça in a pan in my kitchen in Woodside and stood over them with a fork.

I had bought them on Thursday, in a grocery store in Redwood City, in a package of five. I had stood in front of the case for six minutes. There were four brands. I chose the one with a Portuguese name on it and a town in California under the name, because Jo's father would have had an opinion about the others and I did not want to hear it. The sweet rolls were in a bag of six on the shelf above. I had carried the package and the bag to the register myself.

Pancake lay on the kitchen floor with his head tilted to the left. Dr. Ramos had said he might keep a little of it. He watched the pan with one eye higher than the other.

The linguiça split at 4:58. The package said it would. I cut it lengthwise and put it back face down. When the edges went dark I took it out and put an egg in the fat. The yolk broke when I turned it. I did not make another. I put the egg on the bottom half of the roll, the linguiça on top of the egg and the top half of the roll on that. I wrapped it in foil and folded the ends twice.

It was a sandwich. It had taken twenty-two minutes and I was sweating.

Rita came in at 6:05. I had called her to come early. She sat down on the floor beside him with her back against the cabinet. He leaned on her.

“Go,” she said.

***

I parked across the street from my mother's house at 6:44. The sun was not on the street yet. It was on the top of the church sign at the corner and on the roof of the school.

I had a sandwich in foil on the seat and her card in my shirt pocket. Last Saturday night Dawn had asked me what I was bringing. I had said, “Nothing.” The sandwich was what I owed.

It had been ten days since Jo had said anything to me about the wire. She had not answered my letter.

The sixteen-footer came around the corner from Bernardo at 6:56 with Vili driving and Manny beside him, and her pickup behind it. Vili backed the truck into the driveway in one pass and stopped it straight in line with the garage door instead of the front walk. I did not know what it meant.

Jo got out of her pickup and did not go to the truck. She crossed the street to me.

I have watched people walk into rooms to tell me things for eighteen years. A person who has decided something walks faster for the first few steps than she does for the rest. Jo came off the curb fast and slowed in the middle of the street. By the time she got to my side of it she was walking as she walks onto a job.

She had her hair back. There was a thin chain at her neck, the one I had seen at the hospital, going down inside her T-shirt. She had the clipboard against her hip.

“Morning,” she said.

Last Saturday, in that house, she had spoken only to Vili.

“Good morning.”

“Kitchen's last,” she said. “It's all that's left. That, the hearth and the garage.”

“The garage is empty.”

“You said.” She looked at me a second longer than the sentence needed. “Seven to eleven, like it says. Then we'll see.”

She went across the street and up the walk without waiting for me. I followed her. I wanted to put my hand on the back of her neck, under the chain, where her hair was coming loose already. I kept my hands at my sides.

***

She ran it from the middle of the floor. She did not route a single call through Vili. She called the item and looked at me and waited. I said green or blue to her face. She said it back and wrote it down. The coffee maker: blue. The mixing bowls, nested by size, left of the stove, second shelf: green. The kitchen table, where my mother read my report cards: green. I had not decided that one until I heard myself say it.

The table under the globe lamp in the front room had sat with no tape on it since June 6. She did not call it. She did not look at it. At 9:20 I went in and put green tape on it myself. When I came back she had already written it down.

The yellow telephone stayed on the wall. She did not call it. Neither did I.

By 10:40 the kitchen was cupboards with the doors open and nothing on the shelves. Manny wiped them down without being asked. Vili took the kitchen table out to the truck on his shoulder, alone, with a pad on it, and came back for the chairs, and then for the table under the globe lamp.

***

At eleven she called lunch.

Vili came in from the truck with the lunch bags. He handed one to Manny and kept one and handed the third to Jo. There were three. Last Saturday there had been four. I had my own on the counter in foil. I did not know whether she had known I would bring it, or whether she had stopped making mine.

She took her bag out to the atrium. Vili and Manny went out to the truck, where the cab has air conditioning. She sat down on the bench at the end where she always sits, with the maple behind her.

I took the foil out of the kitchen and went out and sat down on the other end of the bench.

It is a short bench. That morning there were about eighteen inches between us, with her bag in them.

I unfolded the foil. The sandwich was flatter than it had been at five. The roll had soaked up some of the egg. I tore it in half. It came apart worse than hers had in May. I held one half out across the bench.

She looked at it.

She looked at it for long enough that I started to bring my hand back. Then she took it. She took it with her right hand. Her fingers touched mine. I did not move until she had it.

She ate it before she opened her own bag. She ate it in four bites, looking at the maple. I ate mine and did not taste it. At the third bite she stopped and looked at the inside of it.

“You broke the yolk,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Next time leave it.” She took the fourth bite. “It runs into the bread.”

*Next time.* I held the foil in both hands and looked at the gravel. I could feel my pulse in my thumbs.

She wiped her fingers on her jeans. Then she opened her own bag and took out her own sandwich and ate that too.

***

At 1:35 in the afternoon the rest of the green was in the truck: both tables and the chairs, the bowls, nine boxes from the kitchen marked in Jo's capitals.

“Walk-through,” Jo said, and went into the house by herself.

Last Saturday she had gone into the house by herself at the end and stayed nine minutes. I had not known what she did in there. I stood in the atrium where she had left me and watched her through the glass.

I watched her go into the front room, where the three boxes were still on the hearth. She went into my mother's bedroom, and into mine, and into the hall bathroom, which had no door now. She opened every closet. She opened the oven. She ran her hand along the top of the refrigerator nook where the refrigerator had been.

In every room she stood in the middle of the floor for a while after she had opened everything. It was longer than the job took. The job took seconds. She stood in the middle of my old bedroom for a minute and forty seconds, with the clipboard against her leg and her face toward the window, without writing anything down. After three months of watching her, I could tell when she was looking for something. She was not looking for anything.

Then she went into the kitchen, last, and crossed it to the door in the corner by the back door. It is the door to the garage. She tried the knob.

She came out to the atrium. Vili was in the front doorway.

“Garage,” she said.

“It is empty.”

“You said that in May.” She turned the clipboard around so I could see it. In her capitals, under the date: *GARAGE. LOCKED. OWNER SAYS EMPTY.* “Everybody forgets the garage. I don't give the keys back on a room I haven't been in.”

“I do not have a key to it.”

Vili had already gone to the truck. He came back with a hammer and a thin steel punch and went through the kitchen to the garage door to look at the hinges.

“Pins are on this side,” he said. “I can have it off in two minutes, boss.”

I put my hand in my pocket for my phone. There is a locksmith on El Camino who would have been there in forty minutes. Remy would have had him there in twenty. I had my thumb on the screen.

Then my thumb was on the other thing in my pocket.

It was a key on a loop of red yarn. Pat Dolan had put it in my hand on May 16 with a bag of my mother's mail. I had put it on my ring with the front-door key that night and carried it for fourteen weeks. I had never asked Pat what it opened. I had not wanted to know.

“Wait,” I said.

I went through the kitchen. Vili stepped back from the door with the punch in his hand. I put the key in the lock.

It turned.

Behind me Vili whistled two notes. I did not look around. I opened the door myself.

***

The light in my mother's garage is a bulb on a chain. I found the chain where it had always been, by feel, two steps in and to the right, and pulled it.

The garage smelled like concrete and lemon furniture polish. The water heater stood in its corner. Over the workbench the pegboard still had my grandfather's tools drawn on it in marker, and none of the tools. It was empty, the way I had said.

Against the back wall, under three moving pads, there was something about five feet long and three and a half feet high.

Jo came in and stood beside me. She put her hand flat on the top pad and pressed. Something under it did not give.

“Those aren't mine,” she said. “Somebody who knew what they were doing put this here. Look how it's set.” She put her hand on the side nearest the wall. “Face to the wall. You do that so if anything hits it, it hits the back.”

She took the top pad off and folded it in thirds and then in half, without looking, and laid it on the bench. Then the second one. Then the third.

It was a piano.

It was an upright, the small kind, dark wood, standing on two short front legs with the keyboard lid shut and its face to the wall. There was a bench pushed up against the end of it with its own pad still folded on the seat.

*Every house has a piano nobody put on the estimate,* she had said to me in June, in her yard. *Where's yours?*

*There is no piano,* I had said.

Jo put her hand over her mouth. For a second I thought she would laugh. She took her hand away. She was not laughing.

“I did not know,” I said. “I have not been in here since 2003.”

“I know.”

Vili was in the doorway with Manny behind him. Jo did not look at them.

She and Vili walked it out from the wall on its legs. Then she got down on her knees on the concrete in front of it and took the panel off the bottom, under the keyboard, with her fingers. It came away on two clips. She put her flashlight in and looked.

“Clean,” she said. She put the panel back and stood up. “Vili. Big door.”

When Vili unlatched the big door and ran it up, the afternoon came into my mother's garage all at once, white and hot, with the ramp lying on the driveway at the end of it and the truck backed up to the edge of the light.

Jo lifted the seat of the bench.

Pianists keep music in a bench. I knew that the way I know most things, from a page. There was music in this one. There were three books on top, thin ones with paper covers. The top one said JOHN THOMPSON'S EASIEST PIANO COURSE, PART ONE. Under the title, in blue ballpoint, in capitals, in the hand that had written ABBY on a bathroom door twelve times, it said *ABBY.*

Under the books there was a spiral notebook, the narrow kind that fits in a purse.

Jo took the books out and handed them to me and did not open them. She took the notebook out, looked at the cover and handed that to me too.

On the first page my mother had written PIANO and a number. *$1,800.* Under it there were lines, ruled by hand with the edge of something straight. Each line had a month and a year and *$100* and a check mark in her pen.

The first line said *Sept. 2012.*

I read it twice.

In September 2012, the month before I paid off her house, she had bought a piano for my daughter on payments. Abby was four. My mother had paid a hundred dollars a month for eighteen months and checked off every one. The last line said *Feb. 2014.* Under it, in her capitals: *PAID.*

Abby had come every summer until she was fourteen. She had sat at this bench with her book open on the stand. She had never told me.

Everybody hides things.

I did not know whether she could play. I did not know whether she still could.

I looked up from the notebook.

Jo was watching me read it. She had been watching the whole time. I know because when I looked up she did not look away.

I lifted the keyboard lid and pressed one key, in the middle. It was flat. I pressed it again.

“Green,” I said.

She did not write it down. She did not take the pencil from behind her ear. She looked at me, then at the piano, before she turned around to Vili and Manny in the light.

It had not been on her estimate. On any other job she would have had the paper out of the truck before I finished the word.

“Four-wheel dolly,” she said. “Two straps. Every pad we've got left.” She turned back to me. “It's about four hundred pounds. Up a ramp, the low end's the heavy end. Two on the low end.”

“Yes.”

“You're low,” she said. “With Vili.”

Vili looked at her. He had been on the low end of everything I had watched him carry for three months. He looked at me before he went and got the dolly.

They padded it. I helped where she pointed. They tipped it onto the dolly on its feet, slowly, Vili's hands under one end and Manny's under the other and Jo's flat against the face of it, guiding. When it was on, Jo ran the two straps around it and cinched them. She did not look at her hands. Then she and Manny rolled it across the concrete, one at each end, with a hand flat on the top.

At the foot of the ramp she stopped it.

The ramp is fourteen feet of aluminum. It was hot enough that I could feel it through my gloves when I put my hand on the rail. Jo went up it first, backward, to the high end, with Manny beside her. Vili and I took the bottom.

The low man calls the lift. It is her father's rule. In the yard in May, the first time I carried anything for her, I had lifted on two.

Vili had his hands under the dolly beside mine. He was waiting for me.

I looked up the ramp at her. She was looking down it at me.

“I will call it,” I said. “On three. I say three.”

“Okay,” Jo said.

“One. Two.”

Nobody moved on two.

“Three.”

It came up off the driveway and onto the ramp. The weight came down the ramp into my hands and my thighs. I took it. It was more than I thought. I held it. Vili held it. Above us Jo and Manny had their hands flat on the top of it and were walking backward.

“Small steps,” I said. “Step.”

We stepped.

“Step.”

I called every step of it. There were eleven. I did not look at my feet. I looked at her. At the top she got it over the lip and into the box of the truck and rolled it to the wall with Manny, face to the wall. They took it off the dolly and belted it to the rail twice and then a third time.

I stood at the bottom of the ramp with my hands open. My arms were shaking. I could hear my pulse in my ears.

Vili clapped me once on the shoulder, hard, and went up the ramp to help with the belts.

***

They closed the truck at 2:50 that afternoon. Vili came down the walk with the truck's clipboard and stopped in front of me.

“Woodside,” he said. “Green load. The piano goes in on the dolly and comes off it in the room you want it in. Not in the hall. You tell me the room.”

“The front room,” I said. “By the window.”

“The front room by the window.” He wrote it down. Then he looked at the house, where Jo had gone back in. “She's staying?”

“I do not know.”

“She's staying.” He put the pencil in the clip. “She already walked it.” He looked at me. “Lift on three next time too.”

“I will.”

He went down the driveway and got in the cab. Manny was already asleep against the passenger door. The sixteen-footer pulled out onto West Remington Drive and turned toward Bernardo at the corner.

***

When I went back in she was in the front room.

Nothing was left in it but the paneling and the globe lamp, hanging over a square of carpet darker than the rest, where the table had been since 1960. The sliders were open to the atrium. The hot air came through them and did not move.

She stood in the middle of the floor the way she had stood in every room. She had the clipboard against her leg.

“Tell me,” I said.

Jo put the clipboard down on the hearth beside the boxes.

She came across the room to me. She did not stop where she usually stops, an arm's length off, where she can see my whole face. She came all the way. She took my right hand in both of hers and put it flat on her chest, on her T-shirt, over her breastbone.

Under my palm, under the cotton, there was something small and hard on the chain, about the size of my thumbnail. I did not know what it was.

She held my hand there.

Her heart was going hard. It was going harder than mine had been on the rock. I could feel it in the heel of my hand, fast and uneven. On the rock off Skyline she had told me to say it again when my heart was not going like that. I had walked half a mile and said it across her hood.

She did not wait for hers.

“I love you,” Jo said.

On the rock I had counted to twenty-six before she moved. I did not count. On 280 that morning I had decided it was not coming.

I opened my mouth.

“Don't,” she said. “Not yet. I'm not done.”

She kept my hand where it was. I left it there. I could feel the thing on the chain go warm under my palm.

“You didn't let Remy pick it,” she said. “You picked it yourself. It wasn't the money.”

“No.”

“I know. I read your letter.” She took a breath. “Three times.”

Then she let go of my hand. I took it back slowly. She stepped back, one step, to where she could see my whole face. Her face went red from the collar up.

“You didn't ask me,” she said.

“No.”

“I made you promise. At your sink. Not a sandwich.”

“Yes.”

“You wrote it in your letter,” she said. “*That is the part I did.*”

“Yes.”

“I was going to pay that house off. Me. November 2036. I typed every date in that spreadsheet myself.”

“I know. You would not show it to me.”

“Denise was bringing a sheet cake.” Her mouth went tight. “It was mine. I'd have had it on my own.”

I had a sentence ready. I had had it since August 12. *If I had asked you, you would have said no.* It was true. It was the whole of my case. I had been making it to myself in the car for ten days.

She said it before I could.

“I'd have said no.” She said it straight at me. “Before you got to the end of the question.”

“Yes.”

“I say it to everybody. I said it to my dad in July, and he drove the sixteen-footer for a week on those knees.” She stopped. “That one's on me. The house was still mine to say.”

She was right. I had known it at 3:12 in the afternoon on August 7. I had sent it anyway.

“Janice said you always give a present before the end.” Her chin came up. “Stephanie came to my yard. She said they get a present at about four months, and they write you a thank-you note, and your office answers it, and that's the last of them.”

“Yes.”

“I didn't write you one.”

“No.”

“I wasn't going to write you one.” She was looking at me hard. “I sat in my kitchen for a week thinking I was supposed to. I thought you were done with me.”

“No.”

“I thought that's what the five hundred and twenty-one thousand was. The last present.”

“I was not done,” I said.

“I know that *now.*” She pressed the heel of her hand to one eye, fast, and took it away. “I didn't know that on the twelfth.”

The sentence I say is *That is on me. I will fix it.* I have said it to boards and to a reporter and to Stephanie. I had it in my mouth. I had the second half of it ready.

There was nothing to fix. The house was paid. The bank could not send it back. Anything I did next would be another thing I had sent.

I kept my mouth shut and stood there.

She looked around the empty room, at the dark square on the carpet and the hearth and the open sliders.

“You watched me,” she said. “Out there. Doing the walk-through.”

“Yes.”

“You timed me.”

“A minute and forty seconds. In my old room.”

“So ask me.”

I had wanted to know since 1:35. I had not been going to ask her.

“What were you doing in there?” I said.

“I was seven,” she said. “My first job. My dad had the one truck. A house in Millbrae with orange shag carpet.”

I waited.

“The husband sat in his car at the curb all morning. He didn't come in. The son was about sixteen, and he carried his own boxes out to his father's trunk, one at a time.” She was looking at the square on the carpet. “The wife followed my dad room to room. She'd put her hand on a lamp and say, That's mine. The dresser. Mine. She talked the whole time.”

“What did she say?”

“His hygienist. Twenty-six. He'd told her she didn't need to work. Fifteen years she didn't need to work.”

“And money?”

“She hadn't had a pay stub since Nixon. Every card in her purse had his name on it.”

“Where did she go?”

“Her sister's. Daly City. The back bedroom and the garage, under a tarp.” Jo's hand went up to her chest, to the chain, and stopped there over her shirt.

“What did you do?”

“When they'd carried the couch out, I lay down on the floor where it had been. In the square. It was the quietest I'd ever been in my life. When I came out the car was gone.”

“And the son?”

“Went with him.”

She was quiet for a second. Then she said, “My dad said in the truck, what you hear in a house stays in the house.”

“Then you should not tell me.”

“I'm telling you.”

She looked at me when she said it. I did not look away.

“In April I moved one in San Carlos. Kept his books nineteen years. He never put her on the payroll. I made the bed for her in the new place.” She stopped. “I walk every house last. By myself. Nobody moves me out of mine.”

I thought of line seven. *Comfortable being looked after.*

“There's more,” she said. “I've never told this one to anybody. Not Denise. Denise was five.”

“All right.”

“The winter I was eight, my dad hurt his knee on a job. The truck sat. He sat in a chair in our front room till spring and didn't talk.” She said it fast.

“And your mother?”

“Hadn't had a paycheck since I was born. There wasn't one coming. So the church fed us. All winter.” She swallowed. “Lasagna, and the chicken with the potato chips on top. Every dish had a strip of masking tape on the bottom with a name on it, so you'd know whose to take back.”

“Who took them back?”

“Me. I washed them and dried them and walked them back. Every one.” She put her hand flat on the paneling beside her. “A man from the parish paid my dad's truck note for three months. Everybody at church knew who.”

“I wrote down every name,” Jo said. “Off the bottoms of the dishes. On a piece of paper. I was eight. I've still got it.”

“On Stephanie's papers,” I said. “You wrote *and what I owe on it.*”

“Yes.”

“You owe nothing on it.”

“I owe you on it.” She did not look away. “Your name's on the bottom.”

“I went to Denise's on Wednesday,” she said. “I wrote you a note. Five hundred and sixteen thousand, at seven percent, the first of every month. My signature and a line for yours.”

“How long?”

“Denise ran it on the calculator. At what the bank makes me pay, two hundred and forty-two payments. October 2046. You'd have signed it.”

“Yes.”

I would have signed it on the hood of her truck. It would have let me give her the house and let her give it back to me, a little at a time, with nobody's name on the bottom of anything.

“I tore it off the pad,” she said. “It's in my purse. I'm not giving it to you.”

“Why?”

“Two hundred and forty-two checks, Cal. With your name on every one.” She looked straight at me. “I'm keeping the house.”

I have sat across tables from people telling me yes since I was twenty-three. I know what they do with their hands when they mean it. She had both of hers at her sides, open.

I did not have a sentence. There was no next thing. I said the only thing I had.

“Thank you.”

She stood very still. Then she put her face in her hands, once, quickly, and took them away. Her eyes were wet. She did not wipe them.

“Okay,” she said. Her voice was not steady. “Okay.”

She lay down on the carpet in the square where the table had been, on her back, with her arms out.

I stood over her. She looked up at me.

I lay down beside her, with my head by her head and my feet going the other way. There was nothing on my other side. Pancake was on my kitchen floor in Woodside with his head tipped over against Rita's knee.

When a plane went over, the glass in the sliders hummed with it and stopped. Her breathing slowed.

After a while her hand found mine on the carpet between us.

I wanted to say it back. My heart was going, so I waited.

It was after four when she sat up.

“The hearth,” she said.

The three boxes had been on it with no tape on them for fourteen weeks.

I knelt on the hearth. The top one was the Mother's Day box. It had gone out in May, seven months after she died.

I opened it myself. I cut the tape with the key on the red yarn.

Inside was a smaller box in good paper. Inside that was something white and smooth, about the size of a loaf of bread, with one round button on the top and a cord wrapped under it.

I turned it over. I did not know what it was. Someone good at it had chosen it.

“It's a humidifier,” Jo said. “For a bedroom. You put water in the top.”

“Blue,” I said. Then: “All three. Blue.”

She tore the tape with her teeth and put a strip on each box and pressed it down with her thumb.

I did not get up off the hearth. I had her card in my pocket.

“May I tell you line twelve?” I said.

She came and sat down on the hearth next to me, with the boxes between us and the wall.

“You wrote you would,” she said.

“Line twelve is *says what she wants.*”

“I know. I read it on Janice's desk.” She looked at her hands. “I didn't say it at Carmel.”

“It is not about you. It was on the list before you.” I stopped. I had not said this to anyone. Not to Stephanie. Not to Dawn last Saturday night. I did not have it in order.

“When my mother was hurt, she stopped talking,” I said. “The longest was nine days. I was eleven.”

“Hurt by what?”

“I did not know. She never said what was wrong. She never said what she wanted.”

“What did you do?”

“I left my report card on the kitchen table.” The kitchen was open in front of us, with the cupboard doors standing open and nothing in them. “She would sit down and read it, and sign it, and start talking again.”

“Every time?”

“Every time.”

Jo looked down at her hands, at the tape-gun callus across the palm of the left one.

“I did that,” she said. “To you. Since the twelfth.”

“Ten days.”

I looked past her, through the open kitchen, at the yellow phone on the wall by the back door. She turned her head and looked at it too.

“She called me from that phone,” I said. “Two days before she died.”

“What did she say?”

“Remy wrote me a summary. *Asks you to call when you have a minute. Not urgent.*”

“Did you call her?”

“I had a minute on the ninth. She died on the eighth.”

Jo shut her eyes.

“I read the summary for ten months,” I said. “I played the message last Saturday night. The summary left out a sentence.”

“What sentence?”

“*You, Calvin. Not Janice.*”

She opened her eyes and got up off the hearth. I thought she was going out to her truck. She went into the kitchen, to the yellow phone, and she reached up and slid it up off its plate, and unclipped the cord from the jack with her thumbnail, and wrapped the cord around it twice. She came back and held it out to me in both hands.

She did not say green or blue. She held it out and waited.

“Green,” I said.

She put it in my hands. It was lighter than I expected. It smelled like the kitchen.

I held it on my knees.

“Nine,” I said. “I will tell you nine.”

She sat back down.

“Line nine is *good family.*”

“We're movers.”

“She handed me a tray five days a week for three years. I made sure the other boys did not know she was mine.”

“And nine?”

“I put it on the list so I would stop being the lunch lady's son.”

“Can I see the card?” she said.

I gave it to her. The corners had gone soft. She turned it over to the back, where eight of my lines were in her capitals with a line through each one. She took the pencil from behind her ear and wrote *GOOD FAMILY* under ten and drew the line through it.

She held on to the card.

“Did you ever go back over there?” she said.

“No.” I looked at the roof of the school. “I sent a check.”

“What check?”

“Every June, from 2012. On the last day of school I bought a cashier's check at the bank on El Camino and mailed it to the district office myself.”

“For what?”

“The lunch accounts. Every child at her school whose balance was under zero. It paid them back to zero.”

“How much?”

“The first year, two thousand two hundred and eleven dollars. The last one went in 2022. After that the state made the meals free.”

“Janice did that?”

“Janice never knew. She would have asked me why.”

Jo turned the card over in her hands.

“She was at the register on the last day of school,” I said. “Every year, until she retired.”

“Did she know?”

“I do not know. She never said. And I never asked her.”

Jo looked at the back of the card. There were nine lines on it now, in her capitals, struck. Twelve was not one of them. She put the card back in my shirt pocket herself and pushed it down with two fingers. She left her hand there on my chest and pressed once.

***

At 5:52 that evening I locked the front door of my mother's house with my key. The shadow of the house was halfway across Remington. Next door Pat Dolan was in her front window, not pretending she was not watching.

I had the yellow phone under my arm and the piano books in my other hand. Jo had her clipboard. She had written something on it in the front room before we left and had not shown me.

On the walk she stopped.

“Walk me to my truck,” Jo said.
