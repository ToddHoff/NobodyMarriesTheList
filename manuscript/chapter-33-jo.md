## Chapter 33

**Jo**

On Tuesday I packed a kitchen in Linda Mar by myself, on four hours of sleep, with dog hair still in the creases of my right hand.

The woman had moved to her daughter's in Sacramento in June and left the kitchen for last. Her daughter wanted the good dishes and nothing else. Everything else was mine. Forty years of a kitchen. A drawer of rubber bands she'd taken off the newspaper every morning and never used once. Six cake pans. A bread machine still in its box with the receipt taped to the top, 1997.

I like a kitchen by myself. Nobody asks me where to put anything. I do the high cabinets first, standing on the counter in my socks, because if you do the low ones first you're climbing over your own boxes by noon. Glasses go in cell boxes, stems up. Plates go on edge, never flat, with a sheet between each one, because flat plates break and plates on edge don't. I can do a cabinet of plates in ten minutes if nobody talks to me.

Nobody talked to me. The fog never came off the hill. By the third cabinet I'd stopped seeing the plates and was seeing a dog on a padded mat with his head tipped over, leaning it on my hand because he couldn't hold it up himself.

I'd washed that hand twice since. There was still hair in it.

I was on the counter with the top cabinet open when my phone went in my back pocket. It wasn't a number I had.

*This is Abby. I took your number out of Dad's phone while he was carrying box 7.*

Then a picture.

It was a stairwell, cinder block painted white, with a window at the top. A flat box was going up it, long, with a 7 circled on the end in black marker. A boy I didn't know had the top end. Cal had the bottom.

He was in a gray T-shirt, wet down the middle of the back. He had the box on his palms, both of them up, under the bottom edge, the way you carry one. His knees were bent. His face was turned up the stairs at the kid, and his mouth was open on something.

He'd taken the low end. On stairs the low end is the heavy end.

I sat down on the counter with my socks hanging over the sink and looked at his forearms in that picture till the screen went dark.

Then another text.

*He's staying till I check in for welcome week tomorrow. Parents can't come to that. He asked me if he could stay till then.*

Then:

*Pancake stood up this afternoon. The hospital called Dad. He said to tell you, then he said not to tell you, then he went to buy a fan.*

I read that three times.

I didn't answer her. I didn't know what you say to an eighteen-year-old who has taken your number out of her father's phone. I put the phone face down on the counter and finished the top cabinet. Then I picked it up again and looked at his hands.

***

On Wednesday I got home from the yard and the mail was on my counter. Denise had come by at lunch for a folder. She'd put it in two piles, bills on the left and everything else on the right.

On the left was a window envelope from my lender.

On the right was a flat cardboard envelope from the post office, Express, overnight, from San Luis Obispo. Denise had put it on top, with a yellow sticky note on it in her capitals. *IS THIS HIM??* Inside it was a white envelope, the long kind. It was addressed to me in blue ink, in square printing that leaned a little to the right. *Jo Silveira.* My street. San Bruno, the zip. The postmark said SAN LUIS OBISPO CA, the eighteenth.

I didn't know the printing. I'd seen two letters of it, CB, in the margin of two pages on my tailgate.

I opened the bank first.

It was a check. Four thousand four hundred eighty-three dollars and forty-five cents, made out to me. *Refund of excess funds. Loan paid in full.* There was a letter with it thanking me for my business.

I put the check on the counter, face up, and didn't touch it again.

Then I opened his.

There were three pages, folded in thirds. The first one was printed. I knew it before I had it flat. Twelve lines, numbered, and a footer at the bottom in small gray type. *Generated by Remy. Approved by C. Brennan.*

Across the top, over the first line, he'd written in the same blue ink:

*I did not write this. I agreed with it. That is worse.*

He'd gone down the left side in pencil. Beside eight of the lines there was a date, small, the day I'd struck it on the back of his card. He had every one of them right. Beside the other four, seven and nine and eleven and twelve, he'd written the same thing four times.

*I will tell you this one myself.*

I sat down on a stool.

The other two pages were in pen, on paper from a yellow pad, with the ragged edge where he'd torn them off. His printing got smaller as it went down the page.

*Jo,*

*Every word on these two pages is mine. I am writing them in my car outside Abby's building. She is at a meeting for her floor.*

*On Saturday, May 16, at 9:33 at night, Remy opened a file on you. It was called Relationship 12. It had a plan in it, the same plan it made for the other eleven. I aproved it. I did not read it.*

He'd spelled approved with one p.

*The car that came for you in June was the plan. There was a weekend in Napa in July. That was the plan too. Abby read it on my phone, upside down. She said, “The usual.” I stopped those two when I saw them. I did not open the rest. I did not ask how it ended until last Saturday night.*

*It ends at month four. There is a gift. Remy picks it. None of the eleven lasted to month five. Each of them ended within fourteen days of the gift. Each of them wrote to thank me for it. Janice answered, and I approved the answer. That was the last I heard from any of them. I did not notice. Remy did.*

I put the page down on the counter and put my hand flat on it.

Stephanie had told me most of it on Monday, in my yard, in her flat shoes. It was different in his printing.

I picked it up again.

*Month four was August. Remy had three gifts ready. I did not open them. I paid off your house myself.*

*In June Owen told me you have not had a day off since he was born. He said you would take one when the house was paid. At your table in July you said that was November 2036. You will be fifty-five. Owen will be nineteen. I wanted it to be this year, while he is nine. I wanted you to go up Sweeney Ridge whenever you liked. I wanted you to have your party in your driveway.*

*Janice told me to ask you first. I did not ask you, because I knew what you would say. I sent it myself on Friday, August 7, at 3:12 in the afternoon. That is the part I did.*

*Since then I have not asked Remy anything about you. I am not going to.*

*The hospital called at 4:10 this afternoon. He stood up. He ate. Someone called them at ten to six this morning to ask whether he had eaten. They did not get her name.*

*Thank you for coming.*

*Cal*

I read it twice standing up. Then I sat back down and read it again.

Three gifts. He hadn't opened them. Mine he'd picked himself, out of something my son told him in June.

Owen will be nineteen.

I'd said that at my own table, to his face, in July. He'd been doing the arithmetic since.

I'd been furious for a week. I still was. *I did not ask you, because I knew what you would say.* He'd known what I'd say. So he hadn't let me say it.

He hadn't written me a word since the pads. Then he'd sat in his car outside her dorm and written it out by hand, on paper torn off a pad, and spelled a word wrong, and stood in line at the post office in San Luis Obispo himself. I'd have bet money he'd never been inside one.

I folded the three pages back the way they'd come and put them in the envelope. I put the envelope on top of the bank's check.

I didn't answer him either.

***

Kevin pulled up at six on Wednesday on the dot. The night before the first day of school is his. He put it on the calendar in June.

He got out and looked up the street and down it. Then he leaned on his car.

Owen came out with his new backpack on both shoulders.

“Is Pancake okay?”

“He stood up yesterday,” I said. “He's eating.”

“Vestibular,” Owen said. “I looked it up. It's the inner ear. Most of them are better in seventy-two hours.”

“He did it in one day.”

“Can I go see him?”

“No.”

He looked at me a second, and then at the ground. I put my hand on his back. He let it stay there.

“Go get in,” I said.

He got in the back and put his earbuds in.

Kevin didn't get back in the car. He stood by the driver's door with his keys and looked at the house, at my truck in the driveway, at me.

“Owen says you went to a dog hospital at two o'clock Tuesday morning.”

“Denise stayed with him.”

“I'm not asking about Denise.”

I didn't say anything.

“He says you came home and haven't called him.” He looked down the street again. Nobody was coming. “He asked me what my record was. For not talking.”

My face went hot from the neck up.

“He's nine. He ranks dogs.”

“He's usually right about the dogs.”

I'd have walked back in the house. I had my hand on the gate. Kevin doesn't start things. In eleven years I couldn't remember him starting one. He waits it out and passes the salt. I couldn't think what he'd want badly enough to start one in my driveway, with our son in the back seat.

“You remember I used to call you from the store?” he said.

“You called me from everywhere.”

“I did.” He turned his keys over. “The last one was March 2017. A Saturday. The Costco in South City. Owen was five weeks old.”

He said it the way he gives me a pickup time. I stood with my hand on the gate.

“I called to ask if we needed diapers,” he said.

I didn't remember it. I tried. I had the baby and the phone and nothing in between.

“What'd I say?”

“You said, ‘When have I ever run out of anything, Kevin?’”

I didn't say anything.

“Then you hung up.” He turned his keys over. “I could hear him crying when you did it.”

“I bought the big box anyway. You put it in the hall closet. You didn't say a word about it. They were the size up.”

I remembered the box. I'd used them in May.

“I didn't call you from a store after that,” Kevin said.

The street was quiet. A plane went out over the hill from SFO, climbing west.

“That was the Saturday before,” I said.

“The Saturday before.”

Saturday the diapers. Tuesday the salt.

In a stranger's house I'd have put those two together in an hour.

“Nine years,” I said. “You never told me.”

Kevin looked at me. He has a patient face. It's what makes him good with the eighth graders.

“Would you have listened?” he said.

I wanted to say yes.

He'd been standing in an aisle with a cart, wanting to bring home something I needed. That was all the call was. And there wasn't anything. I'd seen to it. I'd ordered the diapers that morning so nobody would ever have to bring me a box of anything, not him and not his mother with her Sundays. I'd been seeing to it since I was seven, since the Millbrae wife stood in her kitchen and told my father every card in her purse had her husband's name on it.

Every time he called, I told him I had it. After a while there wasn't a thing in this house he'd put there.

If he'd tried to tell me, I'd have heard a man saying I was doing it wrong. Then I'd have done it better, to show him.

“No,” I said.

Kevin nodded, once. He didn't look surprised.

He opened the driver's door. He looked up the street and down it again.

“I'll take him tomorrow,” he said. “First day. You've got him at three.”

He got in and pulled away, and Owen lifted one hand in the back window without looking up from his phone.

I stood at the gate until I couldn't hear the car.

My hand on the latch was cold. Five weeks old. I'd had him on my shoulder in that hall with a roller in my other hand, and I'd been proud of it.

*I did not ask you, because I knew what you would say.*

It was on my counter, in his printing, under the bank's check. I'd read it three times and been furious at it three times.

He'd known what I'd say because I'd already said it to him. Over a beer at the Palace, before he'd offered me a thing. On my father's dock, the Thursday he came about the truck. About the transmission, the week he found out. I've got it. Kevin heard it from a Costco. Vili hears it every morning.

Nobody had to ask me anything. They all knew the answer.

Kevin quit asking and went quiet. Cal didn't ask and sent a wire.

I'd taught them both.

***

I drove to Denise's. I don't go to Denise's. Denise comes to me, with two coffees, at ten past six in the morning. I'd been in her kitchen on Christmas and Easter and the night Mom died, and not much else.

Rob let me in. He had a dish towel over his shoulder and the TV on in the front room.

“Jo,” he said. “How's the dog?”

“The dog's fine.”

“She's in the kitchen.”

Denise was at the stove in her good blouse with an apron over it, stirring something with chorizo in it. She took one look at me and put the spoon down.

“Who died?”

“Nobody.”

“Then why are you HERE?”

I sat down at her table. Her table has a burn mark from a pot. She covers it with a placemat when company comes. I'm not company.

“Kevin,” I said.

She turned the burner down without looking at it. She put a plate in front of me, rice and the chorizo and two eggs on top, and a fork. Then she sat down across from me.

“Eat,” she said. “Then talk.”

I ate half of it. Then I told her. The store. The Saturday. The diapers, and the size up, and the hall closet.

Denise's face did everything while I talked. When I got to the diapers it stopped.

“The size UP,” she said.

She put her elbows on the table and looked at the doorway to the front room. Rob was telling the TV what it was doing wrong.

“He did that to me,” she said.

“Rob?”

“Rob. The year we got married. The year Dad gave me his shoebox.” She turned her coffee cup by the handle. “I had those receipts on the kitchen table every night till eleven. He'd call me from the store. Bread, coffee, red grapes or green. I'd say, I'm in the middle of something, Rob. Every time.”

“What happened?”

“He quit calling. He came home with the wrong coffee and put it away and didn't say a word. For about a month I thought, good, he's learning.”

“Then what?”

“Then I found a bag of grapes in the trash. A whole bag. He'd bought them, and he didn't want to ask me which, so he threw them out.” She stopped turning the cup. “Who throws out GRAPES?”

“What'd you do?”

“I asked him.” She said it like it was nothing. “I sat on the kitchen floor in front of the trash and said, Rob, what is going ON. He said, You're always in the middle of something.”

“So the shoebox went in the garage after seven,” Denise said. “And when he called from the store, I picked up. Green, Rob. I like the GREEN ones.”

Then she looked at me.

“When Kevin went quiet. That Tuesday. What did you do?”

“Painted the hall.”

“Did you ask him what was wrong?”

“Once. He told me what time he'd be home Thursday.”

“Once? In nine years?”

“Once.”

“Why not again?”

I almost said I'd been busy. I'd had a baby and the trucks.

“Because he'd have told me,” I said.

Denise didn't say anything. In the front room Rob laughed at the TV. She didn't look over.

“And Cal,” she said. “Two in the morning. What did you ask him?”

“What he fed the dog.”

“And why he paid off your house? Did you ask him that?”

I didn't say anything.

She picked up my plate and put the other half of my dinner in a container with a lid and wrote JO on the lid in marker, and put it in front of me.

“What else?” she said. “What did he send you from San Luis Obispo?”

I took the envelope out of my purse and the bank's check with it. I put them on her table, on the burn mark.

She read his letter with her face going ahead of her. At the part about Owen she put her hand over her mouth. She got to the end and turned the last page over. The back was blank.

“He spelled approved wrong,” she said.

“One p.”

She put her finger on *Cal* at the bottom. “Joanna Marie. He did this HIMSELF.”

“I know he did.”

She turned to the second page and put her finger on it.

“It says why,” she said. She read it off the page. “‘I wanted it to be this year, while he is nine.’”

“He'll be ten in February,” I said.

“Before Cal. When's the last day you took off? Not Mom's birthday.”

“Before Owen.”

“He's nine.” She went down the page with her finger. “‘I wanted you to go up Sweeney Ridge whenever you liked.’ Whenever you liked, Joanna. When's that ever been?”

“He should have asked me.”

“And you'd have said?”

I didn't answer.

“You'd have said no,” Denise said. “No to the ridge. No to a day with Owen while he's nine. No to your party. You'd have said it before he got to the end of the question.”

“Maybe.”

“Did he ever ask you what you WANTED?”

“At Carmel.”

“And?”

“I didn't say.”

“So he guessed,” Denise said. “Did he guess wrong?”

I didn't have anything for that.

“So you'll pay him back the money,” Denise said. “Every penny, with interest.” She put the page down. “And the days? You going to mail him those too?”

I didn't say anything.

She picked my phone up off the table and held it out to me.

“No,” I said.

“Then text him.”

“No.”

She put the phone down and picked up the bank's check. She turned it over and back.

“What are you doing with this? Cashing it?”

“It's his.”

“And the rest?”

“Five hundred sixteen thousand, five hundred sixteen dollars and fifty-five cents.”

Denise looked at me. “You did that in your head.”

“Last Wednesday. At my counter.”

“You paid Kevin's mother back with interest, and she never asked you to.” She put the check down square on the burn mark. “Pay him back, then. Where he can see it.”

She got up and came back with a yellow pad and her good pen, and put them in front of me.

“Amount,” she said. “Rate. First payment.”

I picked up the pen. Then I put it down.

“Check my numbers?” I said.

I'd never asked her that. She puts things in front of me, and I sign them.

Denise sat down hard. Her face did everything at once.

“Write,” she said. “I'll get the calculator.”

***

I wrote it at her table, in ink. Denise stood behind me with her arms folded, which she knows I hate. I let her.

*PROMISSORY NOTE*

*San Bruno, California. August 19, 2026.*

*On August 10, 2026, Calvin Brennan paid off the loan on my house in San Bruno without asking me. The bank applied $516,516.55.*

*For that, I promise to pay Calvin Brennan $516,516.55, with interest at 7.000 percent a year, which is what I was paying the bank. I will pay $5,891.81 on the first of every month, starting September 1, 2026, until it is paid.*

Denise put her finger on the fifty-eight ninety-one.

“You can't make that.”

“I made it for three years.”

“You didn't make it in August. The white truck's seventy-six hundred. Hillsborough stopped forty-eight sixty.” She didn't move her finger. “I do your books, Joanna.”

“I'll sell the white truck.”

“With no transmission? For parts? Nine thousand, if you're lucky. Then you're running two trucks. Which two guys do you let go?”

“Nobody.”

“Then sell the whole thing.”

“It's Dad's name.”

“It's MY name.” She said it quiet, for her. “It's on the doors too.”

I looked at her. She'd done the books in that office for twenty years. I'd never thought about whose name it was on the doors but his and mine.

“What did the bank make you pay?” she said.

“Thirty-nine ninety-one eighty-one.”

She ran it on the old calculator with the paper roll, standing up, with her nails going on the keys. She tore the paper off and put it in front of me.

“Two hundred forty-two payments. October first, 2046.”

I'd be sixty-four. Owen would be twenty-nine, with his own driveway.

“And the nineteen hundred?”

“Goes to the truck till Christmas. Then Hillsborough.” She tapped the paper. “After that you put it on, and maybe it's 2041. If nothing else breaks.”

“Something always breaks.”

“So that's your plan,” Denise said. “A check to Cal Brennan on the first of every month till you're sixty. His name on every one.”

I looked at the pad.

I'd paid Kevin's mother back in one check, with interest. I'd felt clean for about a week. This would be twenty years.

“No,” I said.

“Then what?”

I didn't have anything.

Denise pulled out the chair across from me and sat down. She pushed the calculator out of the way with the back of her hand.

“Do you love him?”

“That's not what this is.”

“It's exactly what this is. You let a man you don't love pay off your house? Mom had a word for that.”

“Denise.”

“And if you love him, it's a house.” She put her hand flat on the pad, on his fifty-eight ninety-one. “So?”

He'd said it to me on a rock off Skyline in July, with his heart going under my hand. I told him to say it again when it wasn't. He did, across my hood, half a mile later. I kissed him and told him to get in the truck.

I'd had it in my mouth on that rock. I'd had it in the dark at Carmel, and in Janice's office with her tape gun going behind me. Every time, I shut my mouth on it. I'd told myself I was waiting to be sure. I wasn't waiting. If I never said it, there was nothing he could buy with it. Not a truck. Not a sandwich. I'd kept it in my mouth the way I kept the bill.

Now there was a house, and if I said it, it would sound like the house.

“Did you love him before he paid it?” Denise said.

I thought about my table at one in the morning in July. Him reading three hundred reviews with his whole face still, so I could find nine people, and a spot under his jaw he'd missed shaving. He'd left at twenty to three because I had Millbrae at seven.

“Yes,” I said.

“Say it.”

“Not to you.”

Denise sat back. Her eyes were wet. She didn't wipe them.

I looked at the pad. Twenty years of checks, with his name on every one. That was just the other way of not saying it.

I tore the page off the pad. I folded it in half, and in half again, and put it in my purse with his letter.

“Then I let him help,” I said.

“Tell him why first,” Denise said. “Then the house.”

“Saturday.”

Rob came to the door with the dish towel still on his shoulder. He looked at the two of us, and at the pad, and at the paper curling off the end of the table.

“Do I want to know?” he said.

“Yes,” Denise said. “Sit down.”

He sat down.

***

At home I put his letter on my counter, face up, next to the bank's check. I didn't sign the check over. I didn't cash it either. One thing at a time.

Saturday was the last one on Remington. I had three days.

I took the glass out from under my shirt and held it in my fist, and I left the letter there, with the light over the stove on, and went to bed.
