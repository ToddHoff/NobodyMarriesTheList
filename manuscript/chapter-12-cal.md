## Chapter 12

**Cal**

On Saturday Jo's son asked me nine questions in my mother's back yard, with my dog's leash in his hand.

Eleven minutes earlier his mother had taken hold of my shirt in the hall and told me to bend down. I could still feel where her hand had been, two buttons below the collar. I answered all nine with my back to the glass, because my face was not under control.

He read them off his phone.

“Question one. Can I pet your dog?”

“Yes.”

“Question two. How many voices did you test for Remy?”

“I tested forty.”

“That's right,” Owen said.

He was checking whether I did. I have run interviews that way since the company had four people.

Pancake walked him twice around the yard. Owen held the leash in both hands, as I had shown him, with his back to the glass, where his mother was.

He is nine. My daughter was nine in 2017. I was not here for that line either.

I remembered the last of mine. I was twelve and told her I was too old for it. She made me stand there in my socks anyway. Fourteen years later she started a column for Abby next to mine. She never mentioned it in the four times I made the drive in her last five years.

Jo watched from the other side of the glass with her arms crossed. Her hair was half out of its tie. My hand had been in it in the hall. She watched her son and the dog, except twice, when it was me. I counted.

When Pancake had done his two laps he went to the glass and leaned on it, where her leg was.

“He's leaning on my mom through a window,” Owen said.

“He leans on the one person in a room who would rather he did not.”

Owen looked from the dog to his mother.

“Question six,” he said. “Do you take days off?”

“I do not take many.”

“My mom hasn't had one since I was born.” He did not look at his phone for that one. “She's taking one when the house is paid. She has a spreadsheet. I'm not allowed on it.”

He walked Pancake to the fence and back. I waited. He had not finished.

“In November she fell asleep on the kitchen floor,” he said.

“What time was it?”

“Nine-forty. I got up for water.” He had the leash in both hands. “She had her boots on and her work jacket. She had the sponge. She does the counter last.”

“What did you do?”

“I put the blanket on her. The one from the couch. Then I went to bed.”

“You did not wake her.”

“She was tired.” He said it as a thing he had checked more than once. “She does that.”

“And in the morning?”

“My lunch was in the fridge with my name on it. I asked her if she was okay.” He looked at the glass. “She said she had it.”

I could have had a person in that kitchen by Monday for the counter and the lunches. I could have had a car at her curb and the house on Maui stocked for a week. I had most of it arranged in my head before he finished the sentence. I said none of it. On a job her son asks a client for nothing. He had not asked. He had told me what time it was.

Owen looked at the dog again.

“He's still second in my app,” he said. “I'm thinking about it.”

I tell my board the same thing when I have already decided and want one more quarter of numbers before I say so. I did not argue for my dog.

At 7:30 that night I called Janice, as I do after every Saturday, to tell her about the day so that she could write to Jo.

“Go on,” Janice said.

I gave her the sandwich, the butter and the nine questions. I gave her Owen's handshake, one pump, and the four minutes and ten seconds.

“Is that all of it?”

“Yes.”

It was not. I had left out the bathroom door with my daughter's name on it twelve times. I had left out that Jo took hold of my shirt in the hall and told me to bend down. I had left out question six. I did not want any of them in a message at 9:14.

Her message went to Jo at 9:14. I read it before I approved it. It was about butter. It was good.

I had my thumb over the screen for forty seconds. Every one I have read since the fifth of June has been kinder to her than I would have been. I have approved all of them. I approved that one.

At 10:02 on Tuesday, in the middle of a product review, Remy told me that Jo Silveira was in my building.

```
REMY · 10:02 AM
Visitor: J. Silveira, Silveira Moving.
Estimate, 10:00. Booked by J. Louie.
```

I had not booked an estimate. I looked up from the phone.

She was at the far end of the floor, outside Janice's door, with a clipboard. She was in the boots and the gray T-shirt with SILVEIRA across the shoulders, the pencil behind her ear. She was counting Janice's file cabinets out loud. Janice stood beside her with her hands folded.

Sixty people work on that floor. Every head that came up went to the woman in boots first and then to me.

I had seen her in a bar, on a curb, on her own loading dock and in a green dress. This was the first time in my building. She looked down the length of my floor the way she had looked down my mother's hall, as a job. I wanted her to like it.

In seventeen years Janice has never needed an estimate for anything. She retires in December. She had hired a mover six months early so that she could get a look at Jo.

Every long message on Jo's phone was Janice's. Jo did not know it. Janice had booked the estimate anyway. I have watched her size up people since there were nine of us. She folds her hands and lets them talk. She would not bring up the messages.

“Cal?” Brandon said.

We hold reviews standing, on the open floor. Since I took the chairs out in 2019, the reviews have run eleven minutes shorter. Brandon runs the gifts team. He was nine minutes into a feature that was shipping on Thursday and had not noticed that I had stopped listening.

“Say the last part again,” I said.

“You tell Remy once,” Brandon said. “Your mother's birthday, Mother's Day, Christmas. It picks the gift, writes the card and sends it, every year. You never miss one again.”

He was proud of it, with reason.

“How was it tested?”

“Two thousand accounts for ninety days. No failures.”

“Whose accounts were they?”

“Ours. Employees.”

“How old are the people who work here?”

He looked at the woman beside him, who looked at her laptop.

“Thirty-one, on average,” she said.

“Then their mothers are about sixty,” I said, “and every one of them is alive.”

Nobody answered.

I knew where Jo was without looking. She had stopped counting.

“My office has done this for my mother since 2014,” I said. “It sent her a gift for Mother's Day, on the fifth of May. She died in October. It does not ship until it has a field for whether she is alive.”

I had not said that out loud to anyone who works for me. I said it at the volume I use for a schedule change. It was easier with her there. I do not know why.

“We ship Thursday,” Brandon said. “To forty million phones.”

“We do not ship this Thursday.”

Eleven people picked up their laptops and left the floor at close to a run.

Brandon stayed where he was. Having worked on it for a year, he was waiting to find out whether he still worked here. He was not going to ask.

“The work is right,” I told him. “The sample was wrong. Bring it back in two weeks.”

He nodded and went after the others.

Janice had brought Jo the long way around. They were standing at the edge of the review, six feet from me. Jo was looking at me.

She had read the label on that box in my mother's front room. She was the only person on the floor who knew which box I meant.

“You told me in a bar that you were good at rooms,” she said. “You are.”

My ears went hot. I put my hands in my pockets, which I do not do.

Investors have told me I am good in a room since I was twenty-four. Every one of them wanted something from me when they said it. Jo had come to give Janice an estimate.

She looked down the floor and took in the desks and the stairs.

“Sixty desks,” she said. “I could have this floor in two trucks by Thursday.”

“We are here through 2031.”

“I wasn't offering.”

“Calvin,” Janice said. “Ms. Silveira is giving me a number for my office.”

She calls me Calvin when I am wrong. I had not done anything yet.

“You booked an estimate,” I said.

“Her office had an open slot,” Janice said.

“Her machine called my machine,” Jo said. “I'm used to it.”

“You do not leave until December,” I said to Janice.

“I like to know what a thing costs before I need it.”

Janice turned to Jo. She is a small woman who stands very straight. Jo stands the same way.

“You corrected the card I wrote for him,” Janice said. “In pencil.”

“My son was nine,” Jo said. “Your card said eight.”

“It did. My information was a year old. That was my error.”

“You cross your sevens.”

“I do.”

“He squares your cards to the edge of the table. Did you know that?”

“I taught him to bring them,” Janice said. “The squaring is his.”

Jo pointed the pencil at the cabinets.

“Fourteen. What's in them?”

“Cards,” Janice said. “One for everyone he has met since 2009.”

“Whose are they? Yours or his?”

“His.”

“Then he's in the room when they get packed,” Jo said.

“He has never opened one of those drawers.”

“He will.”

They were discussing me like a piece of furniture. I did not object.

Janice looked at her for some time before she nodded. In seventeen years I have seen her give that nod to two lawyers and a caterer.

“Is there a card for me?” Jo said.

“There are two. I had to start a second.”

Jo laughed. I felt it under my sternum, where I had felt it at dinner. She turned to me still smiling, with the lines at the corners of her eyes that a billboard would have taken out. I had been waiting since Saturday to see them again.

“Owen got through nine of his questions on Saturday,” she said. “He sent me with the other two.”

“What are they?”

“Are your shirts numbered.”

“No.”

“He says they should be. One to forty, so you wear them evenly.”

“He is right. I will have it done.”

“Do it yourself,” she said. “It's forty shirts and a marker.”

“What is the other one?”

“Does Remy write your texts.” She shook her head. “I told him no. I told him no robot knows that about a dog.”

“Remy does not write them,” I said.

She nodded, the way she nods when a count comes out right, and started to turn back to the cabinets.

It was true enough to let me walk her to the elevator and keep Saturday. All I had to do was stop talking.

The line about the dog was Janice's. Jo had used it to answer her son. Owen checks what he is told.

“Janice does,” I said.

Jo stopped smiling. Her eyes stopped first. Her mouth took about two seconds longer.

“Since when?” she said.

“She has written them since 2009.”

“I mean to me,” she said.

“She has written to you since the sixteenth of May. I wrote the short ones. The ones that say ‘noted’ are mine.”

“How many?”

“Twenty-three were from Janice. Nine were from me.”

Jo turned to Janice.

“The line about the dog,” Jo said.

“He told me he did not know why the dog does it,” Janice said. “I had a guess.”

“The one about a house answering your footsteps.”

“My mother's house, in Fresno.”

“I read that to my crew lead.”

“I am glad it was worth reading.”

“The sweet peas, the first morning.”

“They don't keep,” Janice said. “I thought you would rather not owe him a vase.”

Jo named them in the order they had come.

Jo did not say anything for a while.

She held the clipboard flat against her chest with both arms over it. I wanted to say something into the quiet. Every sentence I had ready was one Janice would have written better.

“Who remembered my sister's name?” she said.

“Remy had it from the account at your office,” Janice said. “I put it in.”

Jo looked at me.

“Did you know her name?”

“I learned it on the fifth of June,” I said. “That was when I read the message.”

She kept looking at me. I could see her do the subtraction. By the fifth of June the messages had been going for three weeks.

“That is on me,” I said. “I will fix it.”

“How?” she said.

I ran it. I could have Janice stop, which would leave Jo nothing on Saturday nights but four words with a number in them. If I wrote the long ones myself, she would know by the second sentence. I could tell her that every fact in them was mine. It was not going to help. Every version I ran came out the way the cruise had.

I did not have an answer. She waited for one, then quit waiting.

Janice paused. It was the eighth time in seventeen years.

“Ms. Silveira,” she said. “He told me every one of those things. The labels, the dog, your father's chair. I wrote them down.”

“I know what he told you,” Jo said. “I was there.”

I had been there too, right up until I drove home and gave all of it to Janice at 7:30, with times. I looked at the fourteen cabinets of my cards.

Jo put the pencil behind her ear.

“Fourteen cabinets, a desk and two chairs,” she said to Janice. “Two men, half a day. My sister will send you the number.”

“Thank you, Ms. Silveira.”

“Her name's Denise,” Jo said. “You have it.”

When she walked to the elevator, I went with her. She did not tell me not to. She walks fast when she has decided something, so I had to lengthen my stride.

She pushed the button herself.

We stood a foot apart, looking at the numbers over the door. She smelled like cardboard and the cab of her truck. Three days earlier I had had my hand in her hair. I did not know what I was allowed to do with my hands now, so I kept them at my sides.

Her jaw was set. There was a muscle in it I had not seen move before.

“We still have Saturday,” I said.

“Seven,” she said. “It's a contract.”

The doors opened.

“Don't walk me to my truck,” she said.
