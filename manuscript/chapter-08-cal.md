## Chapter 8

**Cal**

Jo picked a Wednesday, and before I left the house I asked my daughter a question.

The Wednesday was June 3. I had told Jo the place would be in Los Gatos, in a room that is open five nights a month. She picked one of the five without knowing it. I checked whether I had arranged that, and I had not.

Abby was at the refrigerator when I came down. She had the door open and was eating out of a container with her fingers, standing up, with Pancake lying across her feet. There was a sheet of paper on the freezer door under a magnet. It had been there for three weeks, and I took it for a schedule.

“You're wearing the shirt,” she said.

“I am having dinner.”

“With the mover.”

“Janice told you.”

“Janice tells me things. It's how I keep up with you.”

She is eighteen. She has my habit of being exact, and she has had it pointed at me since she was about twelve.

There was an index card on the counter, in blue ink. Janice had sent it over that afternoon. Abby looked at the card and then at me, and I put it in my pocket.

The last real question between us had been hers. At seven she asked me how computers think. I drew her a network on a napkin at my kitchen table. We were at it for an hour. I have counted it as our best hour since.

“What do you want to build?” I said.

She stopped chewing. “What?”

“When you are an architect, what do you want to build?”

She shut the refrigerator. She leaned on it and looked at me for what I estimate was five seconds.

“Who told you to ask me that?”

I considered several answers. “A mover did.”

“The mover.”

“On the first night, she said to ask you before you go.”

“When was the first night?”

“It was May 7.”

“So it took you four weeks to ask me a question somebody else thought of.”

“It took me eighteen years not to think of it,” I said. “Four weeks is an improvement.”

She had not expected that. Neither had I. One corner of her mouth went up, and she got it back down.

“Cafeterias,” she said.

“You want to build cafeterias.”

“School ones. Grandma's didn't have a window. Thirty years in that room, and she never knew what the weather was until three o'clock.” She picked the container up again. “I'd put windows in.”

I ate lunch in that room on every school day I spent in that building. I could not have told you whether it had a window.

Abby had that from my mother. I had never heard my mother say it. She handed me a tray across that counter five days a week. I looked at the tray. I never asked her what the day was like on her side of it.

“I did not know that,” I said.

“I know you didn't.” She stepped over the dog. “Have a good dinner. Thanks for asking!”

I heard the exclamation point. With Abby it means a door has closed.

***

I drove myself. In the parking lot in Los Gatos I took Janice's card out of my pocket and read it. It had four things I was to ask and three I was not to say. I put it in the console, shut the lid on it, and walked away from the car. The other card stayed in my shirt, the one with four lines struck through in pencil.

I was aware of the distance to the console all evening. It was about sixty yards.

The room used to be Manresa. I had decided it was the restaurant I would eat at for the rest of my life, which is what I do when I find the one. Then it closed. It is a members' club now. A visiting chef cooks twelve courses, and I am a member because I could not think what else to do about it.

She was already there, standing outside the door.

Her hair was down.

I had only seen it tied back for work, with the pencil behind her ear. It came past her shoulders. It was darker than I had recorded, and the gray over her left ear was there with nothing done about it. She had on a dark green dress and sandals. Without the boots the top of her head came to a different place on me, and I would have had to measure again.

I had planned to say good evening. I stood on the walk with my hands at my sides and said nothing for four seconds, which was long enough that she raised her eyebrows at me.

My watch tapped, and I looked at it.

“Don't,” she said.

“What should I not do?”

“Whatever it just told you. Keep it to yourself.”

“It was 112.”

“I said keep it.”

She was pleased, though. I am learning to tell.

I wanted to put my hand in her hair. On the first night I had laid my hand flat on top of her head to mark her height, with her own tape running up from under her boot heel. I could feel it for the rest of that night. I could not think of a reason to do it again that she would accept. I held the door for her and kept my hands to myself.

The room is small and low, fourteen tables with candles in glass, and a wall of windows onto a courtyard where a lemon tree is lit from underneath. It was quiet enough to hear the kitchen, a knife on a board and someone saying *Yes, chef.* The air smelled of brown butter. Jo stopped inside the door. She ran two fingers along the back of the nearest chair, under the top rail, where the joint is. I watched her two fingers go along under the rail and back. I wanted them on the back of my neck. Then she looked at me, and I took her to the table.

“Who booked this?” she said, at the table.

“Janice did.”

“I said you pick the place.”

“I picked it. Janice made the call.”

“Huh,” she said, and opened her napkin.

The first course was one pea in a glass spoon. The second was an oyster about the size of my thumbnail. The third came under a glass dome full of smoke, and when the smoke cleared there was a radish.

She ate each one in a single bite. Then she put her fork down and looked at the place where it had been. At the oyster she shut her eyes for two seconds. I left mine on its plate until she opened them.

“Is that the food,” she said at the fourth, “or is that what comes with the food?”

“That is the course.”

“Okay.”

The fifth course was the bread. A boy of about nineteen brought it, one roll apiece and a spoon of butter. The roll was warm enough to steam when she tore it open. It was the first plate she took two bites to finish. She watched him all the way back to the kitchen.

I had eaten in that room since it was Manresa. I could not have told you who had ever brought me the bread.

I asked her the thing I had planned to ask. It comes from a list of questions Dawn Pruitt gives her clients, and I had kept it because it has a number in it.

“What would you do with ten million dollars?”

She did not laugh. She took it as an estimate to be worked out.

“Pay off my house. Buy a fourth truck, a twenty-six-footer with a liftgate. Put Owen's school away. Give my guys a real Christmas.” She had counted them on her fingers. “That's about two of the ten. I'd keep working.”

“Why would you keep working?”

“What else would I do on a Tuesday?” She pointed the fork at me. “You?”

“I would do nothing.”

“Nothing?”

“It would not change anything. I would not notice that it had arrived.”

She looked at me for a while. She did not have an answer ready, and she did not hurry one.

“Ten million's my house and a fourth truck,” she said. “For you it's what, a Tuesday?”

“It is less than a Tuesday.”

I had not run that number from my side of the table. Ten million dollars is less than a tenth of one percent of what I have. When I told her I would do nothing, I had thought it was a good answer.

“Your robot,” she said, at the sixth course. “I know what it does. It answers my phone. What's it for?”

I was nervous by then. I had brought one question of my own, and it was used. When I am nervous I do the thing I am good at.

“We do not sell software,” I said. “We sell an hour. Remy gives you back an hour of your day, and that hour was never ours to keep. It belongs to the people you would spend it on. There is a man in Tulsa who has not missed one of his daughter's games in two years.”

There is more. It runs four minutes, and it has closed three funding rounds.

She let me get to the end.

“You're presenting,” she said.

“I am answering your question.”

“Your voice dropped. Your shoulders came down. Same as for the lady at the bar.”

“It happens to be what I am good at.”

I am not corrected often, and never at dinner.

“I know it is,” she said. “I'm telling you I can hear the difference. That's all.”

Nobody had told me about the shoulders. At the Pied Piper I had talked with her for three hours. The voice had dropped once in all that time, for a stranger. I did not know what I had sounded like for the rest of the night. I had not been listening. I would have liked a recording of it, so that I could find out what I had done and do it again.

She went back to her plate. I squared my knife to the edge of the table, because the card I would have squared was in the console, sixty yards away, with four questions on it.

So I told her something that was not on it.

“Dawn asked me a question in April,” I said. “I had told her I wanted a wife. She asked whether I wanted to be a husband.”

“What did you say?”

“I said I would get back to her.”

Jo laughed out loud. Two tables turned around. It is not a room people laugh in.

“Have you?”

“No.”

“It's June,” she said. She was still smiling. I felt it in my chest, and my watch tapped, and I kept that one too.

The ninth course was a carrot that had been made to look like a different carrot.

“Can I ask you something?” she said, when the plate had gone. “Is there more bread?”

“The bread was the fifth course.”

“I've been up since four-thirty. I moved a dentist's office today, with the chairs. I've had eight plates and one roll, and I think it comes to about a cup of food.”

“It is considered one of the best meals in California.”

“I believe it. Everything has been the best thing I ever tasted, for one bite. I'm still hungry.”

I had my phone out before I knew I had reached for it. I can have a kitchen sent almost anywhere. She looked at the phone.

“I'm not complaining,” she said. “I'm just hungry.”

On a bench in my mother's atrium she had told me to put it away. I did not put it away. I held it where she could see it.

“May I send for something?” I said. “It would be in the parking lot when we walk out.”

“Send for what?”

“It would be something with more than one bite in it.”

She looked at the phone, and then at me.

“You picked the place,” she said.

“I did.”

“Then you owe me something to eat.”

“Yes.”

“All right,” she said. “Tell your robot.”

It was the first time I had asked her before I sent for something. My hands were not quite steady on the phone.

I typed one line under the edge of the table, and it took eleven seconds. I asked for food that did not need a fork. Before that night I had seen her eat three times, at a bar, on a bench and on a loading dock. Each time she had used her hands. Remy came back with an amount to confirm, and I confirmed it. I did not read what the amount was for.

The tenth course arrived. She ate it in one bite.

“Did you ask her?” she said. “Your daughter. What she wants to build.”

“I asked her tonight, before I left.”

“And?”

“She wants to build cafeterias, with windows. My mother worked thirty years in one that had none.”

Jo put her fork down.

“She asked who told me to ask,” I said.

“What did you tell her?”

“I told her the truth. She said it had taken me four weeks to ask a question somebody else thought of.”

“She's not wrong.”

“No. She is rarely wrong. It is a difficult quality in a daughter.”

“You asked, though.”

“I asked.”

“Ask her another one next week,” Jo said. “One of your own.”

I took it as a spec and started on it before the next course came. I could ask her what she wanted for her room at school. I could ask whether she needed anything bought for it. Each question I came up with ended in something to buy. Janice could have had the answers for me by noon.

The eleventh course was three wild strawberries. Not one was larger than the pea. She ate hers.

“Are you going to eat those?”

I moved mine across. She had asked me the same question about six shrimp, twenty-seven days earlier.

She started on them one at a time, with her fingers. I watched her mouth until she looked up and caught me at it.

I did not look away. I could feel my ears go hot. I let her see that too. There was strawberry at the corner of her mouth. I wanted to take it off with my thumb. After that I wanted my own mouth there, in a room where two tables had already turned around once that evening.

“You owe me two lines off that list,” she said. “One for the dinner. One for making me say it.”

“I know.”

I took the card out of my shirt pocket and laid it between us with her side up. There were four lines on it in carpenter's pencil, and each one was struck. They said five foot eight or taller, thirty to thirty-six, dresses well, and athletic. The list has twelve lines. That left eight.

“Pay up,” she said.

I had expected this. In the car I had gone through the eight that are left, to choose two.

“I cannot,” I said. “The first four were the easy ones, and you have had those. The eight that are left are not funny.”

She stopped with the last of my strawberries halfway.

“None of them?”

“No.”

She studied me. I had seen her study a hutch like that, working out where the weight was.

I let her look. I could have said any two of the eight in the voice she had caught me using at the sixth course. She would have struck them. We would have been even by the twelfth course. I did not want to be even with her like that. I did not know yet how else to pay them.

“Then you owe me,” she said.

“I owe you two lines and half a linguiça.”

“I keep books.”

“I have met your sister.”

There is no check in that room. I had forgotten that it would matter.

“Where's the bill?” she said, at ten past nine.

“There is not one. It is a club. It is settled.”

“Then how do I pay for mine?”

“You cannot.”

She stopped smiling, for the first time that evening.

I had been watching that smile since the walk outside. I did not like the table without it. For a moment I considered having a check made up for her, for whatever she wanted to pay. She would have known who had made it.

“I ate a dinner and I didn't pay for it.”

“You told me to pick the place.”

“I said pick it. I didn't say buy it.”

She opened her purse. I could see the tape measure in it. She took out three twenties and put them under the edge of her plate, squared.

“That's for the kid who brought the bread,” she said.

I did not tell her what three twenties were against that dinner. I have learned a few things.

We stood up. She was close enough that I could see where the sun had caught her nose.

“I meant to tell you,” she said. “What you wrote me on Thursday. That you know a house is empty when it starts answering your footsteps.”

I had not written to her on Thursday. On Thursday I had said “approve,” in a car, without looking.

“I've been clearing houses for twenty years,” she said. “I never had the words for that. I read it to Vili.”

I had said nothing to Janice about footsteps, or about empty houses. I did not know where she had got it. Jo had kept it for six days. She was looking up at me, pleased with me. I did not want her to stop.

“Yes,” I said.

“When did you notice it? You've only been in the one house.”

“I noticed it recently.”

That was the first lie I told her. I can give you the time. It was 9:14.
