## Chapter 3

**Jo**

At 5:35 the next morning Vili was at the dispatch window, signing out furniture pads, four dozen to a truck. He swung the screen around on its arm so I could see.

```
REMY STARTER · SILVEIRA MOVING
New estimate. Booked 11:52 PM.
C. Brennan. Estate, Sunnyvale.
Today 7:00 AM, at the house.
Accepted: open slot.
```

“You've got a seven o'clock,” he said.

“I've got a six o'clock in Pacifica.”

“Screen says seven. Sunnyvale.”

I hadn't booked it.

I had gotten home at 11:40 the night before. I didn't text him that I was in, because I didn't have his number. I texted Denise one word, HOME, and she sent back a row of question marks that I left alone.

At 11:52 I was lying in the dark, not sleeping. I was thinking about a hand on the top of my head and telling myself I was thinking about Pacifica.

“Take Pacifica,” I told Vili. “You're lead. If the daughter wants the piano moved, she signs a change order first. It's not on the job.”

“Who's C. Brennan?”

“A client.”

“At seven in the morning. In Sunnyvale.”

“Go.”

He went. Six guys, two trucks, and the white one making its noise all the way up the hill.

The fog was sitting on the hill behind the yard. On a clear morning you can read SOUTH SAN FRANCISCO THE INDUSTRIAL CITY up there in white letters sixty feet tall. That morning I could read INDUSTRIAL and had to take the rest on faith.

A florist's van came through the gate at ten to six.

In my business flowers mean a widow is saying thank you, and they come at two in the afternoon. These came before the sun did. Whoever ordered them knew what time movers start.

They were sweet peas. It was a bunch the size you would buy for yourself, in brown paper, with no vase. The card was typed.

*Thank you for the three hours. Sweet peas don't keep, so you won't have to. Cal*

I read it in the yard with a clipboard under my arm. Then I read it again, because it was right, and I don't trust right. A hundred roses I could have sent back. A vase I'd have had to return. These I could stand in a coffee can on the dispatch desk and let die by Wednesday, and I would owe nobody anything.

Somebody had thought that through. I felt it under my ribs, warm, and I told it to quit.

I put them in a coffee can.

Dawn Pruitt called at six. She knows what time I'm up. She has watched me carry her mother's dresser.

“Joanna. How was it?”

“Fine.”

“Fine.”

“He was fine. The beer was fine.”

“He left me eleven minutes on my machine at midnight,” Dawn said. “With times in them. I know you corrected his card at 8:31 and made him measure you at 8:38.”

“He timed it?”

“He times everything. He would like to see you again.”

“No.”

I heard her pour. Dawn only drinks coffee hot, so she pours half a cup at a time, and over the phone you can count them.

“Is that no to him?”

“It's not him.”

“Then what is it?”

“It's what he would do to my life. I have eleven people, three trucks and a nine-year-old. He has a car that waits at the curb. Where does that go, Dawn? I don't have a slot for it.”

“I didn't ask you to marry him. I asked if you'd have dinner.”

“And I'm saying no to dinner.”

“All right.”

She didn't push. She did divorces for fifteen years, and she knows when a witness is finished.

“I'll tell him,” she said.

“Tell him it was fine.”

“I'll tell him no. He does better with no than with fine.”

“He's on my schedule at seven,” I said. “His mother's house. It's an estimate.”

I heard her pour again.

“It's a job, Dawn.”

“I didn't say a word,” she said, and hung up.

I stood in my yard with a phone, a can of sweet peas and four hours of sleep, and I was relieved. That lasted about ten minutes.

My sister came through the gate at ten past six with two coffees and saw the can.

“Who died?”

“Nobody.”

“Then who's sorry?”

“Nobody's sorry. They're a thank-you.”

She read the card. Denise is forty-one and does my books on Tuesdays and Fridays, and she has never read anything of mine and kept her face still.

“He SENT these?”

“Somebody did.”

“And you said what?”

“I said no. To Dawn. Ten minutes ago.”

“Joanna Marie.”

“I have a seven o'clock in Sunnyvale,” I said, and pointed at the screen. “Which you booked.”

She looked at the screen. She looked at me.

“I did not book that.”

“Your robot did. You told it an open slot was a yes.”

“Well,” Denise said, “it's got better judgment than you do.” She drank some coffee. “Owen's back Sunday. You going to tell him you had a date with the billboard?”

“It wasn't a date.”

“Three hours, Jo.”

I took the sixteen-footer, which is the truck I keep back for estimates and small jobs. It's forty minutes to Sunnyvale at that hour, and I spent them telling myself that an estimate is an estimate.

The house was on West Remington Drive, a wide street with old trees and a church on the corner. It was one of the low glass ones with a flat roof that they built out there sixty-some years ago. I have cleared four of them. There was a black car at the curb with a driver in it.

Cal Brennan was on the sidewalk in front of the house, with his back to it.

He had on the gray shirt. It could have been the same one or one of its brothers. He also had on suede shoes, on a wet lawn, at seven in the morning, and that was reason five.

I have met a great many people at the curb of a dead parent's house. Most of them are looking at the house. He was looking at the street.

In daylight he was worse. I had said no to Dawn at six o'clock, and nobody had told my pulse.

“Your machine called my machine at midnight,” I said.

“At 11:52. You told me to call your office.”

“I meant you, on a telephone, in the daytime.”

“It was the first opening.”

“Did Dawn reach you?”

“At 6:20. I was already on 280.”

“Then you know I said no.”

“Yes.”

I waited for the rest. The rest is the part where a man explains to me what I meant.

“That's all?” I said.

“You said no. I won't ask you again.” He looked back at the car. “This was booked before you answered her, and you told me the house was a job. If you would rather I took it to another company, say so and I'll go. I asked the driver to wait until you had decided.”

The car was at the curb behind him with its engine running. I could have had my morning back with one word.

I had my arms crossed and nothing to push against.

“Is that a real offer?” I said.

“Yes.”

“You'd get back in that car.”

“Yes.”

He was already half turned toward it.

“The house still needs clearing,” I said. “That hasn't changed since last night. Tell him he can shut the engine off.”

He raised one hand toward the car, and the engine stopped.

“Thank you for the flowers,” I said.

He was one count late. “You're welcome.”

“Is the house in a trust or in probate?” I said.

“A trust.”

“Who's the trustee?”

“I am.”

“Since when?”

“Since October.”

“So for seven months you've had the right to walk in that door and do what you like with every spoon in the place.”

“Yes.”

“Then what's kept it shut?”

He looked down at his shoes. They had wet grass on them already.

“I have,” he said.

He hadn't pretended last night either. I was going to have to stop counting on him to.

“Have you got a key on you?”

“Yes.”

“Then let's walk it. That's what an estimate is.”

He didn't move. He took an envelope out of his back pocket and held it out to me.

“You can walk it,” he said.

“I don't go through a house without the owner. I told you that in a bar.”

He lowered the envelope.

“I haven't been inside since October,” he said. “I thought I could this morning. I can't.”

“Then nobody goes in today,” I said. “You tell me what's in it, and I'll give you a rough number out here.”

He put the envelope away.

“Come here,” I said.

I walked him to my truck and pulled the ramp. It was still wet from the fog on 101.

I went up it. He came after me, and suede has no business on wet aluminum. His foot went out from under him on the second step, and I had him by the arm above the elbow before I'd decided to.

There was more arm there than I expected from a man who types for a living. I let go late, and he noticed.

“Different shoes,” I said.

“Noted.”

“Four.”

Inside the box the pads were folded in stacks and the straps hung on the rail. On a hook by the door there was a roll of green tape and a roll of blue. I took them both down.

“This is the whole method. Green goes with you. Blue goes with me, to sell or give away or haul. Nothing leaves a room without one or the other on it.”

“Who decides which?”

“You do.”

“And if I don't know?”

“Then it sits there with no tape on it until you do. I don't guess for people.”

He took the green roll out of my hand and turned it over. I think he was looking for instructions.

“What if all of it is blue?” he said.

“Sometimes it is. Then you'll have stood in every room and said so. That's not the same as never going in.”

He handed the roll back. Our fingers didn't touch, and I noticed that they hadn't.

We went back down the ramp. I made him go first, so that if he fell he would fall on the street and not on me.

“How big is it?”

“Three bedrooms. Two baths. A garage.”

“Is the garage full?”

“I don't know.”

“When do you want it empty?”

“Before my daughter leaves for college. That's the middle of September.”

“All right. There's a fast way and a slow way.” I held up one finger. “Fast, I send three guys and a truck. They walk in on a Monday with boxes, and by Wednesday night there's nothing in that house but carpet. You never set foot in it. I mail you a bill and a set of keys.”

“How much?”

“With the garage and the dump fees, about forty-five hundred.”

He nodded. It was nothing to him, and he had the sense not to say so.

“I make better money that way,” I said. “I'm telling you so you know what I'm selling. I won't do it.”

“Why not?”

“Because whoever owns the thing is in the room when it gets decided, and there's nobody left who owns that house but you.”

“Then what is the slow way?”

“You're in the room. We go one room at a time, every drawer, every coat. You say keep, sell, give or go, and I do the carrying. I bring two guys and a truck. How much time have you got?”

“Saturdays, seven to eleven. Everything else is booked through September.”

“Four hours a week.” I did it in my head. “With you deciding, that's about a room a Saturday. Three bedrooms, two baths, a kitchen, a front room, a hall and a garage nobody has looked in. Call it ten Saturdays.”

“Ten.”

“You're looking at fifteen hundred a Saturday, with the truck and the dump runs. I'll walk the house first, with you in it, and then I'll write you a top price. It can come in under that. It can't go over.”

“That is fifteen thousand dollars against forty-five hundred.”

“I know what it is,” I said.

“For the same house.”

“For the same house. The fast way, you never find out what was in it.”

He didn't answer that. He looked at the house then, for the first time since I had pulled up.

“One term,” I said. “Every Saturday, you are in that room. Seven to eleven. Nobody stands in for you. Not your assistant, not a lawyer, and not whatever booked this at midnight. If I pull up to that house and somebody else is in the driveway holding the key, I turn the truck around. The first one is a week from tomorrow, seven o'clock, and after that it's every”

“Yes,” he said.

“I'm not finished.”

“I know. Yes.”
