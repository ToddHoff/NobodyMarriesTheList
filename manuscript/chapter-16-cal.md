## Chapter 16

**Cal**

At 1:22 on Saturday afternoon a black car was parked at Jo Silveira's curb. I had not sent it.

I had planned the day myself. On Friday night I wrote it on one of my own cards, in my own handwriting, which I had never done: *1:30 Jo. 2:45 Summit Road, wine. 5:00 Los Gatos, walk.* It took me forty minutes. I was proud of it. Then I told Remy that I was planning Saturday myself and it was to book nothing.

*Understood,* it said. *Nothing new will be booked.*

I read the word new. I did not test it.

On the night of May 16 it had asked me whether to start the usual plan for her. I said yes and did not open it. Whatever it had scheduled that night was six weeks old by Friday. None of it was new. I could have worked that out with the card in my hand. I did not ask it what was coming. I wanted the day to be the card and nothing else.

At my mother's house that morning Jo had said very little to me that was not green, blue or a direction. At eleven, when I left, she said, “One-thirty. My house.” Her crew would not be done until one. I did not ask how she would make it. I drove home to Woodside and changed out of shirt number nine into number ten, which put the rotation a day ahead. I had washed my car myself on Friday night, which I had not done since the company was nine people. The fender was still the way she had left it.

She had said yes on Thursday before Vili could answer for her. I heard it again all day Friday, at intervals I did not choose. Janice did not know where I would be after one.

The car at her curb was a black sedan with the engine running. The driver was out of it, holding the rear door open for nobody. He was about sixty. Jo was on her front walk with her arms folded and a canvas bag on her shoulder.

Her hair was down. I had seen it down once, in Los Gatos, for four hours. She had on jeans and a white shirt with a collar. My watch tapped against my wrist before I had my door shut.

“Is that yours?” she said.

“No.”

The driver looked at me, and then at his phone. He had driven for my account long enough to know that a woman on a sidewalk with her arms folded was his problem and not mine.

“Pickup for Jo Silveira, one-thirty,” he said. “It says the usual.”

“The usual what?” Jo said.

“That's all it says, ma'am.”

I knew what the usual was. It was the car that waited at a woman's curb when I had her on my calendar for a Saturday. It had waited at eleven curbs before hers. I had never had to order it. I wanted it gone from her curb before she asked me anything else about it.

Jo was looking at my face. She looked at it for about as long as she looks at a staircase before she puts a crew on it.

“You didn't send it,” she said. “You'd have asked me first.”

“Yes.”

On June 16 she had made me list which parts of a month were me. This took her about two seconds. My shoulders came down an inch.

I had been ready to explain it. I had a sentence half built on the walk up from my car. Every version of it had the plan in it. I did not want to say the plan out loud on her sidewalk. She was right about the car. She did not know what was scheduled behind it. I did not tell her.

She unfolded her arms, went down the walk to the driver and took a twenty out of her back pocket.

“It's paid, ma'am.”

“Then it's paid twice. You drove all the way up here. Go on home.”

He looked at me. I said nothing. He took the twenty, shut the door he had been holding and drove away.

He had looked at me because I was the account. One word from me would have kept him at her curb. He would have held that door until I said otherwise. It was her curb. I had my hands in my pockets so that I would not wave him off myself.

She came back up the walk and stopped in front of me.

“We're taking my truck,” she said.

I had planned to drive. I looked at her and saw that she was not getting into any car today that she was not driving. I did not say anything about my car.

“Yes,” I said.

I reached for her bag. She had already moved it to her other shoulder.

“I've got it,” she said.

It was 1:31.

***

The sixteen-footer was backed into her driveway with her father's name on the door. I got in on the passenger side. This time I had the window. On Thursday I had had the middle, with my knees up and her thigh against mine from the yard to Millbrae.

She started it and looked at me.

“You've got a card in your pocket,” she said. “I can see it from here. Where am I driving?”

“You are driving to Summit Road,” I said. “It is at the top of 17. There is a winery on it.”

“You're taking me wine tasting.”

“Yes.”

“Denise bet me twenty dollars it was Napa.”

“I did not want Napa,” I said.

Every woman I had taken away for a weekend in fourteen years, I had taken to Napa. I had never picked it. It was on my calendar. I went.

She had taken the other side of her sister's bet, twenty dollars that I would pick somewhere else, before a car came to her curb that said the usual. I wanted to know what she had based it on. I did not ask.

When we were moving I took out my phone.

“Send no more cars for Jo Silveira,” I said.

*Removed,* the screen said. *Thirteen items remain. Review?*

“I will look later.”

She looked over at me, and back at the road.

“Look at what?”

“It asked whether I wanted to see what else it has scheduled for me.”

“Do you?”

“No.”

I knew what was in it the way I know what a test will say before I run it. It was the eleven before her, item by item, with 4.1 months at the top. I did not want to read what came next for her in a column.

“Ask me before you do anything for Jo Silveira,” I said to the phone.

*Set,* it said.

Jo moved her hands to the top of the wheel and looked straight ahead. The side of her face I could see went pink. She did not say anything for a mile.

I had not said it for her to hear. A word I had not tested had put a stranger at her curb holding a door. I wanted a word that would stop the next one. Then I heard where I had said it. On May 19, at the fender of her white truck, she had told me next time, ask. I had just told my own product to do it for me. I did not know whether that counted.

The pink went down the side of her neck into her collar. I watched it until she looked over. Then I looked at the road.

She took 101. She drove with her sleeves pushed up. Her forearms were brown to the elbow from the trucks. I looked at them for most of the way to Mountain View.

When the big hangar at Moffett came up on the left, she pointed at it with two fingers off the wheel. It is 1,133 feet long and nearly two hundred feet high. It was built in 1933 to hold one airship. Fog can form near its ceiling. The new siding was the silver of a laptop before the film comes off. I had to turn my head to get from one end of it to the other.

“I used to watch the zeppelin from right here,” she said. “In this lane. It came right over the freeway.”

“When was that?”

“Oh-nine. I was twenty-seven.” She looked up through the windshield. There was nothing there now. “Five hundred dollars an hour. I looked it up every week for a year.”

“An investor took me up in it,” I said. “It was in 2011. He rented it for the afternoon.”

She turned her head. “What was it like?”

“I do not know. I answered email.”

She laughed with her whole body, both hands on the wheel. She was still laughing when she took the exit for 85. I wanted to do it again. I did not know how I had done it.

I wanted to buy her a ride. Nobody flies one here now, so I could not. She had not asked me to.

She took 85 to 17. Past Los Gatos the road climbs into the hills. The reservoir below the road was full to the trees, flat and still, with the hills standing upside down in it.

“There are boats on that at six in the morning,” Jo said. “When I've got a job up here. Long skinny ones, eight people rowing and one in the back yelling.”

“That is the coxswain.”

“Is that what he is.” She watched the water go by. “Everybody pulls when he says. Same as stairs. Somebody calls it, or you drop the dresser.”

On Thursday Vili had called the dresser on the Delgados' landing. I lifted when he said and not before. The corner went past the wall with about a pencil's width to spare. Jo was at the bottom of the stairs. My hands did not close all the way until Friday afternoon. I kept looking at them.

***

At the top of 17 she took the exit for Summit Road and turned east along the ridge. The road ran under redwoods, with driveways going off into the trees. Jo put her window down, and the air that came in was ten degrees cooler than the valley and smelled of dust and bay leaves. The sun came down between the trunks in long bars. Her forearms on the wheel went from sun to shade and back.

There was an orchard on a slope beside the road, apple trees in rows with an old barn at the top. The apples on them were small and green. A table by the road had a box on it for money. Nobody was minding it.

“Honor box,” Jo said. “In the fall you take your apples and leave the money.”

“Does anyone take the box?”

“Not up here.”

“I would not have funded it,” I said.

“They didn't ask you,” she said.

A little farther on there was a small church set back in the redwoods, with an empty lot. Jo crossed herself with her right hand. Her left stayed on the wheel. She did it without looking over.

I had not known she did that. I wanted to know what else she did when she drove alone.

The winery was built like an Italian villa. There were white chairs in rows on its lawn for a wedding. A sign by the gate said members only, by reservation. A woman at the gate had a clipboard. She knew my name before I said it.

“Mr. Brennan.” She looked at the clipboard, and then at me. “I don't have you down. I'm sure we can find you a seat.”

She would have put me at a stranger's wedding before she told me no.

“We are not with the wedding,” I said. “Thank you.”

A rental truck was trying to back through the gate with a load of tables. Jo stopped to watch him.

“He's going to take that sign,” she said.

He took the sign.

I had my phone out before I knew I had taken it out. I put it back. Since 2009 Janice had made every reservation I had. Remy had made the rest. It was 2:41 on a Saturday afternoon. I was standing on Summit Road with a card in my pocket that said *wine*.

Jo held out her hand. I gave her Friday's card. She read it.

“Two forty-five,” she said. “Summit Road. Wine.”

“It is 2:41.”

“Then we're early. You're always early.” She turned it over and back. “Janice didn't write this.”

“No. I did.”

“Janice would have put down a second winery.” She gave it back. “Nobody comes all the way up here for one. Food first. Then I know where we're going.”

My plan had failed at its first stop, in front of her, four minutes before it was due. When a demo fails on a stage, I have about four seconds before the room decides what it means. I am good at those four seconds. I stood at the gate waiting for them to start. She was already walking.

The store was across the road. She knew it. She had moved four houses off Summit Road. On the way across she told me what had been in each garage.

“The last one had twenty-six cans of paint in it,” she said. “The same white, every can. He painted the house once, in 1991. After that he bought a can every time he went to the hardware store.”

“What was it for?”

“I asked his daughter. She said, ‘In case.’”

She had said more crossing that road than in four hours at my mother's house. I would have listened to a fifth garage. I had asked what the paint was for so that she would not stop.

The store was a grocery and a general store under one roof. Jo said the first one had come down in the earthquake in 1989. They built this one after. She said it the way she gives the age of a house.

I had not been inside a grocery store since Abby was small. Rita buys my food. Jo went down the aisle ahead of me without a basket and read every shelf she passed. I walked behind her. Her hair had come forward over one shoulder in the truck. I could see the back of her neck where the collar stood away from it. I wanted to put my mouth there. Two men at the register looked at her when she went past. Then they looked at me. I would have looked too.

The tri tip sandwiches were already made, in the case by the register. There was nothing to order. She took two, with two root beers out of the cooler.

At the register I put my hand to my back pocket, where I have not carried a wallet in six years. Then I took out my phone.

It lit before I could hold it up.

*Summit Store. This looks like it is for Jo Silveira. You asked me to ask. Approve?*

Jo read it upside down.

“It wants to know if you're allowed,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Are you?”

She already had two twenties on the counter, flat, under two fingers.

I could have pressed it. It would have taken less time than her twenties.

I have paid for everything since I was twenty-four. I paid off my mother's house. I paid for Abby's school and for eleven weekends in Napa. My hand knew where the button was without my looking.

I turned the phone over and put it face down on the counter beside her money. My watch tapped. I did not look at it.

“Thank you,” I said.

She looked at me for a second. Then she slid the twenties across. When the boy at the register held out her change, she told him to keep it.

She picked up the bag with the sandwiches in it. I held the door.

My ears were hot all the way back across the road. The boy at the register had watched a woman pay for my lunch and had thought nothing of it. In May I would have pressed the button before her twenties were on the counter. I would have called it manners.

She did not turn back toward 17. She drove past the store and turned off Summit Road. Half a mile on she took a narrower road that climbed through the trees.

“Where are we going?” I said.

“Silver Mountain,” she said. “Four houses I've moved up here. Every one of them told me to go up to Silver Mountain if I ever got a Saturday.”

“Have you been?”

“Not till today.”

My watch tapped against my wrist.

The trees stopped at the top of the hill. The winery was a long, plain building with a roof made of solar panels. Jo pulled in beside it and shut off the engine.

A dog came around the end of the building at a trot to meet us. He was big and brown, with his tail going. He stopped at Jo's door and looked up at it.

Jo had her hand on the door handle. She did not open the door.

On our first night she had told me she would want a railing. I knew what she would want now. I asked anyway.

“Do you want me to keep him with me?”

“Keep him with you.”

I got down on my side and went around the front of the truck. The dog met me at the bumper and put his head under my hand. I found the place behind his ears that Pancake likes. He leaned his whole weight on my leg and stayed there.

Jo got down and came around the back of the truck, the long way. She walked to the rail of the deck and stopped. I walked over and stopped beside her. The dog came with me, on my other side.

The hill went down from the deck in rows of vines. The grapes on them were green and no bigger than peas. Below the vines the mountains fell away in ridges, one behind another, dark with redwoods. Past the last ridge there was a strip of flat ground with towns on it. Past the towns was Monterey Bay, the whole of it.

Santa Cruz was on the right, close enough that I could pick out white buildings along the water. The shore ran left from there in a long curve, around the far end of the bay and back out to Monterey. Monterey was thirty-five miles off, a dark point of land with hills on it. Between Monterey and Santa Cruz the bay opened up. Outside it there was only ocean, all the way to the horizon.

The wind came up the hill off the water and blew her hair across her face. She held it back with one hand flat on top of her head. In seven Saturdays I had seen her stand still only to look at a staircase. She stood at that rail a long time.

I put my hand in my pocket for my phone, to take a picture of the bay. I took my hand out again without it. I looked at the bay. Then I looked at her. Her hair was down and the wind had it. I did not go back to the bay.

The dog went into the winery ahead of us and lay down in the middle of the floor. Nobody there was going to move him. The winery was one long room, cool after the deck, with barrels stacked to the ceiling at the back. It smelled of wine and wet oak. Jo went to the far end of the bar. I took the end nearest the dog.

The man behind the bar owned the place. He did not know who I was. He poured the Chardonnay and pointed the bottle out the door at the vines below the deck.

“Mount Eden cuttings,” he said. “I put them in in 1981. Straight in the ground, no rootstock.”

He had bought the ridge in 1979. It had been an orchard, eighteen acres that nobody had worked in years. Since then he had had floods, landslides, the Lexington fire in 1985 and the earthquake in 1989. The earthquake had started six miles south of us and knocked his winery down. He built it again. He farmed organic, with nothing synthetic on the vines. He got fewer grapes that way.

“You get less,” he said. “What you get tastes like something.”

It was getting warmer on the ridge. He was putting in more Bordeaux grapes because of it. He held his reds five or six years before he sold them.

“Why do you hold them so long?” I said.

“Because they aren't ready.”

He had been on that hill since before I was born. He wanted somebody at his bar to know how the wine was made. He talked until a car came up the hill with four more people in it.

Jo carried the glasses out to the deck herself. We ate the tri tips at a table with the bay in front of us. The sandwiches had gone warm in the truck. So had the root beers. She ate with one hand and kept her hair out of her mouth with the other.

The dog found me again and lay down across my feet, on the side of the table away from her. Jo looked at him for a while.

“Pancake's going to smell him on you,” she said.

“I will tell him it was for you.”

There was a printed list with a line under each wine about how it tasted. The Chardonnay and the Pinot had both won medals. The list said so. Jo tasted each one and poured the rest in the bucket, because she was driving. I started with the Chardonnay.

“What do you get?” she said.

I read it off the list. “Lemon peel and vanilla.”

“That's what the list gets.”

I tasted it again. I looked for the lemon peel first and then the vanilla, in the order they were printed.

“Lemon,” I said. “I do not get vanilla.”

She laughed so hard she had to set the glass down.

In Napa there had always been someone at my elbow to tell me what was in the glass. I had agreed with all of them.

The Pinot came next. I put my thumb over its line on the list and tasted it first. It tasted like cherries to me. Under the cherries it tasted like the ground on Summit Road. Jo saw my thumb. She looked out at the water and smiled. There was a drop of the Pinot on her lower lip. She took it off with her thumb. I read the whole list twice. I could not have told anyone what was on it.

When we took the glasses back in, she stopped me at the door.

“Tasting comes off if you buy wine,” she said. “So we're buying wine.”

She bought a Chardonnay and the Pinot whose line I had not read. The man took the tasting off the bill. I did the arithmetic. She had spent more than the tasting would have cost. She had two bottles of wine to show for it. She carried them out to the truck herself.

Then she came back to the rail. The dog came back to me. We stood there with the bay below us.

Janice did not know where I was. The last time that was true on a Saturday, the company had been nine people.

I told her about those Saturdays. I drove up the ridge to Castle Rock alone and climbed until my hands quit, because nobody at the office could find me there.

“Nobody at the office can find me in a truck,” she said.

I had not told anyone about those Saturdays. She answered with her own, looking out at the water. I had thought of her truck as where she worked. I wanted to ask what she drove it to get away from. I did not ask. She had told me as much as she meant to.

On the way back down she turned off just past the store, up a dirt drive to an old farmhouse where the vines run uphill into the redwoods. There was a porch, and on the porch a plank across two barrels, and a woman of about eighty pouring behind it. A cat slept in a wine crate at her feet. The sun had come around to the west. From the porch I could see the veins in the leaves. Jo sat on the top step with her glass. I sat on the step below her. After a while she put her knee against my shoulder and left it there. The woman poured a red from those vines. I kept my shoulder where it was and did not hear a word about the wine. Jo paid.

She had paid four times that afternoon, counting my driver. I had stopped reaching for my back pocket after the store. I owed her half a linguiça sandwich already. I tried to work out what the afternoon added to it. I could not get a number.

***

Los Gatos is twenty minutes down 17. It was seventy-three degrees there. It had been sixty-four in San Bruno. She parked by the lake. The water was flat and gold with the late sun on it. A heron stood in the shallows on one leg. Jo stopped to watch it. It took a fish while she watched. She said, “Oh,” out loud. We walked north on the creek trail, where the trees close over it and the light comes through green.

Bicycles came up behind us without bells. The third one came fast. Jo took my hand and pulled me onto the dirt at the edge.

She did not let go when it had passed.

I looked at my watch, which I do not do when she is beside me. She saw me look.

“Don't tell me,” she said.

I did not tell her. It was 104.

Her hand was hard across the palm, with a ridge of callus at the base of each finger. She held mine the way she holds the handle of a dolly, without looking at it. We walked a long way like that.

I could feel a pulse where our hands met. I could not tell whose it was. I did not look at the watch again.

I wanted to stop and kiss her. I did not ask. I did not want to give her a reason to let go of my hand.

North of the lake the trail ran between the creek and a string of ponds. Jo said the water district fills them so the water soaks back into the ground. The sun was low over the mountains to the west. The ponds had the sky in them, gold going orange. There were ducks out on the water. People went by the other way with their dogs. Jo watched every one of them.

Farther up the trail there was a fenced field with dogs in it, big ones on one side and small ones on the other. Jo stopped at the fence.

The field was an acre of fake grass, greener than anything else in the valley in June, with a fountain set on a short post for the dogs. The big side had eleven dogs in it. I counted. Their owners stood in a loose ring in the middle with the leashes hung around their necks, talking to each other and not watching. The light came in flat across the grass. Every dog in the field was running at something. Their shadows went out long behind them.

She keeps a truck door between herself and every dog she meets. On the mountain she had kept me between herself and the dog all afternoon. This fence ran the length of a field. She put her free hand on the wire. A gray dog came down the fence line at a dead run with a ball in its mouth. She laughed out loud.

“That's Vili,” she said, “when I call lunch.”

“Owen would rank them,” I said.

“They don't count. You have to meet them.”

“Owen never met Truck Dog.”

“He was before the rule.”

We stood at the fence a long time. I do not know how long. I did not check.

Each time one of the big dogs came down the wire, her hand closed on mine. When it had gone by, her hand opened again. She did not step back from the fence. I would have stood there until dark if she had wanted to.

“What's seven?” she said.

I took the old card out of my shirt pocket, the one with her pencil on the back. I did not need to read it.

“Comfortable being looked after,” I said. It sounded worse out loud than it had on a screen in April.

She looked at the bag on her shoulder. She had carried it since 1:31. Then she looked at me.

“I carry my own bag.”

“I know.”

“Who wanted that one?”

I took a while with it.

I could have said Remy. She knew a machine had written the list in nine seconds and that I had agreed with every line of it. That answer had not been enough in June. I went looking for one that was mine, with her hand on the wire and the dogs going by.

“I send things,” I said. “I like it when they are used.”

“And when they're not?”

“Then I send something better.”

She did not say anything. I watched her think of the three boxes on my mother's hearth. She had read the labels on the first Saturday. She had left them on the hearth with no tape on them every Saturday since.

“Why?” she said.

“I do not know.”

“Then it stays on.”

Six lines were struck on the back of that card. The last two had come off when I could tell her where they came from. Five came out of my mother's dresser drawer. Six came from 2013. Standing at that fence, I could not see where seven came from. She would not take it off on a guess.

I put the card back in my pocket. The sun was behind the hills. A man by the gate was calling a dog that did not want to go home.

I thought she would say it was time to drive back. I was working out how to ask for ten more minutes without asking for them.

Jo took her hand off the fence.

“Come down here,” she said.

I did not ask. I came down.
