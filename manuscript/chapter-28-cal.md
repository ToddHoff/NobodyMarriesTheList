## Chapter 28

**Cal**

On Monday it had been four days since I told her I loved her. I opened her thread nineteen times that morning.

The first time was at 5:04 in the morning, at the fence post by my gate. Every morning at five Pancake and I walk down my drive to the gate and back. At the gate he inspects the bottom of the same post. He takes his time. In eleven years I have not found out what he is checking for. I have run it. Deer cross at that post at night, so for the first year I had deer. Mountain lions come through Woodside. One summer I moved a mountain lion to the top. For two years the leading candidate has been a white standard poodle from three drives down, who is walked past my gate at six. Pancake is at the post an hour before she is. I have not decided what that says about him. I have not put my own nose to the post.

On Saturday he had run the waves at Carmel for an hour with a spotted dog and a little white one. Since then he had been stiff in the back legs. He stood with his nose to the post and his tail going slowly. I stood beside him and read her thread by the light of my phone.

The last thing in it was what I had sent on Friday night. *Understood. We will be at the gate at 5:55.* Since then we had not been apart long enough to need to write.

It was clear and cool. The moon was over the oaks to the south, most of it, bright enough to put the post's shadow on the gravel. The air smelled of dry grass and the bay trees. Two deer stood in the field across the road and watched us.

On the rock she had told me to say it again when my pulse was normal. At her hood my pulse had been normal for half a mile. I said it again, and she kissed me. Then she drove me down Skyline in the dark with both hands on the wheel. Four times before Saratoga Gap she took a breath through her mouth. Nothing came after any of them. On Saturday she drove me to Carmel and talked about the fog the whole way. At Ventana she put her hand on mine on the terrace in front of everybody. I turned mine over and waited. She passed me the bread. On Sunday morning on the deck I had it ready twice, over my coffee. Both times I drank the coffee.

She had told me when to say it. I had said it when she told me to. What came after that, she had not said. I had nothing to work from.

On Sunday at her counter I had read *skip Aug* in her handwriting under her NOV 2036. In July she had turned that statement around so I could read it right side up. On Sunday she turned it face down. Nineteen hundred dollars was not going on her house in August. She had not said why. I had stood in her kitchen with both hands around a mug.

At 5:38 I carried Pancake up the last three steps. He cannot do those anymore. He put his chin on my shoulder while I did it, the way he does in the car.

I opened her thread again at 6:15 with my coffee, and at 7:40 in the car, and sixteen more times before noon. Each time I read the same nine words and closed it.

Janice came in at 8:30 with a folder.

“There's a charge from a shop in Carmel,” she said. “Saturday. A shirt, pants, a sweater, socks and a toothbrush. Delivered to Big Sur. And a bag of dog food.”

“Yes.”

She looked at me over the folder.

“One toothbrush?”

“She had a shirt behind her seat.”

Janice put the folder on my desk. “Nine o'clock,” she said, and went.

At 11:52 Remy put a line on my screen.

*Relationship 12. Month four begins Friday, August 7. The last item in the plan is a gift. Prepare options?*

On Thursday night on 84 it had told me how the other eleven went. Each of them had ended within fourteen days of a large gift. I counted fourteen days from Friday on the edge of my desk with one finger. It came to August 21. Abby moves into her dorm on the eighteenth. The last Saturday at my mother's house is the twenty-second.

I opened her thread. It said what it had said at 5:04.

“It's me,” she said.

I could hear water running, and a pan going down on a burner, and Owen somewhere behind her reading out a number.

“Hang on,” she said.

“Owen wants to take you up Sweeney Ridge,” she said. “Tomorrow, after work. Seven.”

“What is on Sweeney Ridge?”

“He won't tell me. He told your robot.”

“Then I will find out at seven.”

“He says bring the dog.”

Pancake had taken nine minutes at the post that morning.

She laughed, low, with her mouth close to the phone. I was standing at my counter in the dark. I shut my eyes. I wanted her in my kitchen so much that I put my free hand flat on the stone.

“Lot two at Skyline College,” she said. “Bring water. Night, Cal.”

I had the sentence ready. I had said it twice. A third time, she would have had to answer it.

“Good night,” I said.

The call had lasted four minutes and twelve seconds.

On Friday it would be month four.

At 9:30 I sat down at the counter with my laptop. On Saturday she had let me get one night at Ventana. She had paid for the dog herself, a hundred and fifty dollars on her own card. She had said yes to the room. It was the first night she had let me pay for that was not for Owen. I took it as a number I could build on.

Kevin has Owen from Sunday. I worked it to the hour. We would leave on Tuesday the eleventh at nine. It is eleven hours to Tokyo going and about nine and a half coming back. The date line hands the day back on the way home. We would land at SFO on Friday the fourteenth at 10:40 in the morning. Abby comes to me at five.

I have been to Tokyo fourteen times. I have seen the inside of one hotel, two office towers and a stage, and a great many cars in between. From the hotel you can see Mount Fuji on a clear morning. I had been told so fourteen times. At 11:20 I looked it up. In August the air is too wet. On most days you cannot see it at all.

The eleven had gone to Napa. Nobody had been to Tokyo with me. It was not in the plan. I wanted four days with her somewhere I could see everything coming.

I held the plane for Tuesday anyway, and a suite. It would cost nothing unless we used it.

At 6:52 on Wednesday evening I parked in Lot 2 at Skyline College, in the corner the park keeps for people walking up to the ridge. The college sits on the hill above San Bruno. From the lot I could see the roofs of San Bruno going down the hill toward the airport. Behind the buildings the hill kept going up into brush. Over the top of it the fog was coming, white against the blue, so slowly I had to look away and back to see it move.

Her pickup came in at 7:04. Jo got out in my fleece.

It was the one I had held out to her at my gate on Saturday morning. She had it zipped to the collar with the sleeves pushed up past her wrists. My stomach dropped and stayed down.

She had kept it. She had worn it where she knew I would see it. I wanted that to be an answer. I did not know whether it was one.

She stood by her door and looked at a row of spaces along the buildings.

“I parked here for two years,” she said. “Class at eight, the yard by one. Never went up.”

In June she had told me she did two years here, business, and then the trucks needed somebody. She was looking at one space in particular. I let her look.

Owen came around the truck with his backpack on both shoulders and shook my hand, one pump, looking at me. Then he looked into my car.

“Where's Pancake?”

“He is at home,” I said. “He cannot walk four miles anymore. His back legs have been stiff since the beach.”

Owen looked at me for some time. Then he unzipped his backpack and took out a mixing bowl. He looked at it. He put it back and zipped the pack and put it on again.

It was the bowl from the back of Jo's truck at Carmel. She had poured my dog water in it on the asphalt. Owen had carried it from his mother's kitchen so that my dog could drink out of it on top of a ridge.

I could have had Pancake on top of the ridge by eight. There is a paved road up from Sneath Lane, closed to cars. Somebody has the key to the gate. It would have taken two calls. I kept my phone in my pocket and walked beside him.

The trail went up a fire road around a hill of coastal scrub, gray-green and waist high, gone dry at the tips in August. Where our sleeves brushed it the air went sharp and medicinal.

“Smells like my dad's knees,” Jo said.

I did not know what that meant. She went up ahead of me with her hands in the pockets of my fleece.

At three quarters of a mile we passed old concrete foundations in the brush, with grass coming up through the cracks. Owen left the trail to walk across them.

“Owen,” Jo said. “Poison oak.”

There was a bush of it at the edge of the concrete, leaves in threes, going red at the edges. I had walked past it without seeing it. Owen came back to the trail the long way around it.

Past the foundations the trail dropped into a ravine so steep that she went down it sideways in her boots, one foot set before she moved the other, the way she takes a loaded ramp. She climbed out the other side without stopping. I came up behind her. The whole climb out I watched her hips go up in front of me in my fleece. My watch tapped against my wrist. I let it.

Owen walked ahead of both of us and said nothing for a mile and a half.

At a mile and a half the trail came up onto the ridge at a cluster of low cement-block buildings. Their roofs had fallen in. Every wall was painted over in six colors, names and initials sprayed on top of each other for fifty years. Owen walked into the middle of them and stood there.

“They had missiles up here,” he said. “Nuclear ones. They could shoot them in one minute.”

“This was the radar,” I said. “It found the planes and aimed the missiles. The missiles were on Milagra Ridge.”

He thought about that.

“Remy said missiles.”

“It is a missile site. The missiles were somewhere else.”

“That's misleading,” Owen said.

Jo laughed out loud. She had to put her hand on a wall.

“Yes,” I said. “I will tell them on Monday.”

He looked at me to see whether I meant it. I did. He went and read the paint on the nearest wall with his hands behind his back.

The last half mile was a paved road along the ridge, past a water tank, and then a few hundred feet of gravel. Owen dropped back on the road and walked beside me. Jo was twenty yards ahead with her hands in the pockets of my fleece.

“She's never been up here,” Owen said. “I asked her in June. She said she'd come when the house is paid.”

He said it to the road.

“So I told her you wanted to see where they found the bay. Then I got Remy to tell me all of it, so it'd be a real thing.”

“I did not say I wanted to see it.”

“You came, though.”

The wind hit us at the water tank. It came over the ridge from the ocean, steady, cold enough to make my eyes run, and it had the fog in it. I could feel the wet on my face.

At the top there was a granite post with the names of the mountains around the bay cut into it. West of it there was nothing but white.

The fog lay on the ocean side of the ridge, level with our boots, a field of it going out to where the ocean should have been. The sun was low over it. The top of it was gold. In the wind it came up over the edge in pieces and went past us, wet and cold, and ten feet down the other side each piece came apart and was gone. I put my hand out into one. It came back wet.

On the other side it was clear. The whole bay was below us, flat and silver in the evening light, from the airport out as far as I could see in both directions. The runways went out into it. To the southeast the San Mateo Bridge ran across it, low and long, with the sun on the cars. Past the water the East Bay hills were gold, and over them Mount Diablo stood up by itself. Far to the north, over the fog lying on the city, one more mountain stood up alone, blue, with the sun on its top.

“Tamalpais,” Owen said, reading the post.

I have taken off from the airport under that ridge more than four hundred times. I had my laptop open for most of them. I had never stood up here.

Past the bridge the bay went on south into the haze. My mother's house was at the bottom of it, thirty miles off, on West Remington Drive. I could not see that far.

Jo had stopped on the gravel behind me. She was looking at the bay with her mouth a little open and her hands still in the pockets of my fleece.

Owen was looking at her.

Owen stood at the post with his hand flat on top of it.

“In 1769 they walked here from San Diego,” he said. “It took almost four months. They were lost and sick and they didn't have any food. They were looking for Monterey.” He looked at me to make sure I had it. “They walked right past it. They didn't know what it looked like.”

“Then the Ohlone walked them up here,” Owen said. “The ones from Pacifica. They already knew it was here.” He looked out at the bay for a while. “So it doesn't count.”

“You've been busy,” Jo said.

“Remy did the dates,” Owen said.

Jo looked at me.

“The dates were right,” I said.

Owen went around to the other side of the post to find Diablo.

Jo came and stood in front of me with her back to the wind. Then she leaned back against my chest. I put my arms around her, over my fleece. Her hair came loose. The wind blew it out in front of her toward the bay. The back of her neck was bare. I wanted my mouth on it. Under my hands I could feel her breathing slow down, the climb going out of her. I wanted her so much that I held still.

She stood against me and watched the bay. I waited for her to say it. I could feel each breath she took through the fleece. I counted them. At nine I stopped, because I was not counting to anything. I had been waiting since Thursday. My watch tapped against her hip.

Out over the bay a line of lights hung in the air, one behind another, each one a plane with its lights on, coming in toward the runways under us. I counted eight. While I counted, the one in front came down onto the runway and a new one showed at the back of the line, far out over the water.

Then a wide-body came off the airport the other way, toward us, climbing west into the wind. It went over the ridge to the north of us and into the fog. The sound came after it was gone and kept coming.

“May I take you to Tokyo for four days?” I said.

She did not turn around. “When?”

“We would leave next Tuesday at nine. We would land on Friday at 10:40 in the morning. Owen is at Kevin's that week. Abby comes to me at five.”

“You've got a plane.”

“I am holding one.”

“Holding.”

“A hold costs nothing until it is used.”

She laughed once, through her nose.

“I can't leave on a Tuesday,” she said.

“May we leave on Wednesday?”

“I can't leave on a Wednesday either.”

She said it to the bay. Her back was against my chest. I felt her say it more than I heard it.

“Do you really need to work this much?” I said.

I heard it after I said it.

“Yes,” she said.

Under my hands her hands closed into fists in the pockets of my fleece. The back of her neck went red from the collar up. Her face does that when she has been caught at something. I did not know what I had caught her at.

She stood against me a second more. Then she stepped out of my arms and went around the post to Owen. The wind came in where she had been.

It was line eleven. *Doesn't need to work. Free to travel.* I had agreed with it in April, with the other eleven lines, in about the time it took Remy to write them. Since May she had struck eight lines in carpenter's pencil on the back of my card. From line five on she had struck one only when I could tell her why it was there. I could not have told her why this one was. I had just said it to her on a ridge, in my own words, with my arms around her.

The sun went down into the fog at 8:13. The top of the fog held the light for a minute after it was gone. On our side the light went up the East Bay hills and stayed on top of Diablo after everything else had lost it. Below us San Bruno's streetlights came on in rows.

“Our house is right there,” Owen said. He pointed into about four thousand houses.

“One of those,” Jo said.

I looked anyway. I knew her street, and her corner, and her kitchen, which is eleven feet long. I could not find it from up there.

She would take a day off when the house was paid. I knew what she owed on it to the cent. I had known since July.

At 4:06 that afternoon I parked on her street of warehouses. The wind was coming off the ocean through the gap in the hills, fifteen knots at the airport and gusting to twenty-three. In her yard it had nothing to stop it.

Jo was on the dock with her boot on a stack of pads. Every time the wind came, the top pad lifted at the corner and she pressed it back down. The white truck was against the back fence with its hood up. Somebody had tied a tarp over the engine at the corners. It cracked in the wind.

Jo watched him do it with her boot on the pads. Then she saw me at the gate.

Jo looked at the office. Then she looked at me. “Course she did.”

“May I get you a truck?”

“No.”

“No.”

She said both of them to the white truck, not to me. Behind her Frank had rolled up the back of the sixteen-footer and was pulling pads off the load one at a time and dropping them on the dock. He kept his eyes on the pads and listened to every word.

“What is wrong?” I said.

She looked at me then. Her eyes were red at the rims from the wind. She had a pad over her shoulder, and her hair had come out of its tie on one side. I wanted to put it back.

She went past me down the dock steps and across the yard to the office, with the pad still on her shoulder. The office door shut behind her.

Frank came over with a pad in his hands. He held one end of it out to me.

I took my end.

“Corners,” Frank said.

He stood with his hip against the back of the truck to take the weight off his knees. I took the two corners at my end and he took the two at his. The pads are six feet by six feet eight, quilted, blue, with a binding around the edge that is coming loose on most of them. They smell of the inside of a truck and of every house they have been in. The wind took the first one out of my hands. It went around my legs and then my head before I got it back. Frank waited.

“Back to the wind,” he said.

I turned my back to it.

“Thirds.”

He laid his long side in a third of the way, and I laid mine over it.

“Walk it in.”

I walked my end to him, and he took it out of my hands.

“Again.”

He folded it once more against his chest and dropped it on the stack. It landed square.

We did forty-eight. It was every pad on the truck. He called every fold and said nothing else. By the tenth I knew the order and was turning my back before he said it. By the twentieth he had stopped saying “Corners.” Twice I looked up and saw Jo in the office window with the phone at her ear. The second time she was looking at us. When she saw me see her, she turned away from the glass.

When the last one was on the stack Frank looked at it. There were forty-eight pads, every edge lined up with the one under it.

“Thank you.”

He went to his chair and let himself down into it with both hands on the arms. The office light was still on when I drove out.
