## Chapter 26

**Cal**

In the garage, after she said *Or nothing*, I asked her where she wanted to sign it. She said anywhere my office could not find me. There is no cell signal at Castle Rock. I told her there was a waterfall.

“I'll drive,” she said.

She picked me up in Woodside at 4:47 that afternoon. Pancake watched us go from the front window. The park does not allow dogs.

At the top of 84, where it crosses Skyline, she pulled in at Alice's Restaurant. It is a low wooden building at a crossroads in the redwoods. It was built in 1918 as a general store for loggers. Thirteen motorcycles were backed in along the front of it at one angle, their engines ticking as they cooled. The men who rode them stood around in leather with gray in their beards, holding coffee and pointing at each other's machines. There was an old silver Porsche with a man asleep in the passenger seat. Over the redwoods I could smell the grill.

“Dinner,” Jo said. “Unless your robot packed you one.”

“It did not.”

“Named after the song.”

“It was named for the woman who owned it. She bought it in 1960.”

“You looked that up.”

“I looked it up in 2009.”

“Course you did.” She got out.

She came back with two burgers in a paper bag and set them on my knees.

“What do I owe you?” I said.

“A waterfall.”

At the entrance to the park a sign said the gate locks at sunset. Jo read it, drove past it and parked on the shoulder with two wheels in the dirt.

At 5:38 she let her tailgate down and laid my two pages on it. She took the pencil from behind her ear.

I had written them myself between 2:20 and 3:15, at my desk, with the door shut. Nobody else was in the document. At the top of a blank page I typed what she had said to me in the garage, so I would not drift from it. *Two pages. Your name in every place mine is. Your people in it the same as my people. Your robot too.* Then I wrote to the spec. It came to two pages and four lines. I cut the four lines.

She read the first page standing up, with her weight on one hip and the pencil an inch off the paper. I have watched a great many people read a term sheet. They read the number first and the names last. Jo started at the top and read every line in order, the date included. She read it the way she walks a house, one room at a time.

Page one said neither of us would tell anyone about the other's health, money, house, company, children or dog, or post a word or a picture of the other anywhere, for as long as either of us lived.

I had approved the other one in a car.

On Wednesday at 6:24 in the morning, three minutes after she told me *I sign it* and hung up, Remy had put a second item on my screen. *Confidentiality agreement for Jo Silveira, prepared from your three prior agreements. Approve?* I was still hearing her say it. I said approve. It was one of three hundred and six things I approved that day. I did not open it.

At 2:05 this afternoon, after her truck went down the ramp, I opened it and read all fourteen pages. When my two were done I asked Remy for the three it was made from, and I read those. I had approved every page of them too. I wrote three letters on my own paper, one to each woman who had signed one, Ms. Holcomb first. Their addresses were in the notice lines. Each letter said she was released from every term and owed me nothing under it. I signed them and put the stamps on myself. I mailed them on the way to San Carlos.

I had not told Jo about the letters. They were owed to three other people.

She turned to the second page. I watched her read it. She had changed her shirt since the garage. Her hair was down. I kept my hands in my pockets.

She stopped at section four and put the point of the pencil on it.

“Your robot's in,” she said.

The line said *Calvin Brennan, Recall Technologies, and the agent known as Remy*.

“Yes.”

“Does it sign?”

“I sign for it.”

She looked at me for a while. Then she went back to the page.

Section five was where I had copied her people onto my side. *Jo Silveira will see to it that her employees, agents and family members do the same. Calvin Brennan will see to it that his employees, agents and family members do the same.* The pencil stopped on *family members* and stayed there.

“I can't sign for my father,” she said. “He talks after Mass.” She drew one line through *family members* in her sentence. It was straight. “Or Owen. Or Denise.”

“I cannot sign for Abby,” I said. “She tells people what she thinks of me.”

“Good.”

She held out the pencil. I drew the line through *family members* in mine. My line was not as straight. She wrote her initials beside hers in square capitals, JS, and I wrote CB beside mine. There was a gray smear of pencil along the side of her hand where it had dragged across the paper. I wanted to put my mouth there.

Then she crossed out *employees, agents* in her sentence and wrote THE COMPANY PAGE above it.

“Do I change my side?” I said.

“Your side's got a robot. Leave it.”

It was no longer the same on both sides. I initialed her change.

Section six was the number. I had copied it from the old one. *Two hundred and fifty thousand dollars for each breach, payable by either party.* She read it twice. She did not look up.

“What's that to you?” she said.

“It is twenty-three millionths of what I have.”

She wrote 42 in the margin.

“That's forty-two months on my house,” she said.

I knew her payment. I had run it at her table in July, principal and interest and the nineteen hundred she put on top. I ran it again on the tailgate. It came to forty-two months and two weeks. I had written the same number on both sides and called it the same.

She drew a line through the number. Then she turned the page sideways and wrote along the margin in her capitals, with the pencil flat so it would take.

IF EITHER OF US BREAKS IT WE'RE DONE.

She turned the page around to me.

I read it twice. Since 2013 every agreement with my name on it had a number in it, so that no woman could sell what she knew about Abby. There was no number in this one. I initialed the margin. She initialed under me.

She signed on her line in carpenter's pencil. She handed me the pencil. I signed on mine.

There was one copy. She folded it in thirds and put it in her glove box, on top of the registration.

The shoes were in a bag on her back seat with a crash pad, a chalk bag and two headlamps.

I had bought them myself at REI in San Carlos at 4:24. At 2:12 I had sent her one line. *What size shoe do you wear?* She wrote back *7.5. Why.* My own climbing shoes were in a bin in my garage, the pair I had bought in 2008. The rubber had gone hard. I bought two pairs. At the register I asked the man to leave the receipt in her box.

She opened the box on the tailgate and found the receipt under the tissue. She got her wallet out of the cab and counted the number onto the tailgate, to the cent, in bills and change. I had known she would want to pay for her own shoes.

“Thank you,” I said. I put the bills in my shirt pocket with the card and the coins in my jeans.

She sat on the tailgate and pushed one foot into a shoe.

“They pinch.”

“They are supposed to pinch.”

“Huh,” she said. She took it off and put it back in the box.

“What's that?” she said. “A mattress?”

“It is a crash pad. It is what you land on when you come off.”

It folds in half and has shoulder straps. It weighs ten pounds. I had the burgers in the shoe bag and was reaching for the pad when she lifted it off the seat and got her arms through the straps.

***

The trail to the rock is a quarter mile, uphill, in the shade. She walked ahead of me with the pad on her back. I had walked it alone thirty-eight times. In 2009, when the company was nine people, I came up here on Saturdays and climbed by myself. I had not been back.

The trail goes up through black oaks and madrones. A madrone sheds its bark every summer. The old bark comes off in thin red curls, and the trunk under it is green and cool, like the inside of a wrist. Jo put her hand flat on one as she went by. Then she stopped and put her hand on the next one, and left it there a while.

Castle Rock is a sandstone block the size of a house, set in the trees. Its whole face is full of holes. Rain soaks into the stone and dissolves what holds the grains together. The inside crumbles out from behind a hard skin. The holes are called tafoni. Some are the size of a fist. Some go back far enough for two people to sit in. The late sun came through the trees onto it in pieces. Every hole was full of shadow.

Jo walked once around it. She put her head into the biggest cave and looked at the ceiling.

“Five by eight,” she said. “Six high at the front.”

“I ate lunch in that one.”

“Alone.”

“Yes.”

She looked at the cave a little longer. Then she slid the pad off, crawled in, and sat with her back against the far wall and her knees up.

“Come down here,” she said.

I ducked in. There was room for two people if they did not mind each other. The stone was cool against my back after the sun. She took my face in her hand and kissed me, slowly. My watch tapped on my wrist. I put both hands on her waist.

Then she climbed out and sat down on the pad and put the shoes on.

I showed her the lowest problem on the north side, a short wall with good pockets and a flat landing. I put the pad down where she would land and pointed out the first three holds. Then I stood behind her with my hands up and my thumbs against my fingers, so that if she came off I would not break them on her. I had read how to spot a climber in 2008 and had never done it, because I climbed alone.

She put her right foot into a pocket and pressed it before she trusted it. She does that on a ramp. Then she went up.

She did not hurry. Every time she moved one hand she had the other three set. Her forearms stood out. There was chalk on the back of her jeans where she had wiped her hand. I kept my hands two inches from her back. I wanted them on it. My pulse was up. She was the one climbing.

She reached the top of the wall, looked over it, and came back down the same way. At the last hold she let go and dropped onto the pad. She landed on her feet. My hands were still up.

“That's it?” she said.

“That is the first one.”

She did the second one. Then she did the third, which had taken me four tries. She did it on her second.

I went up the third one after her. I came off at the fourth move and landed on my back on the pad. Then I came off at the fourth move again. The third time I put my feet where she had put hers. I got to the top.

When I dropped onto the pad she had her hands up behind me with her thumbs in. They caught me under the ribs for a second. Then she stepped back.

***

The falls I had promised her were a dark streak down a rock face, seventy-five feet of it, with nothing coming over the top.

We looked at it from the platform at the end of the falls trail. A little green grew at the bottom where the water would have been. The rest of the canyon was dry grass and brush going gold. Below the platform the canyon went down and away for miles, one ridge folded into the next.

“That's your waterfall,” Jo said.

“It was a waterfall in March of 2009.”

She laughed. She put her hand over her mouth and laughed into it.

“You quoted me a waterfall off a job you did seventeen years ago,” she said.

“Yes.”

She stood at the rail with the pad on her back and looked at the dry streak a while longer. I looked at her. A strand of her hair was stuck to the chalk on her cheek, where she had pushed it back with her hand. I took it off with one finger. She let me.

Goat Rock is a hundred feet of sandstone at the end of the ridge, a mile from the road. We got there at 7:58. Climbers rope up on the front of it. From the back there is a way to the top with hands and feet.

She went first. I had planned to go first. She was at the top before I had decided where to put my second foot.

At the top it was still warm. The sandstone had been in the sun all afternoon and gave the heat back through my jeans. The whole San Lorenzo Valley was below us, ridge after ridge of redwoods going down toward the sea, black green in the folds and gold on the open slopes where the grass had dried. To the south the long ridge of Ben Lomond Mountain blocked most of the ocean. Past the end of it Monterey Bay was clear, flat and pale, with Santa Cruz along the near edge. Farther out the fog lay on the ocean in a gray bank, low, under us. Two turkey vultures came along the face of the rock below our feet, rocking side to side, close enough that I could see their red heads. There was no sound but the wind in the dry grass.

Jo stood at the edge with her hands on her hips. The wind put her shirt flat against her back. I wanted my hand there.

My phone said No Service. I took it out three times in ten minutes. It said the same thing each time.

“It's not going to change its mind,” Jo said.

I put it away. I took off my watch and put it in my pocket too.

She sat down on the rock with her knees up. I sat down beside her and opened the bag. Her shoulder was against my arm. The pad was behind us.

Somebody at Alice's had written on the foil in marker. Mine said BMW. Hers said JALOPY.

“It's got Wagyu,” Jo said. “Don't get used to it.”

I had finished mine before she had hers unwrapped. She looked at my empty foil and gave me half of hers.

“You told me about the red one,” she said.

It took me a second.

“The ribbons,” she said. “You told me about the mold bread.”

“The blue one from 1995 was a coin sorter,” I said. “I was in the fourth grade. I drew it on graph paper first. It was a plywood ramp with four holes cut in it, smallest at the top. A dime is seven-tenths of an inch. A penny is three-quarters. The nickel is next, and the quarter is at the bottom. Every coin rolls down until it finds a hole it fits. Under each hole there was a cup.”

“Did it work?”

“It worked the first time. I poured in the jar from on top of our refrigerator. There was fifty-three dollars and sixty cents in it. Every dime went in the first cup.”

“You took your mother's jar.”

“I put it back sorted.”

She laughed again, quieter.

“I ran the jar through it twice,” I said.

“Why twice?”

“I wanted to hear it again.”

I had not told anyone that. I could still hear it. The sorter sat on the floor of our garage on West Remington Drive, with the door up. The first dime went down the ramp, found its hole and dropped into the cup. Nothing in the garage was wrong. It did what the drawing said. I sat on the concrete and listened to fifty-three dollars and sixty cents go into four cups.

“What did she say?” Jo said.

“She asked whether I had washed my hands.”

Jo put her forehead down on her knees and laughed until she had to wipe her eyes on her sleeve.

The sun went down behind the ridges to the west at 8:18. The sandstone under us went orange and then a dull red. The bank of fog went pink on top. Monterey Bay went gray. Jo watched the fog with her chin on her knees. There was chalk on her cheekbone again. Her mouth was a little open. The light was going off her face one degree at a time. I watched it go.

I have a word for most things. Since May I had been trying to find the one for this and file it. I had tried *test condition*, and *anomaly*, and *the evening I did not want to end*. None of them held. At 12:31 that morning I had sat down at her table. I had read three hundred and twelve reviews to find nine people. I would have read three thousand.

I had nothing written down. I bring a card to everything that matters, and I run what is on it until I know where each line will land. Everything I have said that mattered since 2009 I said first to Janice, or to a room, or to the mirror in a hotel bathroom. The only card I had on that rock was in my shirt pocket, and it was hers. Seven of my lines were struck through on the back of it in her pencil.

I could not run this one. She might laugh into her hand, as she had at the falls. She might say nothing at all, and get up, and drive me home down Skyline, and I would sit beside her for forty minutes with it said. In May Remy had told me how long my relationships last. It was four point one months. It was twelve weeks to the day since the Pied Piper. My pulse was in my neck. I put my hand flat on the rock beside her. It was still warm.

At 8:19 I tried the plain word. It held.

“I love you,” I said.

She turned her head and looked at me. I counted to twenty-six before she moved.

I counted because I did not know what else to do with the time. At four I thought she would laugh. At eleven I started to build what I would say on the walk down if she said nothing. I stopped. She looked at my face the whole time. I let her.

Then she put her hand flat on my chest, on my shirt, over the pocket with the card and her shoe money in it. My heart was going hard under her hand. She could feel it.

“Say it again when your heart's not going like that,” she said.

I had been sitting on that rock for twenty-one minutes. It was not the climb.

I have sat across tables from people who were about to tell me no since I was twenty-three. I know what they do with their hands. Jo kept hers where it was.

The sun had been down four minutes. There was a mile of trail and about twenty-five minutes of light.

“We should start down,” I said. I did not move.

“Then get up.”

***

We came down the ridge in the last of the light. She went first with the pad on her back. At the trees I put a headlamp on her and one on me. Hers sat crooked over her hair. She fixed it herself.

On Skyline her truck was the only one left on the shoulder. My pulse had been normal for half a mile. I stood on my side of the hood and she stood on hers.

“I love you,” I said.

She came around the front of the truck and kissed me. She put her hands on my face. They still had chalk on them. Her mouth was warm. She kissed me for a long time with her headlamp still on, so that the trees behind me lit up and went dark when she moved. I do not know what time it was when she stopped. She kept her forehead against my chest.

I stood still and waited for her to say it. Under her forehead my pulse was up again.

“Get in,” she said.

She drove us down Skyline in the dark with her high beams on and both hands on the wheel. She did not talk. I watched the road come at us in the lights. Four times before Saratoga Gap she took a breath through her mouth. Each time I held mine so I would hear her over the engine.

In between I went back over everything she had said to me since May, looking for a second condition. I read a contract that way, for the clause that will cost me. On the rock she had given me one condition. I had met it at her hood. I could not find another. Her hands stayed at ten and two. I could see them in the light off the dash. I could not see her face.

I kept still from Saratoga Gap to 84. I had a question ready the whole way. I did not ask it, because I did not know what I would do with the answer.

On 84, coming down into Woodside, my phone found a signal and shook in my pocket. It kept shaking. There were two hundred and four messages and four missed calls from Janice. The last message was about the meeting I had moved from 4:00 and not explained.

I did not answer any of them.

She had not said it back. I wanted to know what came next. The only record I had was the eleven. I opened Remy below the dash, where the light would not reach her side, and typed.

*How did the other eleven end?*

It took two seconds.

*Each of the eleven ended within fourteen days of a large gift. Average gift value: $31,400. In each, her last message was a thank-you for the gift. No final conversation is on record. Twelve items remain in the plan for relationship 12. Review?*

I had approved all eleven of those gifts. I could not have named one of them.

Line ten of the list said *Never married*. None of the eleven had been. I had approved it in April in about a minute. Jo had been married for eleven years. She was driving me home with her eyes on the road. There was chalk on my face where she had held it.

I closed the screen.
