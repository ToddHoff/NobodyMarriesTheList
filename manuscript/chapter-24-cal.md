## Chapter 24

**Cal**

I drove the meat loaf to Sunnyvale on the passenger seat with the belt across it.

It was my mother's meat loaf. The card was in the green tin, in her blue ballpoint, MEAT LOAF across the top and the oven at 350 for one hour. In the corner she had written how many it served. The 2 had a line through it and a 1 beside it.

I made it for two.

I had made it twice. The first one went in at 10:50 on Monday night, after dinner at Frank Silveira's house. I followed the card to the quarter teaspoon. At 11:52 it was gray on the outside and pink in the middle. The card did not say to heat the oven first. My mother had not needed it to. The card had no onion on it. I gave Pancake a slice. He thought it was the best thing I had ever made. I put the rest in the refrigerator for him. The second went in at 5:40 this morning, after the walk. Pancake lay in front of the oven for the full hour and watched it through the glass. It came out brown all the way through.

Abby was coming on the fourteenth. I had decided to cook every card in the tin that had a 2 on it, in the order my mother filed them. I would make the mistakes on someone who would tell me.

Jo had saved the closet across from the hall bathroom for tonight.

On Saturday she had worked around that door all morning. She packed the frames on one side of it and my room on the other and never put her hand on the knob. That afternoon, after Vili had gone, she told me that closet would wait for a weeknight with no crew.

“Tuesday,” she said. “After my last job.”

She knew what was in it.

I knew too. I had walked past that closet door on six Saturdays since the hutch and had not asked her where the boxes were.

I did not want to see them.

I knew what they would be a count of.

***

I parked on West Remington Drive at 6:31 on Tuesday evening. Jo had said seven. I did not sit in the car.

I let myself in with my key, turned on my mother's oven and put the meat loaf in it to warm. The kitchen was nearly empty. The table was still there, and the coffee maker, because Jo says everybody needs the coffee maker till the end. The house had held the heat of the afternoon. The hall said seventy-nine. Pancake went to the slab and lay down on it with his legs out behind him.

I stood in the hall and looked at the closet door. I could have opened it at 6:40, before she came. I wanted her in the hall when I did.

Her truck turned onto Remington at 7:14. It was the sixteen-footer. She backed it into the driveway in one pass and sat in the cab for a minute before she got down.

There was dust on her from the knees down and in the creases of her shirt. She had pad lint in her hair on one side. Her face was tired in a way I had not seen on a Saturday. I wanted to take the lint out of her hair. I put my hands in my pockets.

“Belmont ran long,” she said. “Two loads.”

Her company has three trucks. The white one takes a three-bedroom in one load. She had done a three-bedroom in Belmont in the small one, twice. Two loads is two round trips. She would have started before six.

At Kevin's car he had told her that in eleven years she had asked him for one thing. On 280 afterward I counted the things Jo had told me to do since May 7. I was at thirty-eight by the Woodside exit. Every one had been an order.

She had said “Work.” I did not ask about the truck.

“There is dinner,” I said.

She stopped in the doorway with her clipboard.

“You cooked.”

“I cooked twice. You are getting the second one.”

***

We ate on the bench in the atrium with the plates on our knees. On Saturday we had eaten her sandwiches there. The sun was low enough to come straight through the windows on the far side. It made the gravel orange.

She ate the first bite and chewed it for a while.

“It's dry,” she said.

“The card said one hour at three hundred and fifty degrees.”

“Then the card's dry.”

She ate the rest of the slice and held her plate out for another.

“Abby is coming in seventeen days,” I said.

“So you've got seventeen more of these.”

“I have twenty-four cards with a 2 on them.”

She laughed with her mouth full and had to put her hand over it.

“Your father said to bring me back,” I said.

“I heard him. So did the neighbors.”

“When is back?”

“With him that's Sunday or next July.”

“Then I will take Sunday.”

“Sunday he goes to Mass first. Then he sits in the boat.”

“What time is Mass?”

She looked at me to see whether I meant it. I meant it.

When there was one piece left she took it without asking. It was the end, with the crust on three sides. My mother gave me the end every time. I had not remembered that until I watched Jo eat it. My mother cut the loaf from the far end, so the first slice was mine. She ate from the middle. I ate the end at her table every time she made it, until I left in 2003. I did not know which piece she liked.

Jo ate it in three bites and wiped her fingers on her jeans.

I wanted to put my hand on the back of her neck, under the hair, where the dust stopped. I took her plate instead and stacked it on mine. She let me.

“Okay,” she said, and stood up. “Your closet.”

***

Jo stood back from the door with her clipboard against her chest and waited for me to open it.

I opened it.

The closet was full of boxes to the shelf. They were brown, all one size, with the labels facing out. RECALL TECHNOLOGIES · OFFICE OF C. BRENNAN. Each one had a plastic window on the side for the packing slip. Every window was empty. I knew where the slips were.

She had stacked them by date, oldest at the bottom left. There was not one box out of order.

Every box sat square on the one under it, with the edges lined up. I square whatever I put down. On Sunday Abby had squared my card to the edge of Stephanie's table. My hands were cold. The hall said seventy-nine.

I counted them against the slips. There were thirty-four.

Above the boxes, on the top shelf, were shoeboxes in two stacks, with a year written on the end of each one in blue ballpoint.

Jo had not moved.

“You knew,” I said.

“I guessed. In May.”

“You guessed from the slips.”

“Thirty-four boxes that size fill a closet to the ceiling. Pat never saw one in the recycling.” She looked at them. “I've done a lot of closets.”

“I counted the slips too, on May 23,” I said. “I did the same arithmetic.”

She looked at me then. She took both rolls of tape off her belt and held them out, the green and the blue, the way she had at the hutch.

I did not take either one.

“I would like to open them,” I said.

“All of them?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the closet and the hall floor. She had been on her feet since before six that morning. It was 7:48.

“Okay,” she said, and sat down on the slab with her back against the bathroom door frame and the clipboard on her knees.

***

She gave me her box cutter. On the first box I ran the blade in too deep and opened the box inside it too.

“Short,” she said. “Just the tape.”

I kept the blade short after that.

The first box was May 2014, Mother's Day. Inside it was a smaller box in good paper, with a gift receipt on top in a printed envelope. It was the same paper Abby's lamp had come in on Sunday. Inside the paper was a cashmere wrap, the color of oatmeal, folded around a sheet of tissue.

“One wrap,” Jo said, and wrote it down. “Cashmere. Tags on.”

The second box was Christmas 2014. It was candles, three of them, in glass, still in the shrink wrap. The third was March 2015, my mother's birthday. It was the wrap.

“That's two,” Jo said.

I read the gift receipt. It had no price on it. It had the name of the item, an item number and the line *Ordered by: Office of C. Brennan.*

By 2019 the light had gone off the back windows. By 2021 Jo had turned on the hall light.

There was a white robe with the tags on, and slippers in size seven, and a tea sampler, and a blender with nine speeds in its foam. There were chocolates from 2016 that had gone white on the outside.

“They do that,” Jo said. “It's the fat coming up.”

There was a heated throw, and a picture frame that showed photographs on a screen, with a card in the box that said the photographs had been loaded for my mother. I did not turn it on. There was a bath set, two more sets of candles, and a second robe, the same robe.

Seven of the receipts had the same item number.

I sat on the slab with the seventh wrap in my lap. I own forty of the same gray shirt. I had not known what my office was sending my mother. I had approved the standing order once, in 2014. It had run without a failure since.

My mother had carried every one of these in from the porch herself. She would not let the driver. She had never opened one.

I did not need to look up where I had been on those days. Most of them I had spent on a plane.

Jo called every item out loud. Each time I said green or blue, she wrote it down and read it back. It took two hours and four minutes. Pancake came and lay down across the hall with his chin on Jo's boot. She let him.

Everything went blue except one wrap.

“Green,” I said, of the last wrap.

Jo wrote it down. I did not know what I wanted with it.

***

At 9:52 there was nothing left in the closet but the shoeboxes on the top shelf.

“Top shelf's yours,” Jo said. “My ladder's in the truck.”

I took them down one at a time and set them on the slab in a row. There were fourteen. On the end of each one was a year, in the same blue ballpoint as the height marks on the bathroom door. They ran from 2012 through 2025.

I opened 2012.

There were two white envelopes in it. On the front of each my mother had written *Calvin, house*, with the month under that. *Nov 2012. Dec 2012.*

I opened November. Inside were thirty twenty-dollar bills, a ten and two ones. It was six hundred and twelve dollars.

I did not need to count the others. I counted them anyway. The 2013 shoebox had twelve envelopes, one for every month, and so did every box after it through 2024. The 2025 box had ten. The last envelope said *Oct 2025.*

My mother died on October 8. The payment would have been due on the first. Two days before she died she left me a voicemail. Remy's summary of it said it was not urgent, to call when I had a minute. I had never played it.

I opened October 2025 too. It was the same, thirty twenties, a ten and two ones. I counted it twice.

There were one hundred and fifty-six envelopes. They held ninety-five thousand four hundred and seventy-two dollars. My watch tapped against the inside of my wrist. I did not look at it.

Jo had not written anything down yet. She was looking at the row of shoeboxes on the slab.

“Six-twelve,” she said. “Was that her payment?”

“Yes. I paid off the house in October 2012. She did not ask me to.”

I put the twenties back in November and closed the flap. My hands were not steady, so I squared the envelope to the edge of the shoebox until they were.

I had paid off the house so that my mother would not have to make the payment. She had made it anyway, on the first of every month for thirteen years, into a shoebox with my name on it. Ninety-five thousand dollars had sat in cash on a closet shelf the whole time and earned nothing. Janice could have had it in an account by noon on any one of those days. My mother had Janice's number.

I knew how many envelopes there were, and what was in each one, and the month she had put it by. I did not know what she had meant by any of it.

“She was stubborn,” I said.

Jo looked at me for a long time. “I'd have made it too,” she said. She looked down at the clipboard and wrote it out in her square capitals, the number of envelopes and the number of dollars, and turned the board around and held it out with her pencil.

She knew what the envelopes were. I could see it in her face. She had known since I opened November, maybe since she saw the years on the boxes. She did not tell me. I had watched her decide not to tell me something before, in June, with the recipe tin in both hands. It looked the same.

I signed the found sheet under her line. It was called, seen and signed for. It went green.

***

At 10:31 we were lying on the slab in the hall with our heads together and our feet going in opposite directions. We had lain like that on Saturday at three o'clock. The slab was still cool. Her hair was against my ear. It smelled like pads and the cab of her truck. I turned my head until her hair was against my mouth. My face was hot. The fourteen shoeboxes were in a row along the wall, with the green tape on each lid. Pancake was asleep in the empty closet.

Jo's phone buzzed on the floor between us. She picked it up and held it over her face.

It was Denise. There was a link, and then *JOANNA MARIE!!*

Jo looked at it for a long time. Then she handed me the phone.

It was the photograph from Sunday. The girl from Stephanie's front door had taken it at the cake. Jo had her shoulder against my arm and her head back, laughing. I was looking at her and not the camera. Behind us, out of focus, was the green and gold of the cake.

I had let the girl keep it. It was 10:32. I knew who to call to have it gone by midnight. My phone was in my pocket. I left it there.

It had been posted at 9:20 to a page that puts up pictures of people like me. It had been up an hour and twelve minutes. The page had captioned it in two words.

*Brennan's mover.*

I looked at Jo. Her eyes were on the two words. Her mouth had gone flat. Her father's name was on both doors of the truck in the driveway.

She took the phone back out of my hand and put it face down on the slab between us.
