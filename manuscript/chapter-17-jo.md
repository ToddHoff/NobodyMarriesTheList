## Chapter 17

**Jo**

On Tuesday afternoon I walked an empty house in Redwood City by myself. I made it about a minute at a time without thinking about Cal Brennan.

I do the last walk-through alone. The crew had taken the second truck back to the yard. The owners were in Sacramento with everything they owned. I open every door in a house before I give back the keys. Every closet, every cupboard, the oven, the dryer, the space under the stairs. Last year I found nine hundred dollars in a dryer in Millbrae. The woman cried on the phone for ten minutes.

I opened the oven and thought about his guest room. I opened the dryer and thought about it again.

So I did the dryer over. I got down on my knees with the flashlight and ran my hand around the back of the drum. There was nothing in it. The first time through I hadn't been looking.

An empty house is loud. You can hear the refrigerator from two rooms away. You can hear your own boots. It's the only time a job is quiet. I like it more than I tell anybody.

I did the garage last. Everybody forgets the garage. There was a coffee can of screws on the shelf over the water heater. I wrote it on the sheet, *Can, screws, garage, shelf,* and put it on the passenger seat to drive to Sacramento with Thursday's load. Nobody wants a can of screws. You still don't throw it out. It isn't yours.

My bag was on the passenger seat too. I'd packed it that morning before work: a T-shirt to sleep in, a clean shirt for Wednesday, my toothbrush. I had taken the toothbrush out twice and put it back twice.

The first time I took it out, I wasn't going. Jobs run long. I'd text him from the cab that Redwood City ran long. He'd write back *Yes*. Then I heard myself. When I asked Kevin what was wrong, he'd tell me what time he'd be home. I put the toothbrush back.

The second time, I was forty-four years old and arguing with a toothbrush in my own bathroom in the dark. I put it back and zipped the bag.

*Guest room,* I'd written him on Sunday.

*Yes,* he'd written back.

That was the whole conversation. I'd decided on the drive down from Summit Road that the first time would be on my ground, in my house, where nobody tries to carry my bag. I hadn't told him why. He hadn't asked.

I'd have asked. If a man wrote me *guest room*, I'd want to know what was wrong with mine. Janice would have sent me a paragraph about the sheets. He let me have it with no reason attached. I didn't know yet whether I liked that.

It had been seventeen days since the dog park. He'd written me every night since, himself. Every one of them had a number in it.

I knew it was seventeen because I'd counted them. I count hours on a job. The last time I counted days, Owen was due.

I checked every message for a semicolon. There never was one. Most nights I wrote back short, because what I wanted to write back was long.

***

His house is at the end of a gravel drive in Woodside, under oaks. I parked the sixteen-footer on his gravel and looked at the house the way I look at any house. Three trucks, if they had anything. There was nothing on the front step. No shoes, no hose, no dog toy. Somebody picked up after a man who didn't leave anything out.

His gate had been standing open at the bottom. Past it the drive climbed between split-rail fences. In the field across the road a horse stood in the shade. Somebody rakes this gravel. Under the oaks the air through my window was cooler than Redwood City's and smelled like dry grass and bay leaves.

The house was long and low, gray wood and a lot of glass, set into the hill so you couldn't see all of it from one spot. I've moved a dozen houses in Woodside. Long and low means every box goes the length of it.

I'd driven up his drive looking for the piano. A house that size always has one.

I sat in the cab with the engine off for a full minute first. I've sat in a lot of driveways. I'd never sat in one because I was scared to get out.

Every house I walk into, somebody's paying me to be there. I've got a clipboard and a reason. Nobody wonders what I'm doing in the kitchen. Nobody was paying me to walk into this one. If I got out, I was a woman with an overnight bag in a man's driveway. Whoever worked for him was going to see the bag.

The dog came across the gravel first. I stood by my door and let him come. He leaned his whole side on my shin. I let him do that now. I don't know when that started.

A woman about sixty came out behind him in a cardigan and put out her hand for my bag.

“Let me take that, Ms. Silveira.”

“I've got it.”

She kept her hand out a second. Then she looked at my truck and put the hand in her pocket.

“I'm Rita,” she said.

“Jo,” I said. “It's a T-shirt and a toothbrush, Rita. If you carry it, I'll have to tip you.”

Line seven was still on his card. *Comfortable being looked after.* At the dog park he'd said it out loud and couldn't tell me why, so it stayed on. I'd been on his gravel two minutes. I'd already said *I've got it* once.

He came down the steps behind her two at a time and stopped a foot short of me. He had on the gray shirt with the sleeves pushed up. My face went hot in front of his housekeeper. There wasn't a thing I could do about it.

“You found it,” he said.

“A minute ago,” I said. “I've been sitting in your driveway working up my nerve.”

I could have said the traffic on Woodside Road. He'd have taken it. I'd decided in the cab I wasn't walking into his house on a lie, even a small one about traffic. His ears went red.

Janice Louie was in his kitchen, at an island you could set a sofa on, with a stack of index cards and a rubber band. She looked up when I came in with my bag on my shoulder.

“Ms. Silveira.”

“Janice.”

The last time we'd been in a room together, she'd booked an estimate for her own office so she could get a look at me. We'd been polite to each other. It had cost us both.

Janice already had a card on me, somewhere in his office. She looked at my bag once. I figured it was going on the card. My face was still hot from the driveway.

“Your cards for the board meeting,” she said to Cal. “Eight o'clock tomorrow. Eleven cards.”

Rita came in behind us.

“Mr. Brennan asked for the halibut,” she said to me. “Or anything you'd like.”

I looked at the kitchen. There was nothing on the counters but Janice's cards. The stove had never had a pot boil over on it.

There were two things on his refrigerator. One was a school form of Abby's under a magnet. I don't read what people put on their refrigerators. The other was a white plastic badge on a clip that said FOOD SERVICE and DARLENE. It still had my green tape on it, folded over on itself, the way he'd torn it on May 16. It was the only thing in that kitchen anybody had put out on purpose.

I'd found that badge on the first Saturday, in a gray wool car coat, left pocket. I'd set it on the bench between us and watched him close his hand on it. Green goes home with the owner. Most of them have the tape in the trash by the first night. He'd had two months. My eyes stung. I wasn't going to do that in front of Janice Louie.

“Is there bread?” I said. “I've been up since five. I'd eat a sandwich standing up.”

Rita looked at Cal. Cal looked at me.

Janice put the rubber band around her cards.

“Calvin,” she said. “The regulars order a sandwich.”

It was the first thing Janice had ever said in my favor. She said it to him, not to me. She didn't look up from her cards.

In June he'd told me Janice liked me and nobody paid her to. This was the first I'd had it from her. I wanted to thank her. I kept my mouth shut, because then she'd have had to pretend she didn't know what for.

She picked up her purse and said good night to all three of us. On her way out she stopped at the thermostat in the hall and moved it up a degree, to sixty-eight.

Rita went home next. She took the halibut.

He made the sandwiches himself. He got the bread out of a drawer and the butter out of the refrigerator. He buttered both pieces out to the edge with the knife flat, every corner. I watched him do it from a stool on the other side of the island. I'd told him that in June, over a sandwich that had gone to soup by eleven. He's done it every Saturday since.

I watched his hands more than the bread. I'd have told Denise I was checking his work.

The Thursday before the dog park, on the Delgados' ramp, those hands wouldn't close. He'd sat down when I told him to and looked at them. I wanted them on me. I'd asked for his guest room myself, in writing. I sat on his stool and wanted them anyway.

We ate on his back step with the dog between us and the oaks going dark. He told me the board would ask him about margins for an hour and he would answer the same question eleven ways. I told him about the coffee can. He asked what kind of screws.

“Wood screws, mostly,” I said. “Some drywall. Two toggle bolts and a dime.”

“You wrote down the dime.”

“I write down everything. Owen says if he sits still long enough, I'll put him on the found sheet.”

He laughed out loud. The dog lifted his head to see what was wrong with him.

I'd already put him on a found sheet in June. His seven, with his name signed under them. I didn't bring that up on his back step.

“How many bedrooms?” I said.

“There are six.”

“Who sleeps in them?”

The dog put his head on my knee while I waited. I left it there.

“Abby sleeps in one. She is at her mother's this week. She starts at Cal Poly in September.” He looked out at the oaks. “The schedule is on the refrigerator.”

That was the form on his refrigerator, then. I'd been a foot from it and hadn't read it. I didn't go back in and read it now. He knew which magnet it was under. I'd have bet my truck he hadn't read it either. Owen's nine. I put that away before I could work out what year I'd be packing his room.

“And you.”

“And I sleep in one. The dog sleeps where he likes.”

“So he's got his pick of five.”

“He picks mine.”

I sat there with that and the dog's head on my knee until the oaks went black. My face burned the whole time. It was dark enough that he couldn't see it.

Then he walked me to the guest room. He'd shown it to me when I came in, so I'd know where my bag was going. He hadn't touched the bag.

At the door he stopped.

“May I kiss you good night?”

“You can stop asking that one. I'll tell you if it changes.”

“I would rather ask.”

So I let him ask. Then I let him.

He kissed me like a man with nowhere to be in the morning. He had the board at eight. My back was against his guest room door. I had a handful of his shirt and didn't remember taking it. My whole body had an opinion about the guest room.

He was the one who stopped. He stood with his forehead against mine, breathing like he'd brought something heavy up a flight of stairs. He'd asked me the one thing he walked me down the hall to ask. He left the rest to me.

I went in anyway. I shut the door on him in his own house and lay down on a bed with seven pillows on it.

His house was quieter than the empty one in Redwood City. I couldn't hear a refrigerator. I could hear one owl in the oaks.

I'd told myself the guest room was about my ground. A car had come to my curb in June that said *the usual.* I wasn't going to be the usual in this house.

I didn't sleep. Owen was at his father's till Sunday. My crew wasn't till seven. Nobody was going to need a thing from me before morning. I lay there and made the case for his door.

***

In the middle of the night I was in his hall in my T-shirt with my hand up to knock on his door.

There was light under it. I could hear the dog's tags on the floor. I could hear a page turn. He was awake too, reading something, a door away from me.

I had a sentence ready. I'd had it since midnight. It started with *I know what I said.*

The rest of it was short. I'd fixed it and fixed it until there was nothing in it a man could argue with. He wasn't going to argue. He'd open the door and say yes. Then I'd be a woman who asked for the guest room in writing and came down the hall at two in the morning to take it back.

Knocking is asking. I stood there with my hand up and couldn't do it.

If Denise had told me she did that, I'd have laughed at her for a week. I put my hand down. I went back to the guest room and got under all seven pillows. I called myself every name I call Vili when he backs into a pole.

Toward morning I heard the gravel. He was walking the dog.

I listened to the two of them go down the drive. I was still listening when they came back.

When I came into the kitchen before it was light, his hair was wet from the shower and curling at his collar. I had to look somewhere else. He had a frying pan out on the stove with the price sticker still on the bottom. There was a carton of eggs on the island. His phone was propped against it with a recipe on the screen. It was a recipe for scrambled eggs.

Somebody had bought that pan for this morning. He had the board at eight. He was looking up how to scramble an egg.

“I can do it,” I said.

I had my hand on the pan before I said it. I'm good at eggs. I've made them for Owen since he had teeth. I could have had us both fed in four minutes.

He didn't argue. He took his hand off the handle and waited to see what I would do.

In Millbrae I'd stood at the bottom of the Delgados' stairs and held my breath while he brought the high end of a dresser around the landing. My hands had wanted that dresser the whole way around.

He'd have let me have the pan. He'd have stood in his own kitchen with his hands at his sides and watched me make his eggs. On my truck that's a client.

I took my hand off the pan. I sat on the stool on the other side of the island and put my hands under my legs so they'd stay there.

“Go ahead,” I said. “I'll be over here, not helping.”

It was the hardest thing I did all week.

He read the recipe out loud, one step at a time. He cracked the first egg on the edge of the island and got shell in the bowl. He went after it with a spoon, then with his fingers, then with half the shell, which works. He looked up at me when it worked like he'd found the stud in a wall on the first try.

I've seen that look on new men, the first time a load goes in square. Nobody had ever aimed it at me over a bowl of eggs. I wanted to go around the island and kiss him with the shell still in his hand.

I didn't say a word. My hands stayed where they were.

He beat the eggs until they foamed. The recipe said medium-high. He picked the high part. The toast came out of his toaster black on one side and white on the other. The eggs were gray. They squeaked.

He took one bite of his and put the fork down.

“These are bad,” he said.

“They're hot,” I said. I ate all of mine. Then I looked at his plate.

“Are you going to eat those?”

He pushed it across the island. I ate his too.

He watched me eat every bite with his chin on his hand. He didn't look at his watch.

Nobody had made me breakfast since my mother. She'd have thrown these out and started over.

We did the dishes side by side at a sink as big as a bathtub. He ran the water. Then he stood there.

“Where's your dish soap?”

He opened the cupboard on the left, then the one on the right, then a drawer with nothing in it but a corkscrew. I opened the door under the sink. It was there behind a box of dishwasher pods, with the label turned out.

“It's always under the sink,” I said. “Even in Woodside.”

He looked at it for a while.

Rita had put it there with the label turned out, so whoever came looking would find it. He'd never washed a dish in that kitchen. He didn't have to tell me.

I washed. He rinsed. He was careful with the pan, like it had cost him something. We got through the plates without talking.

“On Summit Road you paid for everything,” he said. “May I ask why?”

I scrubbed the pan. The sticker wouldn't come off.

I had an answer for that. *I've got it.* It's true. Nobody argues with it. He'd said *May I* first, though, at his own sink with the water running. Then he'd waited for something else.

I didn't look at him. I couldn't have said it to his face.

“Kevin's folks gave us the down payment on our house,” I said. “In 2012.”

He turned off his water.

“Every Sunday his mother said grace,” I said, “and then she said how nice it was we had the room.” I set the pan in the rack. “When we split up I paid them back.”

“Did they ask you to?”

“With interest.”

I hadn't told anybody that last part but Denise.

“Promise me something,” I said.

“Yes.”

He said it before he knew what it was. On his mother's wet lawn in May he'd said yes before I was done. I'd told him I wasn't finished. He'd said *I know. Yes.* He hadn't missed one of those Saturdays since.

“You don't pay for anything of mine without asking me first. Not a sandwich. Nothing.”

He dried his hands on his shirt, because he didn't know where his dish towels were either.

“I will not pay for anything of yours without asking you first,” he said.

He said it word for word, the way he says everything back to me.

I found the towels in the drawer by the stove and handed him one.
