## Chapter 35

**Jo**

On Sunday afternoon, with Owen's backpack by the front door for his father's four o'clock, I opened the drawer by the stove.

Cal's letter was in the drawer, with the bank's check under it. They'd been on my counter since Wednesday night. Owen came home Thursday. Every morning before he was up I put them in the drawer. Every night after his door shut I put them back by the coffee maker and read the letter again.

I shut the drawer. In July he'd asked me what time and what he should bring. I'd sent back *6. Nothing.* He'd walked to the end of my street twice to use up the minutes.

I typed a time. Then I typed what I wanted him to bring.

*Six. Bring Pancake.*

I sent it before I could fix it.

He answered before I'd put the phone down.

*Yes. We will be there at six. He is slow on steps.*

My step's seven inches. I measured it the day we moved in.

***

Kevin pulled up on the dot. Before he was out of the car Owen was on my front step asking whether Pancake could walk yet.

“He's walking,” I said. “Slow on steps.”

“Is his head still crooked?”

“Some.”

“Can I see him?”

On Wednesday I'd said no to that in the same spot. Kevin was out of the car, looking up the street and down it with his keys in his hand.

“He's coming tonight,” I said. “I'll send you a video.”

Owen looked at me. Then he looked at his father, who was looking at the hedge across the street.

“Of him walking,” Owen said. “Not sitting. Anybody can sit.”

“Walking.”

He got his backpack up on one shoulder. At the car he stopped with his hand on the door.

“Tell him he's still first,” he said. “The head only makes him cuter.”

“I'll tell him.”

Kevin waited until Owen was in and belted. Then he lifted his chin at me over the roof of the car and got in.

***

I made caldo verde, because it takes an hour and I had three.

My father makes it every Sunday it rains. It's potatoes and onion in water till the potatoes give, then you mash them in the pot and put in the kale and the sausage. The kale is the work. You strip the stems and roll the leaves up tight. Then you cut across the roll as thin as you can go, so it comes apart into thread. My father cuts it with the same knife he uses for everything. His comes out in ribbons an eighth of an inch wide, every one. Mine never do. I had the roll on the board and nobody in the house. My hands weren't steady.

I cut it anyway. I got it as thin as I could and put the thick ones in with the rest.

On Saturday I'd given him his mother's keys back. Ten Saturdays with a clipboard in my hand. Nobody was paying me to be in a room with him anymore.

The linguiça was my father's, from the freezer. I sliced it in rounds and fried them in the pan till the fat came out. They went in the pot, fat and all. I turned the burner down and set two places at the table and moved Owen's charger off his chair onto the counter.

Then I changed my shirt. When I came back I took the glass out from under it and let it hang on the chain where it showed.

***

The fog came over the ridge. It does in August. The street went gray. The sprinkler across the street came on and ticked.

His car came down my street at six with its one headlight on in the fog. He parked at my curb. The fender I'd put in it in June was still in it.

He got out and opened the back door and leaned in. When he straightened up he had Pancake in his arms, with his chin on his shoulder.

He set him down on the sidewalk. Pancake stood. His head was over to the left, like a man listening to something on that side. He leaned a little that way to match it. Then he started up my walk.

It took a while. Cal walked at Pancake's pace with the leash slack and his other hand empty. He watched his feet. At the end of my walk Pancake stopped and looked at the step, seven inches. Cal bent and lifted his front end up onto it, then his back end. He set him on my mat.

Then he looked up at me. His eyes went to the glass on my chest and stayed there.

He hadn't seen it since Carmel.

“Denise did the wire,” I said. “Off a video.”

“You put it on the outside,” he said.

My face went hot from the collar up. I stepped back out of the door.

“Come in. He can have the kitchen.”

Pancake had his nose up before he was through my door. He went down the hall with it going, head over. He bumped the wall once. In the kitchen he stopped at the stove with his nose up at the pot.

I came in behind him. He turned around, slow, and leaned his head on my knee, the heavy side. He let me have the weight of it.

I got down on my kitchen floor. I had his head in both hands. He breathed out through his nose and his eyes closed. There was hair all over my hands again. I left it there.

“Owen says you're still first,” I told him. “The head only makes you cuter.”

I got up and filled my big mixing bowl at the tap and set it on the floor by the table. He drank with his head over. Half of it went on my floor.

“Can I give him a piece of the linguiça?” I said, with my back to the door.

“One,” Cal said. “He is still on chicken and rice.”

When I turned around he was in the kitchen doorway, under Owen's marks. He was in a gray shirt with the sleeves pushed up. He'd put the leash on the counter.

I fished a round out of the pot and blew on it till I could hold it and gave it to Pancake off my flat hand. He took it and looked at the pot.

I wanted Cal's hands in my hair. I stirred the soup.

“Jo,” he said.

I kept stirring.

“On the seventh of August I was supposed to ask you something. I did not. I would like to ask it now.”

The spoon stopped.

I knew what it was. The no was already in my mouth. Since I was seven I've said it before people got to the end of the question.

He hadn't got to the end of the question.

I turned the burner off. I turned around. I put both hands behind me on the edge of the counter and held on. I shut my mouth on the word.

“May I pay off your house?”

Pancake breathed on my floor. A plane went out over the hill.

“Yes,” I said.

I let go of the counter.

He didn't move. He stood in my doorway and looked at me. I watched his face. I can see all of it from the stove. His mouth stayed shut. Then his eyes filled.

He didn't say it was the horseradish.

“I am sorry,” he said.

I waited for the rest of it.

There wasn't a rest.

My eyes went too.

“Glasses are by the sink,” I said. “Get yourself some water.”

He came in. He went to the sink and opened the cupboard on the right.

He had it open before I could say left. He stopped with his hand on the door.

My tin was on the second shelf.

It's my mother's recipe tin, with a hinged lid, the size of the one in his mother's kitchen that I'd taped shut without asking him. I haven't opened it since she died.

He took it down.

I didn't move. If he opened it I'd have to say something. I had nothing.

He held it shut in both hands, the way I'd held his mother's. He didn't turn it over to read the bottom. He stood at my sink with it against his chest.

Then he put it back on the second shelf, square to the edge, and shut the cupboard. He opened the one on the left and took down a juice glass.

He didn't ask. He filled the glass at the tap and drank half of it and set it on the counter.

I turned the burner back on.

***

He ate two bowls. He had the first one down before I'd got the sausage out of the bottom of mine. He sat with the empty bowl in front of him. I took it and filled it.

“What is it called?”

“Caldo verde.”

“I have never had it.”

“My father's. He makes it when it rains.”

He ate the second one slower. Pancake was under the table on my feet, asleep.

“The bank sent me back forty-four eighty-three forty-five of yours,” I said.

“It is yours.”

“It's going in the white truck. I'm ordering the part in the morning. The shop gets the rest on the first.” I put my spoon down. “My dad's been in that cab since July.”

“Then he is off the truck,” he said.

“He's off the truck.”

He nodded once. Then he took the card out of his shirt pocket and laid it on my table with the pencil side up and squared it to the edge.

Nine lines in my capitals. Nine lines through them.

“I can tell you seven,” he said.

I put my spoon in the bowl.

“At the fence in June you asked me who wanted it. I said that I send things and that I like it when they are used.”

“I remember.”

“That was half of it.” He had his hands flat on the table on either side of the card. “I wanted someone who would use what I sent. Then I would not have to come. I could pick it out from the back of a car and never be in the room.”

He said it to my face.

I put my hands under the table.

For three months I'd sent back everything he sent.

“And when she doesn't?” I said.

“I did not have that answer in June.”

“Now?”

He looked at Pancake on my feet. Then at me.

“I am in your kitchen,” he said.

I got my pencil out of the drawer by the stove. I turned the card over to the back and wrote *COMFORTABLE BEING LOOKED AFTER* under GOOD FAMILY, in capitals, and drew the line through it. I pressed hard enough that it showed on the front.

That left two.

I handed it back across the table. He put it in his shirt pocket and put his hand flat over it.

***

After dinner I got Pancake up and walked him down the hall to the front door and back with my phone out, for Owen. He went slow and crooked and didn't fall. I sent it.

Owen wrote back right away. *Still #1.*

I showed Cal the screen. He read it twice.

By the time the dishes were done it was dark out. Pancake was asleep on my kitchen floor, snoring. Cal had dried the bowls and put the pan away where it doesn't go. I left it.

“He sleeps better on his own bed,” he said. “I will carry him out at nine. He will not wake up.”

I had the dish towel in my hands. I folded it in thirds and in half without looking at it and put it on the counter. My mouth already had its sentence. *Go on. I've got it.*

I made a different one.

“I need you to stay.”

He looked at me.

I'd never said that word.

“Both of you,” I said. “Tonight. And the next time something breaks.” My voice wasn't steady. I didn't fix it.

He didn't say yes. He took his watch off. He turned it face down and put it on the counter by the coffee maker, in the clear spot, where my statement used to be. Then he came across my kitchen and put his hand on the back of my neck, under the chain, where my hair was coming loose. He left it there.

“He goes out at five,” he said.

“Then we go out at five.”

***

In my room I didn't tell him what to do.

His hands weren't steady. He didn't hide it.

I pulled my own shirt off over my head. He looked at me under my own lamp. I let him, which I don't.

The glass was on its chain between us. He put one finger on it, on my breastbone, where his whole hand had been on Saturday. Then his hands went up my back and stayed.

I got him out of his jeans and got on top of him and took him in, with the lamp on and both my hands flat on his chest. He kept his hands on my hips. He watched my face. I put my whole weight down on him, all of it, on purpose. He took it.

I set the pace. He kept to it. The glass swung on its chain against his chest. He put his hand over it and over my hand and held both there. He said my name once, with his eyes open. Then his hands tightened on my hips and he quit waiting. I didn't want him to wait.

After, the lamp was still on. Pancake had come down the hall and was lying across my bedroom door with his head on his paws, looking at nothing.

I had my hand flat on Cal's chest. His heart was going under my palm. I'd told him on a rock to wait till it wasn't.

“I love you,” he said.

It was the third time.

I didn't tell him to wait for anything.

“I love you too,” I said. “Mine's doing the same thing.”

He put his hand over mine on his chest and held it there. We lay there with both of them going.

In the doorway Pancake let his breath out through his nose and put his head the rest of the way down on his paws.

***

Pancake woke me. His nails on the kitchen floor, down the hall. Then Cal's feet on my floor, trying to be quiet about it.

The window was black. The fog was on it.

“Five?” I said.

“It is four minutes to five.”

I sat up. He was standing by my bed in his jeans, with his shirt in his hand.

“I said we.”

“I will take him to the end of your street and back. I will be eleven minutes.”

“Put your shirt on.” I got up.

His fleece was on the back of the kitchen chair. I put it on over my T-shirt and zipped it to the chin and put my boots on without lacing them.

He looked at the fleece. He put his shirt on.

Outside it was cold and dripping. The streetlight at the corner had a ring around it. At my step Cal lifted Pancake down, front end and then back. Pancake went down my walk with his head over and his nails loud on the concrete. On the sidewalk he found the direction he wanted and leaned into it. We went that way, slow, the three of us, down the middle of my street, because there was nobody on it.

At the corner there's a signpost. Pancake stopped at it and put his nose to the bottom of it and stayed there.

Cal stopped. He stood with the leash slack and looked at the fog where the ridge is. He waited for his dog.

I stood next to him with my hands pulled up inside the sleeves of his fleece.

I held out my hand for the leash.

Cal looked at my hand. Then he put the loop of it in my palm and let go.

Pancake finished with the post. He turned around and went back up my street the way he'd come, slow, with me on the end of the leash. Cal walked up the middle of the street beside me with nothing in his hands.
