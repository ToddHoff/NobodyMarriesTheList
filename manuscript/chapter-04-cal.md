## Chapter 4

**Cal**

~~3. Dresses well~~

I reached my mother's house at 6:19 in the morning on the first Saturday, forty-one minutes early. I spent all forty-one of them in the car.

The house is 15.4 miles from mine. It takes twenty-two minutes. Until that Friday on the sidewalk I had not made the drive since the week she died. Before that, four times in five years.

It is an Eichler on West Remington Drive, a wide street with old trees down both sides. Hers is in the run of Eichlers beside the church on the corner at the Bernardo end, facing the middle school.

My grandfather bought it new in 1960 for $21,500. I own a watch that cost more. They sell for three million now. The buyers are paying for the glass: a back wall that is all window and an atrium in the middle of the house with no roof on it.

My mother worked the cafeteria line at that school for thirty years. Every day she handed me my tray, I thanked her exactly as I thanked the woman next to her.

She paid $612 a month on the house until 2012, when I paid it off. I had considered that the end of the subject.

At 6:21 my phone rang. It was Dawn Pruitt.

At midnight on the seventh I had left her eleven minutes on her machine, from the car, with the times in them. At 6:20 the next morning she had reached me on 280 to tell me that Jo had said no to dinner. I had said all right. She had not called since.

“You're in the car,” she said.

“I am in the car outside my mother's house. How do you know that?”

“You booked Joanna's Saturdays. Her crew starts at seven. You're early to everything. And if you were in the house you wouldn't have picked up on the first ring.”

I heard her pour her half cup. On 280 on the eighth she had poured three of them before she told me Jo had said no.

“You left me eleven minutes,” Dawn said. “Not one of them said whether you had a good time.”

“I stayed three hours and five minutes.”

“That's a duration.”

“It is the longest I have stayed.”

“Did you have a nice time?”

I have been asked that question about dinners for fourteen years. The answer has always been yes.

“Heart rate 104,” I said. “No workout detected.”

Dawn did not say anything. She practiced family law for fifteen years before she did this. She knows what a man sounds like when he has stopped one sentence early.

“I laughed twice,” I said. “I did not put that in the eleven minutes.”

“Why not?”

I looked at the house. From the car I could see the front door and the edge of the glass, but nothing behind it.

“My mother died in October.”

“I know, Cal.”

“I have not been inside her house since. With Jo I went three hours without thinking about my mother.”

“That isn't a confession, Cal. That's good news.”

She poured again.

Over the shrimp Jo had asked about the girl who named the dog. My eyes filled at the table. I had blamed the horseradish. Jo had passed me a napkin and waited until I used it.

“Tell me about her,” Dawn said.

“About Jo?”

“About your mother.”

Nobody had asked me that. At the funeral a great many people told me about my mother. They had worked with her, or had lived on the next street since 1961. I stood by the door and thanked them. None of them asked me anything.

“She worked at the school across the street,” I said.

I waited for the rest of it. I had lived eighteen years in that house. What came was the school.

“What was her name?”

I had it. It is on the deed and on the trust. It was on the program at the funeral, under a photograph Janice had chosen.

“Everyone at the school called her by her first name,” I said. “The students too. She did not like Mrs.”

Dawn waited. I heard what I had done a second after she did. I did not go back and fix it.

“All right,” she said.

She did not ask again.

“When my mother died,” Dawn said, “I cleared her garage myself. I gave the house to a mover and kept the garage. Every Saturday for a month. One Saturday, under a bag of potting soil, I found the dish she brought to every Thanksgiving. I sat down on the concrete and cried so hard I couldn't see it.”

“What did the mover do?” I said.

“She put the box down and went and sat in her truck for ten minutes. Then she came back and picked it up again.”

I did not have to ask who the mover was.

The street was empty. Two houses down, somebody's sprinkler was ticking against a car. Ten minutes in a truck.

“She said no to dinner,” I said.

“She did.”

“She said it fast.”

“Under a second,” Dawn said, and poured. “Write that one down with your other times.”

“I told her on the sidewalk that I would not ask her again.”

“Then don't.”

“That is what I said.”

“Good,” Dawn said. “Then she'll have to.”

I did not see how that followed. I filed it.

“Your file stays open,” she said. “Go in when she gets there.”

The line went quiet. She had hung up.

It was 6:44. Pancake put his chin on my shoulder from the back seat. I sat with the phone in my hand and looked at my mother's door.

I had walked Pancake at five, as I do. Nobody has ever asked for a meeting at five, so it is the one hour of my day that has stayed mine. After that I had run out of things that were mine to do, so I drove. He rode with his chin on my shoulder from the back seat. In eleven years it is the one habit of his I never tried to correct. At 6:50 I lifted him down from the back seat, sixty-four pounds, and put him in the back yard through the side gate. Jo had told me where she wanted a dog. A gate does the work of a railing.

I had a plan for the door. On the sidewalk she had told me that she did not go through a house without the owner. I had been over that sentence for eight days, looking for the edge case. It said nothing about who went in first. I would give her the key. She would open the door. I would come in behind her. Behind her was still in the house. I had run it on the drive down and again in the car. It held every time.

Her truck turned onto Remington at 6:59.

It was the truck from Friday. She was driving. Two men got down from the passenger side and started pulling floor runners off the back without being told.

She came around the front with a clipboard, the pencil already behind her ear.

She was wearing jeans gone white at the knees, a gray T-shirt with SILVEIRA across the shoulders, and the boots. Her hair was tied back and had not started to come loose yet. I had left my watch in the car so that nothing would report on me. It made no difference. I could feel my pulse in my neck.

She looked me over, top to bottom.

“You wore the shirt,” she said.

“It is a shirt.”

“It's the shirt. It's on the billboard.”

“I have forty.”

“Forty what?”

“I have forty of this shirt.”

She waited. When people wait, I explain.

“I tried thirty-one shirts in 2015,” I said. “I sent thirty back. When I found this one I bought forty, so I would not have to do it again.”

“You bought forty shirts so you'd never have to buy a shirt.”

“Yes.”

“That's six,” she said.

“What is six?”

“Never mind.” She pointed the clipboard at my feet. “Those are new.”

They were work boots. I had asked Janice what movers wear, Janice had asked a man at a store in Redwood City, and they had come on Thursday. They were the right boots. I had a blister on each heel from the walk to the car.

“They came on Thursday,” I said.

“They'll be all right by August.” She was still looking at the shirt. “Is that on the list? Clothes?”

“It is line three.”

“Which says what?”

“Dresses well.”

She looked down at herself. She took in the knees of the jeans and then the boots, where she stayed a while.

“I'm dressed right for this,” she said.

“You are the only one of us who is.”

I had not meant it to be funny. She laughed, one short breath through her nose, and held out her hand.

“Card.”

It was in my shirt pocket. It had ridden in a shirt pocket every day since the bar, moved each morning from one of the forty to the next, which I would rather she had not learned. I gave it to her.

She took the pencil down and wrote *DRESSES WELL* under the other two in box capitals. She drew a line through it and handed the card back.

That made three. She watched me put it in my pocket.

She kept more than one count. “Noted” had stood at four since the ramp on the eighth. Six was a second count. All I knew about it was that the shirts had moved it. I went back through the bar and the sidewalk for the first five. I found candidates and could not confirm any of them. The only count I could move was the one with my word in it. I decided not to say “noted” again that day.

“Key,” she said.

I had one. Janice had sent it over in an envelope with the address on the front in blue ink.

In an Eichler the front door does not let you in. You open it and you are outdoors again, in the atrium, under the sky, with the house standing around you behind glass. As a boy I thought every house had one.

The key turned. I got as far as the atrium.

There was a Japanese maple in a pot, its soil wet. Somebody else had a key. Through the glass I could see the paneling, the globe lamp over the table, and her chair. Nothing had been moved in seven months.

The bigger of the two men came in behind us with a roll of runner on his shoulder. He looked at the glass, and then at me.

“What's one of these go for?”

“They go for about three.”

“Hundred?”

“It is three million.”

He whistled, two notes. Then he stood there with the roll on his shoulder, waiting on the glass door.

Jo was waiting on it too. She had stopped a step short of it, without touching the handle.

“It is not locked,” I said.

“I know. I'll wait.”

She had found my edge case and turned it around. I have sat across tables from people prepared to wait me out. I know what it looks like when they mean it. She would have stood there until eleven.

I had told her eight days earlier that I could not go in. That was still true. But two people were waiting in my mother's atrium, neither of whom could start until I did.

I took hold of the pull. That door has stuck at the same place on its track since I was eight. You lift it a quarter inch and it goes. My hand lifted it before I had decided anything.

I was standing in my mother's living room.

“Runners,” Jo said, and the big man went past me with the roll.

I stayed where I was, one step inside the glass. I put down my hand, which was still out from the pull. My mother's chair was where I had seen it from the atrium. I did not go any closer to it. I waited for the step to cost me something. Jo was still on the other side of the glass.

She stopped on the threshold and smelled the house before she came in. I have since learned that she does that at every job.

“Slab floor,” she said. “No attic. No crawl space.”

“Is that good?”

“It means whatever she hid, she hid at standing height.”

“My mother did not hide things.”

“Everybody hides things.”

She walked the house with the clipboard, with me behind her. She counted out loud. Three bedrooms, two baths, eleven doors, nine of them sliding. She did not open a drawer.

The door to the garage was locked. My key was for the front door, the only one I had ever had.

“It is empty,” I said. I had not been in it since 2003.

She tried the knob once. Then she wrote it down.

In the hall she stopped at the photographs. There were nine frames. Six held Abby. One held me at a podium. Two held people I did not know, a couple laughing on a beach and a boy with a kite.

I looked at those two for some time before I understood that nobody knew them. They had come with the frames.

Jo looked at them as long as I did. She wrote something on the clipboard and did not tell me what.

A woman came in through the front door without knocking. She was pulling a folding luggage cart with three boxes strapped to it and a grocery bag on top.

She was about eighty, in a quilted vest. She looked at me twice. The first look was the one I get from people who have seen the billboard. The second I do not get from anybody.

“You're Calvin,” she said.

“I am Cal.”

“Darlene always called you Calvin. I'm Pat Dolan. I've been next door since 1974.” She let go of the cart. “These are yours. I didn't know who else to give them to.”

They were brown boxes, all one size, with the same label.

“The first one came the week before Christmas,” Pat said. “Then one in March, on her birthday. That top one came a week ago Friday. I took them in so they wouldn't sit out.”

“Thank you. They should have stopped in October.”

“Don't thank me. Your mother was the kindest woman on this street. She took my mail in for forty years.”

“I called the number on the label,” she said. “A very pleasant voice asked if I would like to leave you a message.” She considered me. “I said I didn't think I would.”

I knew the voice. I had picked it.

Jo crouched by the cart and tilted the top box to the light. She read its label and the slip in the plastic window. She read the second box and the third. She kept her eyes on the boxes.

I could read the top one from where I stood.

I had not written it. It was a standing order with a recipient, an address and three dates a year, running since 2014 without a failure. It did not have a field for whether she was alive.

“She carried them in herself,” Pat said. “She wouldn't let the driver. I've looked at that woman's recycling for forty years. Not one of those boxes ever went out.”

Pat handed me the bag, which was mail, and a key on a loop of red yarn. She said it was not for the front door. My mother had given her a key to that years ago, which was how the maple got watered. She said she would leave us to it. She took her empty cart back out the front door and closed it behind her without a sound.

I had needed her to tell me her name, though she had lived next door for all eighteen years I lived in that house. Pat still had my mother's name for me. My first thought was to have something sent to her. I could not think what. I did not know one thing about her.

Jo stood up.

“Where do you want them?”

“I do not know.”

She took the two rolls of tape off her belt. “Green or blue.”

“I do not know.”

“Then they sit.”

She carried the boxes to the hearth and set them down side by side with the labels to the brick. She put no tape on them.

I employ people who would have told me it was not my fault before the boxes touched the floor.

“Kitchen,” she said, and went ahead of me.

The kitchen in that house has no door. Nothing in an Eichler has a door if it could have a view. The hall carpet stops, the linoleum starts, and there is a strip of aluminum between them.

I stopped at the strip.

There was one cup upside down on the drain rack. Past it, on the wall, was the telephone. It is yellow, with a cord long enough to reach the stove. She never gave it up. On October 6, at 2:14 in the afternoon, she called me from it. I was in a product review. Remy took the call and had this for me at 2:15.

I had a minute on the ninth. She died on the eighth.

The message is still on my phone. I have read the summary perhaps two hundred times. I have never played it.

Jo was at the sink with her back to me, counting cabinets. She turned to ask me something and saw where my boots were.

She looked at the strip of aluminum. She looked at the telephone, for no reason I had given her. Then she looked at me without asking.

“Kitchens go last,” she said.

“Why is that?”

“You need the coffee maker till the end. Everybody does.” She came back across the linoleum and stepped over the strip to my side of it. “We start in the hall closet. I need forty boxes built while Vili's on the runners.”

She had given my boots a reason to be there. She was close enough to touch. I wanted to take hold of her arm above the elbow, where she had taken hold of mine on the ramp. She was on a job. I kept my hands at my sides.

She put a tape gun in my hand.

It had a handle, a roller and a row of teeth. I held it by the wrong end until she turned it around.

“Small box. Short flaps first, then the long ones. One strip down the seam and one across each end. It makes an H.”

“It makes an H.”

“Your mother's dishes go in these. The bottom falls out, that's on me.”

“Understood.”

“That's 'noted' in a different shirt.”

She did not say five. I had gone more than an hour without the word. She had caught the replacement the first time I used it.

She built one to show me. It took her six seconds. I counted.

I unbuttoned my cuffs and turned the sleeves back to the elbow. Of the forty of that shirt I own, not one had been turned above the wrist until that morning.

My first box took a minute and ten seconds and was not square. On the fourth I ran my thumb along the teeth.

“Let me see,” she said.

“It is nothing.”

“Let me see it.”

She took my hand and turned it toward the light from the atrium. Her palm was warm and rough across the middle, where the callus was. It was a shallow cut. She tore two inches off the green roll with her teeth, wrapped it once around my thumb, and pressed it flat.

“It is green,” I said.

“That one goes with you.”

She could have let go then. She kept my wrist and stopped looking at the thumb. She looked at my forearm, where the sleeve was turned back, for about as long as it takes her to build a box.

I let her look. Her fingers were on the inside of my wrist, over the pulse. If she had wanted the number, she could have counted it. I did not want her to let go. I held the arm exactly where it was.

Then she saw that I had seen.

“Thirty-six more,” she said.

She went to find Vili. The back of her neck was red all the way down to her collar.
