## Chapter 1

**Jo**

Dawn Pruitt told me three things about the man at the corner table. He was worth eleven billion dollars. He had a list of what he wanted in a wife. I wasn't on it.

She left out the fourth thing, which was that I'd been driving under his face for three years.

It's on a billboard on 101 North, a quarter mile before the Oyster Point exit. A man in a gray shirt, forty-eight feet wide, and three words: CALL YOUR MOTHER. My mother died in 2019. I'd been taking that billboard personally every weekday since it went up.

So I knew who Cal Brennan was. Everybody did. He made Remy, which books my sister's dentist and, since March, answers the phone at my office when nobody else can get to it. She set that up. I don't talk to it.

Dawn had called on a Tuesday while I was signing a permission slip.

“One drink,” she said. “Forty-five minutes. He came in with a list, twelve lines long, and I have three women who match every line.”

“Then you don't need a fourth.”

“You're not on it, Jo. That's why I'm calling.”

I was also twenty minutes late.

That was a recliner's fault. A widow in Daly City had kept one hand on a La-Z-Boy until I was already late, and then the hotel valet looked at my truck and said a hundred and ten dollars. Oversized vehicle. I told him I'd be forty-five minutes. He said a hundred and ten. I put it in a yellow zone on Minna Street, where the curb said loading until six, and changed my shirt in the cab.

I keep a clean one behind the seat in a dry-cleaning bag. People will let you into their dead mother's house in a clean shirt. The boots I could do nothing about.

At the hotel door I texted my sister, Denise.

*Going in. Palace Hotel, the bar with the painting. Cal Brennan.*

*THE BILLBOARD??*

*You want a photo of him?*

*I have a photo of him. I drive under it.*

*45 minutes,* I wrote. *If you don't hear from me by nine, send Vili.*

Vili is my crew lead. He is six foot four, and he can carry a refrigerator up a flight of stairs by himself.

I came in past a room off the lobby with a glass ceiling glowing amber high up on marble columns and crystal chandeliers hanging down the middle. I was twenty minutes late. I slowed down anyway. I've wrapped chandeliers for estates in Atherton. I'd want two men and a day for one of those.

The bar is named for the painting behind it, which I'd put at sixteen feet and which shows a piper walking a line of children out of town. Somebody decided that was the thing to look at over a drink.

He was at the corner table, reading something in his lap.

I'd have bet on a phone. It was an index card.

He stood when he saw me, and for a second that was all he did. I know the look. I got it more at twenty-five, but I still get it, mostly from men who haven't seen the boots yet. Then the card went into his back pocket and the gray shirt came up to eye level. I had assumed the shirt was a costume for the ads. It was just his shirt, and nothing off a rack fits anybody like that.

I'd been counting on the billboard to have exaggerated. It hadn't. It had left out the gray coming in over his ears and a front tooth that sat a little behind its neighbor. He had gotten to his feet for a woman in work boots.

My stomach dropped the way it does when the truck tops a hill I'd forgotten was there. I'm forty-four. I hadn't felt that since before I was married, and I did not appreciate feeling it in a hotel bar for a man with a list.

He put out his hand. Mine has a tape-gun callus across the palm and had, that day, a cardboard cut on the thumb. His was warm and dry. He held on a half second longer than a handshake takes, and he was looking at the cut.

“You are Joanna,” he said.

“Jo. I'm late.”

“You are twenty minutes late.”

“I had a widow and a recliner. Neither one would move.”

He looked at my boots, then at his watch, and I watched him do the subtraction.

“You have twenty-five minutes,” he said.

“I have what?”

“I book forty-five. You have used twenty.” He said it the way I tell a customer the truck is full. “It is not a penalty. It is arithmetic.”

That was the first reason not to like him. After that handshake I was collecting them.

The trouble was that I bill by the hour myself.

I had come in meaning to drink one beer and be in bed by ten. Twenty-five minutes should have suited me fine. I sat down mad about it. I wanted the whole forty-five.

The bartender was there before my chair stopped moving. I asked for whatever was on draft, and he said it would go on Mr. Brennan's tab.

“I've got it,” I said, and put a twenty on the table.

Cal Brennan looked at the twenty.

“It is a fourteen-dollar beer,” he said.

“Then I've got six dollars coming.”

“Noted.”

There was a shrimp cocktail in front of him. Six shrimp hung off a glass of ice around a cup of red sauce, and he hadn't touched one.

“Are you going to take that card out,” I said, “or keep sitting on it?”

His ears went red, but he didn't pretend. He took the card out of his pocket and laid it on the table between us, face up, and squared it to the edge with one finger.

It was a woman's handwriting in blue ink. She crossed her sevens.

```
JOANNA SILVEIRA (Jo?)
Silveira Moving, South S.F. Est. 1987.
Divorced. One son, 8.

ASK: her work. Her father.
DON'T: say "noted." Look at your watch.
       Mention the list.

8:45 car.
```

I write a card like that for every house I walk. Stairs, elevator, piano, who signs the check. I had never been on one. Somebody had looked up the year my father started and put it under my name, so this man would know what to ask me. When I walked in, he'd been reading about me.

“You looked at your watch,” I said.

“I did.”

“And you said 'noted.' That's one.”

“You are counting.”

“Somebody should. She's going to be disappointed in you.”

“She usually is.”

I unzipped my purse. It holds a twenty-five-foot tape, a roll of green tape, and a flat red carpenter's pencil I sharpen with a box knife. I took out the pencil.

“Is that a pencil?”

“It's flat so it won't roll off a truck.”

I crossed out the eight and wrote a nine.

“You cannot write on that,” he said.

“I just did. Owen turned nine in February. He'd want it fixed.”

“Noted.”

I looked at him. He looked at the card.

“That's two,” I said.

I crossed out the question mark after my name. I wrote *& Estate* after *Moving*.

I stuck the pencil behind my ear, which is where it lives on a job. I didn't know I'd done it until I saw him looking.

“What is the estate half?” he said.

“When somebody dies, the family calls me. I go through the house.”

“How do you go through it?”

“Every drawer. Every pocket of every coat.”

“Why do you go through the coats?”

“That's where the money is.”

He had stopped looking at the card. People get a look when I tell them what I do, and it's the look you give a nurse. He didn't have it. He studied me the way my father studies a staircase.

“How is your father?” he said.

“You don't know my father.”

“The card says to ask.”

“He's seventy-four and his knees are gone. He rides along on Tuesdays to tell my guys they're doing it wrong. He has a list too. It's one line.”

“What is the line?”

“Can he carry.”

“What does he have to carry?”

“He never says.”

My beer came. The six dollars came with it, and I left them where they were.

He had asked about my father because a card told him to. I'd told him more than I tell clients who ask on their own. While I drank, I looked at his hands on the table, because my father would have. They were clean to the wrist. I'd have bet they had never been under the heavy end of anything. I had my answer. I kept looking at them anyway.

The last line on the card said not to mention the list. I drew a line through DON'T.

“Dawn told me,” I said.

“She was not supposed to.”

“She told me I'm not on it. That's why I came.”

“That is a reason not to come.”

“It's the only reason I'm here. I don't date.”

“May I ask why?”

“Eleven people on payroll. A nine-year-old. A truck that's started making a noise on hills.” I drank some of my fourteen dollars. “A man who's already crossed me off, I can have a beer with.”

He took his time with that.

“What is the ask?” he said.

“The what?”

“You drove in from Daly City to buy your own beer. You want something.”

He didn't ask it rudely. He asked like a man who wants to know what a couch weighs before he picks up his end.

“One line,” I said. “Off the list. I've got ten minutes left, and I'd like to know one thing I'm not.”

He looked at the ceiling. Then he looked at me.

“Five foot eight or taller.”

I unzipped the purse again and set the tape measure on the table between us.

“What is that?”

“Twenty-five feet of Stanley. You don't take a number on faith, do you?”

“No.”

“Then come measure.”

I stood up. He came around the table.

I put the end of the tape under my boot heel and handed him the case. He ran it up my back without touching me. Then he laid his hand flat on the top of my head to mark the place.

Nobody had put a hand on my head since my father measured me against the garage door frame. This was not like that. He was close enough that I could smell him, and he smelled like a clean shirt and nothing else. I stopped hearing the bar.

“You are sixty-four inches,” he said, near my ear.

“In boots.”

“The boots are an inch and a half.”

“Inch and three-quarters. I had them resoled.”

“You are sixty-two and a quarter.” His hand hadn't moved. “You are short by five and three-quarter inches.”

“I've been short my whole life. I own six ladders.”

He laughed. It was one syllable, and I felt it more than I heard it. He looked at his hand on my head like he had just found it there. He took it back, and the tape ran home into the case with a snap that made both of us jump.

That was the second reason, and it went in the wrong column.

We sat down. My face was warm, and I blamed the walk from Minna Street. His ears had gone red again, and he had nothing to blame.

I'd changed my shirt in the cab thinking this would be the easiest beer I'd had all year. A man who had already ruled me out, with a car coming for him at a quarter to nine. It said so on his card. Now his hand had been on my head for as long as it takes to read a tape. I could still feel where it had been. I told myself a quarter to nine was good news.

I picked up his card and turned it over. On the back I wrote *5'8″ or taller* in carpenter's pencil. Then I drew a line through it.

He had stopped telling me what I couldn't write on.

“That one is not on the card,” he said.

“It is now.”

“The list is a document. That is a note card.”

“Then this is my copy.” I slid it across to him. “It's pencil. Rub it out if you want her that bad.”

He tried it with his thumb. The line stayed, and his thumb came away gray.

“Carpenter's pencil,” I said. “You'd have to sand it.”

He looked at the line for a while. Then he laid the card down with that side up and squared it to the edge.

“Are you going to eat those?” I said.

“They come with the table.”

“Things come with your table?”

“I meet everyone here. It is the same table, same order. That way the only thing that changes is the person.”

“Like a taste test.”

“It is a controlled one.”

“Do you even like shrimp?”

He looked at the shrimp. “Somebody decided I did. I have never had one.”

I'd had two lumpia standing up in a driveway at lunch and nothing since. I took a shrimp. The sauce was mostly horseradish. It went up the back of my nose and cleared out eleven hours of cardboard dust.

“Is it good?” he said.

“Have one. It's your table.”

He ate it braced, like my son with anything green. Then he took a second one.

I wondered how many women had sat in this chair across from six shrimp nobody ate. I hadn't been in it long enough to finish a beer. I'd already written on his card and fed him. Denise would want all of it by morning. I knew I wasn't going to give her any.

A woman from the bar was standing at his elbow with her phone held against her chest.

“I'm so sorry,” she said. “You're him. My dad's on Remy. She got him to dialysis three times a week for a year when I couldn't.”

Cal Brennan stood up, and the billboard stood up with him. His shoulders came down. His voice dropped and slowed down. He asked her father's name. He said to tell Walt that Walt had done the hard part. He leaned in for her photo like a man with all night, and she went back to her stool lit up.

Then he sat down, and he was the one with the card again.

“You're good at that,” I said.

“That is a room.”

“This is a room.”

“This is a table,” he said. “I am good at rooms.”

I get that voice in driveways, from men who want my crew to like them. They keep it on until the truck pulls out. His had worked on me from across the table. It hadn't even been pointed at me. I had never had a man sit back down and tell me it was a voice.

That was the third reason. I didn't know where to put it.

“Do you?” I said.

“Do I what?”

“Call your mother.”

He picked up a shrimp and put it down again.

“She died in October. The ad is from three years ago.”

“Did you call her before?”

“I sent things.”

I had been angry at forty-eight feet of this man since the day that billboard went up. I'd pictured saying so to his face.

“Mine died in 2019,” I said. “I couldn't do what your sign said. I've been taking it out on you every morning at Oyster Point.”

“Has it helped?”

“It was easier when you were forty-eight feet wide.”

That was four.

A minute before his car, the bartender set a leather folder at his elbow. Nobody had asked for it. This table ended at 8:45, and the man had worked enough of them to know.

Then the phone lit up beside the card.

```
REMY · 8:45 PM
Your car is out front.
Your 9:00 is confirmed.
```

It rang a second later. He let it ring.

“You have a nine o'clock,” I said.

“No.”

“Your phone says you do.”

“It says that so I can leave.”

“Does that get you out of all of them?”

“It has gotten me out of every one so far.”

That was what I had come in for. One beer and a man who would stand up at a quarter to nine and go. I got my purse into my lap, so I'd be the one standing first.

He looked at the phone, then at the folder. Then he looked at me, and it was the look from the tape measure. I had a shrimp tail in my fingers and my eyes were still running from the sauce.

He turned the phone face down.

I have stood in a lot of living rooms while somebody's son checked his phone over his mother's things. My stomach went over the hill again. I didn't blame Minna Street.

“I will have another,” he told the bartender.

“Another what, Mr. Brennan?”

It was an honest question. Cal Brennan had to look into his own glass to answer it.

“And for you?” the bartender asked me.

Owen was at his father's until Sunday. There was nobody at my house to know what time I got in.

I had a six a.m. load in Pacifica. I had four reasons by then, and I couldn't have told you which column any of them were in.

I texted Denise under the table. *Call off Vili.*

“One more,” I said, and put a second twenty down.

The bartender took the folder away with him.

The next time I looked at my watch his car had been waiting an hour, and nobody had brought the folder back.
