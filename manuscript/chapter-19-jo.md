## Chapter 19

**Jo**

On Sunday morning, in my crew shirt and my reading glasses and not much else, I was asked if I wanted to go see a volcano.

He'd been at my kitchen table about a minute. He'd squared my chipped mug to the edge of it.

Before that he'd stood at the end of the table with the mug, looking for somewhere to be. I'd moved him around my kitchen by the hips the night before. He still hadn't found it.

I'd left him asleep in my bed. I stood in the door a while wanting to wake him up. I was thinking about his hand on the back of my leg last night, on the scar.

“Owen loves volcanoes,” he said.

I smiled at him over my glasses.

“He does,” I said. “For his birthday he wanted a cake that erupted. I put baking soda in the middle. It went all over the counter. He made me do it twice.”

“In June he told me he was going to do volcanoes first, before the dogs. He said you cannot meet one around here.”

“Lassen's asleep. It doesn't count.” I'd been standing in that atrium when Owen ruled on it. “That's the law in this house.”

“Kīlauea is not asleep. It has gone off fifty-one times since December of 2024. The last time was Wednesday.”

I took my glasses off.

“Where's that?”

“It is in Hawaii, on the Big Island.”

“Cal.”

“I have not said anything to Owen,” he said. “I wanted to ask you first.”

I hadn't figured on volcanoes.

“Ask me what,” I said.

He told me in order, with times in it. A plane on Monday at nine. A house on Maui that he owned and had never been to. Tuesday on the Big Island with a volcanologist. Home Wednesday night.

“Between eruptions the ground at the top swells back up,” he said. “When it is full, it goes. Dr. Okamura says it is swelling now. She says it could go while we are there. She will not promise it.”

“Who's Dr. Okamura?”

“She is the volcanologist. She has agreed to give us Tuesday.”

“And she just said yes.”

“Yes.” He moved the mug a quarter inch. “And there is an airship.”

“Like a blimp.”

“A blimp has nothing inside it to hold its shape. This one has a frame. It is a zeppelin.”

I looked at him for a while.

“You picked the zeppelin.”

“Yes.”

“For Owen.”

“It is for you,” he said. “I had it put on a ship on the twenty-ninth of June. Owen had already told me about the volcano.”

I sat there in my crew shirt and waited for the piano.

He could have said it was for Owen. It was true enough. I'd have had nothing to say. Instead he'd told me the part I could hold against him.

“I've got two trucks in Burlingame at seven tomorrow,” I said.

“I know. I cannot do anything about Burlingame.” He looked at the counter, where my statement was under the envelope for the mail. “Owen asked me nine questions in my mother's back yard. Question six was whether I take days off.”

“What'd you tell him?”

“I told him I do not take many. He said you have not had a day off since he was born. He said you will take one when the house is paid.”

He'd just read the statement. He knew the month.

I took two days when my mother died, and I take her birthday. I don't count those.

“He's nine,” I said. “He tells the dentist I don't floss.”

“Do you?”

“That's not the point.”

He waited. He's gotten good at waiting since May.

I thought about Vili running both trucks in Burlingame, and Denise on the phone with the client when he ran late. I thought about my son at the window of a zeppelin. I thought about three days.

“Who else is going?”

“There will be two pilots and a woman who looks after the cabin on the plane. A man named Gil keeps the house. On Tuesday there will be Dr. Okamura and the crew that flies the airship.”

“Then I'm making the lunches.”

“Yes.”

“For the crew too. All of them.”

“I know.”

“And you ask Owen,” I said. “You asked me first. You can ask him.”

***

He came back that afternoon, after Kevin's car had gone, and asked my son in my front room. Owen stood with his hands at his sides and heard all of it before he said anything.

“Is it erupting?”

“It is between eruptions right now.”

“Then it's asleep.”

“Dr. Okamura says it is resting. She says it might go while we are there. She will not promise.”

“Is it the Pompeii kind?”

“No. This kind pours.”

“Nobody in Pompeii knew theirs was a volcano,” Owen said. “We did it in third grade.” He thought about it. “I'll decide when I see it.”

Cal said that was fair. Owen asked whether the zeppelin had a bathroom. It did. It had a window in it. Owen went to his room and shut the door. Through it I could hear a woman explaining lava. He'd found every video Dr. Helen Okamura ever made.

After bedtime I could hear him in there saying one word over and over. It was mostly vowels.

I texted Kevin. He wrote back, *Whatever works for you.*

I texted Vili. He wrote back, *Do I get Saturday?*

I wrote back, *No.*

Denise wrote *HAWAII??* and a minute later *JOANNA MARIE.*

***

The plane had eight seats and a woman whose whole job was the three of us. Owen asked her where she slept. Cal didn't open his laptop. I watched him not open it for five hours.

Coming in over Maui, the ocean under us changed color. It went from the dark blue we'd had since San Francisco to a green I'd only ever seen in the fish tank at a dentist's office, so clear that the reef showed through it in brown and gold patches, with a white line of surf drawn around the edge of everything. Owen and I had our foreheads on the same window.

Then a mountain filled it. It rose straight from the water, green at the bottom and rust red above the green, with a cloud sitting on its top, and it kept going up after I thought it had to stop.

“That is Haleakalā,” Cal said. “It is ten thousand and twenty-three feet.”

Owen studied it. “When did it go last?”

“It last went about five hundred years ago.”

“Asleep,” Owen said, and sat back.

Cal looked at me. I didn't help him.

***

The house was on the south side of the island, past the hotels, at the end of a road with a gate. Gil opened the gate himself. He was about sixty-five, in a pressed aloha shirt. He called Cal Mr. Brennan twice before we were out of the car. Cal shook his hand and asked him his last name.

I got out and looked for the front door. There wasn't one.

There was a roof, wide and low, with dark wood under it and black stone walls on both sides. Between the walls was a gap twenty feet across with the ocean in it. Somebody had built it so that would be the first thing you saw.

A wind came off the water through the gap, warm and salty, and lifted my hair off my neck on its way out to the gate. I've never been in a house that let the weather walk through it.

Owen touches a doorframe twice going into any house. There was no frame. He touched the stone twice.

The floor was koa, a gold wood with the grain running through it in waves. The boards were a foot wide and twenty feet long. Each board was most of a tree. The living room ran sixty feet. On the ocean side it had no wall. The doors were pocket doors, twelve feet high. Every one of them was slid back into the wall. The room went straight out onto a stone terrace. The terrace went out to a lawn. The lawn went down to a strip of white sand, and the sand went into water so clear I could see the rocks on the bottom from the house. Past the rocks the water turned blue, then a blue so dark it was nearly purple. Out in the purple a little island lay in a half circle, like a bangle somebody had taken off and left on a dresser. Behind it was a big brown island with nothing on it at all.

My bag slid off my shoulder onto the koa. I didn't check the floor for a scratch.

“You could back a truck in here,” I said, “and not touch a thing.”

Cal stood beside me with his hands behind his back. He looked at his own living room like a client on a walkthrough. It was the first time he'd seen it too.

Owen didn't look at the room at all. He looked at the water.

“Sunscreen,” I said. “Then go.”

He did his face and arms and was gone before I could check his back, with Cal's sleeve in his hand. Cal went with him in his good shoes.

Gil looked at me.

“You want to see it?” he said.

“All of it.”

It was my first day off in nine years. I walked his house like I was going to quote it.

***

I opened every door. I do that before I give back keys.

Five bedrooms, every bed made with the corners tucked. Nobody had ever sat on one to take off a shoe. A closet of beach towels folded in thirds. Good towels in every bathroom. Not one of them had ever been wet. I've cleared two hundred houses. Everybody has good towels nobody uses. His mother had six. He had a house of them.

“This one's his,” Gil said, at the last door.

The big bedroom had no wall on the ocean side either. The bed faced the water. The pillows had never had a head on them. The closet was bigger than my kitchen. The only thing hanging in it was a white robe with the tag still on the sleeve.

A library. Floor to ceiling, with a ladder on a rail. I took a book down and opened it an inch. The glue in the spine crackled. Nobody had ever opened one of them. I put it back.

Out on the lanai a bed hung from the beams on four ropes, facing the water, made up with a white cover.

“Nobody's been in it,” Gil said.

The pool had no far edge. The water ran to a stone lip and went over, and past the lip was the ocean, so from the house it looked like one water.

“Where's it go?” I said.

“Trough underneath. Gets pumped back around.”

I looked over the lip. There was the trough, and a grate, and the pump housing tucked in under the lawn where you'd never see it. Whoever built it had thought about where the water went. I'd have liked to shake his hand.

I've done houses in Atherton that yell at you about what they cost. This one didn't say a word. Somebody with taste had built it. Then nobody had come.

The refrigerator was full. Milk with a Thursday date. A papaya cut in half under plastic. Portuguese sausage, which they put on everything here. A loaf of Portuguese sweet bread in the drawer, the same kind my father buys.

“They stock it Thursday,” Gil said behind me.

“And?”

“The old goes to the food pantry. Six years.” He looked at the refrigerator the way I'd look at a truck somebody else had loaded. “We keep it ready. He's never been.”

“Six years of milk.”

“He bought it in February of 2020. He was coming in April.”

I didn't have to ask what happened in April.

“Nobody came anywhere that year,” Gil said.

“And you?”

“Six of us. He kept us on. Full pay.”

“What'd he say about it?”

“He didn't say anything. The checks came.”

He'd told me the same thing the night I met him, about his mother. *I sent things.*

Off the kitchen was a little office with Gil's desk in it, his schedules taped up in rows, and over the desk a picture of Cal printed off the computer. It was the billboard. I drive under it on 101.

“So everybody'd know him if he came,” Gil said.

He was smiling in it. It was the smile he does for a room. I'd seen the other one on Saturday night, in my bed, when I told him he was presenting.

On my way back through the house I checked the back of the bathroom door. I always do.

“What are you looking for?” Gil said.

“Habit.”

There was nothing written on it. Nobody had ever stood against it to be measured.

***

I went out on the lanai and put my hands on a rail somebody oils every week.

Down at the water Cal stood at the edge in his good shoes and let a wave come over them. He had his sleeves pushed up. Owen was in up to his waist, yelling at Cal that it was warm. Cal took a step in after him. Then another one. Then he stood there in his good pants with the ocean around his knees, laughing at something my son had said. I watched his forearms longer than I watched the ocean.

I took a picture and sent it to Denise.

*WHOSE HOUSE IS THAT??*

*His. He's never been here.*

The dots went for a long time.

*MARRY HIM.*

I put the phone in my back pocket.

I'd known since May that he had eleven billion dollars. I'd known it the way you know how far away the moon is. It's a number. You can say it. It doesn't come in the house. Standing on that lanai I found out what it was. It was a place kept ready every day for six years, so that the day you showed up, it was already yours.

It hadn't crossed my mind since May. I'd been too busy not owing him for things.

It came up on me the way the truck tops a hill I've forgotten is there.

So I let myself have one minute of it.

Owen here every summer, brown as a penny. My father in a chair on that lawn, with both his knees done by somebody good. Denise in that pool in her earrings. No payroll on Friday. Owen at whatever college he wanted, not the one my spreadsheet picked. November 2036 would be a thing I'd said once in my kitchen. It would take him one word. He wouldn't have to get out of the water.

And me, asleep at seven in the morning. Nobody in my family has slept until seven in a hundred years.

That was the minute. Then I went looking for the piano.

It wasn't hard to find. I couldn't pay him back for a house like that. All I could do was lean on him, me and my kid, with our whole weight.

***

I went down anyway. I'm not stupid.

Gil had left masks and fins on the sand, and a stack of vests. Cal put one on and buckled it. It was orange.

“Eleven billion dollars,” I said, “in an orange vest.”

“I am a buoy. That is the idea.”

The ocean at home is fifty-five degrees and the color of primer. This one was warm all the way down. I put my face in it. The whole reef was crackling, like bacon in a pan. Light came down through the water and lay on the sand in nets that moved. There were yellow fish, hundreds of them, turning all at once. There was a black one with a white stripe that came right up to my mask and looked in, like it had a question about the estimate.

Owen came up next to me, pulled his snorkel out and said a word. It was the one from Sunday night. It had all the vowels. He got through every one of them.

“That's the state fish,” he said. “I practiced.”

Past the reef the sand went down in a long slope, and then there wasn't any bottom. There was only blue, going darker the farther I looked, with the sun coming down into it in long bright ladders that swayed. I hung over the drop and breathed through the tube. For a while I forgot I had two trucks in Burlingame.

Owen grabbed my arm.

The turtle came up out of the blue like it was riding a liftgate, slow and level. Its shell was the size of a card table, brown and gold, with a few barnacles on one edge. It went to a rock and put its face against it and started eating. I could hear it scrape.

Owen backed off. He kicked backward, with his hands up.

“Ten feet,” he said into his snorkel. “It's the law.”

The turtle didn't know the law. It finished its rock and turned and came straight at him. Owen tucked his arms in and went still. He made himself into a rock. The turtle went under him so close he could have counted the plates on its back. He didn't touch it. Cal hung in his vest a few yards off with his face in the water and didn't move a finger.

The turtle came up for air a yard from my head. It put its face out and breathed once, a big wet puff I could feel on my mask. It looked at me with an old eye. Then it went down again, into the blue. It didn't hurry.

None of us said anything when we came up. Owen took his mask off and looked at the water where it had been.

“I didn't go to him,” he said. “He came to me. That's allowed.”

I came up the sand last, in the black suit from Target I've owned since Owen was in kindergarten, with my mask pushed up on my head and my hair down my back. Cal was sitting on the sand working the buckles on his vest. He stopped working them. He watched me all the way up the beach. I could feel exactly where he was looking.

I didn't reach for a towel.

***

Late that afternoon he drew a pufferfish nest in the wet sand.

He paced it first, heel to toe. Six and a half feet across. He drew the circle with a stick. Then he showed Cal how to make the ridges, from the edge in toward the middle, all the way around, like the spokes on a wheel.

“It's as long as your hand,” Owen said. “It works a whole week on one. It's for one girl fish.”

Cal made his ridges the same distance apart all the way around. I didn't ask if he was measuring. He was on his knees in the sand in his good pants. He'd given up on the pants around three.

They were two ridges from done when a wave came up farther than the others and took half of it.

Cal sat back on his heels and looked at what was left.

“Do we fix it?”

“No,” Owen said. “It makes a new one every time. That's the rule.”

“It makes a new one every time,” Cal said.

I went and stood in the water up to my knees for a while and looked at the little island instead of him. My stomach had gone over the hill again.

***

The sun went down far to the right of the little island, behind a long flat island way out. The sea between us and the sun turned gold, then the color of the inside of a peach, and the far island went black. Owen had read on the plane about a green flash, a green spark right as the sun goes under. He watched for it with his whole body.

It didn't come.

“It's resting,” he said, and looked at Cal. Cal laughed out loud.

We ate at the kitchen counter, sausage and eggs on sweet bread, because nobody wanted to sit at a table for twelve. Gil went home. By bedtime every good towel in that house had been wet. Most of them were on the floor of Owen's bathroom. I picked them up. I hung them. I didn't fold them.

Then we went out on the lawn in the dark, on beach towels, and looked up.

I'm from San Bruno. We have the planes. I'd heard about the Milky Way. I'd never seen it. There it was, across the whole sky, thick, like somebody had spilled sugar on a black counter and not wiped it up. There were so many stars I couldn't find the ones I knew.

“Which one's that?” Owen said, pointing.

“I do not know,” Cal said.

“Which one's that?”

“I do not know that one either.”

Owen thought about it. “You don't know any of them.”

“I have never looked.”

Owen fell asleep in the middle of telling us which ones he'd name. Cal picked him up off the towel. He bent his knees to do it and kept him close to his chest. I'd have hired him. He carried him up the lawn and into the house. I heard him trying doors until he found the right bedroom.

He came back out and lay down next to me on the towel.

“Six years of milk,” I said.

“I know. I have seen the invoices.”

I laughed. He waited until I was done.

“I was going to come in April of 2020,” he said. “After that I was going to come every April.” He was looking straight up. “Owen asked me if I take days off.”

“I heard.”

“He asked me on the thirteenth of June. On the fifteenth I called about the zeppelin.”

I did the dates. I can't help it. The fifteenth was a Monday. The next day I sat in his parking garage and read thirty-two messages out loud. He didn't know I was there. The same day I had him sign for his seven on a found sheet in my sixteen-footer.

He'd been working on a zeppelin that whole week. He could have led with it. I'd have had to answer a zeppelin. He sent me four words about a sandwich instead. I let them sit three days. He didn't send a second one.

I've got four good towels in my hall closet at home. They were a wedding present. They've never been wet either. I was saving them. I couldn't have told you for what.

I didn't say that. I rolled over and kissed him on a lawn somebody mows every Tuesday for nobody. He put his hand in my hair. It was a while before either of us looked at the sky again.

Owen was across the hall. I slept in my room and Cal slept in his.

***

Before it was light on Tuesday I made sandwiches in his kitchen. On the way in from the airport I'd had the car stop at a store in Kīhei. I paid with my own card. The food in his refrigerator was going to the pantry on Thursday. I wasn't taking it off them.

I made twelve, plus one for Gil. Two pilots, Dr. Okamura, four men on the ground lines, the three of us, and two more. There are always two more people than anybody tells you. Sausage and cheese on sweet bread, buttered out to the edge.

Gil came in and watched me wrap them.

“First time anybody's used it,” he said.

We flew to the Big Island before sunup, forty minutes over water into the sunrise. Behind us the top of Haleakalā stood up out of the clouds, pink. Ahead of us the Big Island ran up from the water like a loading ramp, two mountains so wide and so slow I couldn't tell where they started, with their tops above the clouds and the first sun lying across them. The airship was waiting in a field outside Hilo, tied by its nose to a mast on the front of a truck. It was white. I paced it off without meaning to. Nine of my big trucks nose to tail, with some left over.

In 2009 I watched one of these go over 101 every week from my cab. I'd looked it up so many times Denise asked if I was saving for it. I didn't say anything now. I stood there with a grocery bag of sandwiches until Owen pulled on my sleeve.

“That's her,” he said.

Dr. Okamura was small and gray and wore a sun hat with the strap under her chin. Owen walked up and shook her hand once, looking at her, the way Kevin taught him.

“How many have you met?” he said.

“Volcanoes? Forty-one.”

“In person?”

“In person.”

Owen nodded. She'd passed.

I handed out the sandwiches. The pilots looked at each other. The men on the ground lines said thank you, ma'am, and ate them right there with the lines in their other hands. Cal watched me do it. He didn't offer to help. He knows by now.

Getting it here had taken a phone call to the governor. Cal had told me on the plane that he'd promised to build something in Hilo. I didn't ask what. It wasn't mine to ask.

This was a zeppelin in a cow pasture at first light, and a scientist with forty-one volcanoes giving her Tuesday to a nine-year-old, and four men holding ropes so my son could go up.

The airship never sat down. It hung on its engines a foot off the grass with a little set of stairs under the door. Every time two of us went up the stairs, the men on the ground took two bags of lead shot off, to keep it even. It was the cleanest load-in I've ever watched.

Then the men let go of the lines. The ground went down.

That was all. Nobody pushed back in a seat. Nothing roared. I've been in freight elevators that made more fuss. I looked out. The truck was a toy. The men were looking up at us with their hands over their eyes. I'd missed the part where we left. Our shadow went across the pasture, nine trucks long, and over a fence and into the trees.

Inside it was one long room with windows down both sides and a big curved one around the back. One window on each side opened. Owen had his open before the pilot finished telling us we could, and put his whole head out. I held his belt. I'd have held it if he were thirty.

Then I put my own face out beside his. The air was warm and wet and smelled like rain on hot ground. Under us the forest went by close enough to pick out single trees, and here and there a top had gone red with flowers, like spatter off a roller. I had one hand on my son's belt and nothing else in the world to carry.

It was quiet. The engines were out on the sides and behind, not under us. You can't talk on an airplane. Here you could talk in your regular voice. With the window open you could hear a dog barking in Hilo, a thousand feet down.

“I can't rate him,” Owen said, with his head out the window. “I didn't meet him.”

We went out over Hilo with the bay on one side, flat and silver, and the town on the other, all tin roofs. Behind it the land went up and up into a mountain with buildings on its top, little round white ones, like salt shakers.

“Telescopes,” Dr. Okamura said. “Thirteen thousand feet.”

Then we turned down the coast. The green went on for miles, so green it looked wet. Then it stopped.

It stopped at a line. On one side were trees, and a road, and a mailbox. On the other side was black. The black went on for miles, all the way to the ocean, rough and wrinkled, like somebody had poured a whole county's worth of asphalt and walked away while it was still hot. There was the end of the road where the black had come over it. There was half a house.

“Twenty eighteen,” Dr. Okamura said. “There are seven hundred houses under there.”

I looked at the black for a long time.

“Nobody under there got to pack,” I said.

Dr. Okamura looked at me. “Some had a day. Some had an hour.”

Where the black met the ocean there was a beach, black too, with people on it. Past it the black stuck out into the water in a fat new point.

“There was a bay there,” she said. “It was full of fish. The lava filled it in. That's all new land.” She looked at Owen. “It's younger than you are.”

Owen pulled his head in from the window. He looked at the land for a long time.

“Then I'm older than Hawaii,” he said.

“Than that part.”

He thought about it. He put his head back out the window to have another look at it, now that he knew.

On the highway below us a pickup pulled off onto the shoulder. A man got out and stood in the road with his hand over his eyes, looking up at us.

That was me. Twenty-seven years old on 101.

I turned around to say so. Cal wasn't looking out the window. He was looking at me.

“You're missing it,” I said.

“I am not.”

My face went hot at a thousand feet.

***

Then we went up the mountain toward the top, over black ground and green ground and black again. The steam came first, out of cracks in the ground in long white rows. Then the ground fell away under us all at once. We were over the crater. Heat came in Owen's window like opening the back of the truck at three o'clock in August, and the whole airship rose a little on it, the long room all at once, like my father's boat lifting on a wake.

I measure everything by eye. I've done it since I was a kid in the cab with my father. I couldn't put a number on that hole. It went on for miles. The walls were cliffs, gray and tan, with layers in them. The floor was black and shining, like a parking lot after rain. Down in one end of it were two black hills, like the piles at the transfer station.

“North vent and south vent,” Dr. Okamura said. “Those are what fountain.”

The north one was red at the top.

It was the first red I'd seen. In daylight, in the sun, red. It sloshed up in the top of that hill, and a little ran over the lip and down the side, slow as pancake batter off a spoon. Steam came off it where it ran. The cabin smelled like a match right after you strike it.

“She's been spilling over since four this morning,” Dr. Okamura said.

Owen had both hands flat on the glass. “Is it going?”

“It's getting ready.”

“When?”

“Tonight, maybe. Tomorrow, maybe.” She said it to him straight. “She doesn't tell us.”

“Halemaʻumaʻu,” Owen said, to the window. He'd said it right. He'd practiced that one too.

Dr. Okamura looked at him like he'd handed her something.

About then the wind came across the top of the mountain, and the cabin swung with it, side to side, slow. Cal went gray. He sat down on the bench at the back with his eyes shut. It was the best seat on the airship, with the big curved window all around it.

“Are you okay?” I said.

“I am not. I would like to stop talking.”

He took a rag out of his back pocket. It was mine. He'd had it since the curb in Los Gatos.

I sat down next to him. I tried not to laugh. I laughed.

“Eleven billion dollars,” I said, “and you're sick in your own zeppelin.”

“It is not mine,” he said with his eyes shut. “It is leased.”

“You've been in two of these now. You haven't seen a thing out of either one.”

“I answered email in the first one.”

“I know. That's why I said it.”

I stayed on the bench with him. He'd had the zeppelin put on a ship and sent across an ocean so I could ride in it. He was going to spend it with his eyes shut. I put my shoulder against his, so there'd be one thing on board that held still. After a minute he leaned on it. Out the big window the crater was going by. I told him what was in it, front to back, like I was reading him an inventory, until it was behind us.

***

We landed late that morning. After lunch Dr. Okamura drove us up to the top in her own truck, which had a cracked windshield and a dashboard full of rocks. She took us out to a place on the rim with a rope across it and a ranger who knew her name. From there the crater lay out in front of us. The two hills were down at the far end of the floor, a couple of miles off. The north one was still spilling.

Every few minutes it did something I didn't have a word for. The red would rise up inside the top of it, and a roar would come up with it, louder and louder, like a truck with a hole in the muffler going up a grade. Then it would stop dead. The red would drop out of sight. Then it would come back up and spill a little more.

“Gas pistoning,” Dr. Okamura said. “She's clearing her throat.”

Owen sat on the ground at the rope with his knees up and watched every one.

Late in the afternoon it was still clearing its throat. Then Dr. Okamura looked at her phone for a long time.

“The tilt is still going up,” she said. “It may be tomorrow afternoon.”

“We fly home tomorrow,” Owen said.

“I know, honey.”

Owen didn't ask me. He asked Cal.

“Can we stay until it goes?”

“Ask your mother,” Cal said.

“We're staying until it's dark,” I said. “Then we'll see.”

It was cold at the rim. Nobody tells you a volcano is cold. It's four thousand feet up. The wind comes across the top. I'd looked it up and packed three sweatshirts, one of them for Cal. He put it on without a word. Owen sat on the ground in his, with the hood up and the sleeves past his hands, and watched the north vent cough.

Then he quit talking.

He goes quiet when he's told no. Kevin could go quiet from a Tuesday to a Sunday and pass you the salt the whole time. Owen sat with his knees up and looked at the crater and was gone.

I had the words ready. *Next time, bud.* They never work. I've said them a hundred times.

I waited for Cal to make a call. I'd have bet the truck he could get somebody on the phone about a volcano. He'd gotten a governor. He didn't take his phone out. He sat down on the cold ground next to my son, in his good pants. He sat there.

I looked at my watch eleven times. He didn't look at his once. He'd worn it face in since Saturday.

I'd figured he'd turn it back the minute he got to his car. He hadn't.

The sun went down behind the big mountain to the west. The cliffs went pink and then gray. Down the rim, people were coming out from the parking lot in jackets, a few at a time, and lining up along the rope. Half a moon was already up over the crater, very white. The red in the north vent got brighter as the sky got darker, and the steam over it lit up orange from underneath.

Then the north vent cleared its throat. It didn't stop.

The roar went up and kept going up. The red jumped up over the lip in big loose gobs, ten feet, thirty feet. Owen stood up. The gobs went fifty feet and came apart in the air and fell back burning. Then something came up under them from inside the hill, all at once. It went straight up.

I saw it before I heard it. My father taught me to count thunder in the cab when I was little. I counted without meaning to. One. Two. I got to nine. Then the sound got to us.

It was the 6:40 out of SFO going over my house at full throttle, except it didn't go anywhere. It stayed. It came up through the ground and into my feet and stayed there.

The fountain went up. I put it at three hundred feet. Then five hundred. Then I quit.

At the bottom it was white, too bright to look at. Above that it went gold, then orange. At the very top it went red and came apart. The trade wind caught the top and leaned it over, like the spray off a fire hose on a windy day. Big pieces came off the sides and fell, glowing, and turned dark on the way down. Where they landed they piled up and glowed.

Then the south vent went.

Owen yelled. It was a word, but I don't know which one. Two of them, side by side, two miles off and higher than anything I'd ever looked up at, roaring. The steam over them went up into the sky in a column, lit orange from underneath, miles up, past the moon, and turned the bottom of every cloud for twenty miles the color of a stove coil.

The light reached us. Every ledge on the cliffs across the crater lit up gold. I could see rocks a mile off that I couldn't have seen at noon. Every face along that rope was orange. Owen's was. Cal's was. Dr. Okamura, who has met forty-one of these, had her hand over her mouth.

“How high?” Owen said. He had to yell it.

“Over a thousand feet,” she said. “Fourteen hundred, maybe.”

I did it in my head. Almost six of our zeppelin, stood on its nose.

Down on the crater floor the lava had found the low ground. It went out from the bottom of the fountains in rivers, red and gold, splitting and joining up again, all of it going the same way. It looked like 101 from the top of my street at night, all the taillights going south. It went that slow. It didn't stop.

Down the rim from us a woman started to chant. She was older, with gray hair down her back. Her voice carried over the roar and held there. I didn't know the words. They weren't for us. Nobody along that rope said anything while she did it.

Something came down out of the air and landed on Owen's sleeve.

It was a single gold thread, finer than one of my hairs and as long as my finger. It shone in the light from the fountain.

“Don't touch it,” Dr. Okamura said. “It's glass. Pele's hair. The wind spins it out of the fountain.”

Owen held his arm out in front of him and didn't move it.

“Grandpa's was gray,” he said. “At Mount St. Helens. It was like flour.”

“This one's hers,” Dr. Okamura said. “People don't take it.”

Owen looked at it a while longer. Then he turned his arm over and let the wind have it.

I've never told him my rule about jobs. He had it anyway.

I had my hand flat on my own chest. I didn't know when I'd put it there.

Cal hadn't made a call. He'd sat on the ground in the cold.

I looked at him. He wasn't counting anything. His face was orange and it was wet. It was cold enough up there that it could have been the wind. I didn't ask.

Owen sat back down on the ground next to Cal. I sat down on his other side, on the cold ground. I reached around behind him and put my hand flat on Cal's back. I left it there.

“It counts,” Owen said.

“It counts,” Cal said.

It went for three hours. We stayed for all of it. Then it quit, both vents at once, like a truck when you turn the key. It was so quiet I could hear Owen breathing.

Then the people along the rope started to clap. They clapped for a long time. You clap for a volcano. I didn't know that.

Down on the floor the rivers were still running red. They would for hours. Over them the stars came back, all of them.

Owen looked out at it with his hood up.

“Fourteen hundred feet,” he said. “It's first.”

***

We flew back to Maui after midnight. Owen slept the whole way. Cal carried him in again and found the right door on the first try.

On Wednesday I woke up and the room was full of light. I looked at my watch. It was past seven.

I've never done that. Not when Owen was a baby. Not on Christmas. I lay there for a while and let it be past seven.

I took my coffee down to the beach. Gil was on the sand with his. Cal and Owen were out in the water up to their chests. Cal had been up since four. His body thought the dog was waiting.

“Put your ears in,” Owen was saying. “Your ears are the problem.”

“My ears are not the problem.”

“They're the problem.”

Cal put his ears in. He lay back and sank. He stood up and wiped his face and lay back again. On the third try he stayed up, with his arms out and his eyes shut and the light coming across him.

“Now don't do anything,” Owen said.

Out past them the water broke in a line of fins, a dozen, then twenty, going along easy. One of them shot clear of the sea, spun around three times in the air like a top, and fell back in on its side with a smack I heard from the sand. Owen screamed. Cal opened his eyes and sank.

Gil drank his coffee.

“He asked about my granddaughters on Monday,” he said. “Wanted their names. How old.”

Cal came in out of the water a while later with his hair flat to his head.

“Gil,” he said, “did Mahina's cast come off on Friday?”

“Friday,” Gil said. “She's back in the water already.”

He was in swim trunks. There was nowhere to put a card.

Before we left I walked it one more time. I always walk a house at the end. There was sand in the hall. There were wet towels on the rail by the pool, and a sandwich wrapper on the counter, and a dent in the hanging bed where somebody had slept on it, and the sand by the water was smooth where the circle had been. It looked like a house somebody had been to.

I went to strip the beds. Gil stopped me in the hall.

“Leave it,” he said. “I'll do them.”

***

On the plane home Owen had Remy write a song about Pancake. Remy did it in about the time it takes to say so. It rhymed *Pancake* with *make no mistake*. By the second time through, the woman who looked after the cabin was humming it. By the third time Cal knew the words. He didn't want to. I'll have it in my head until I die.

Somewhere over the ocean Owen fell asleep on Cal's arm with his mouth open.

“What's eight?” I said.

Cal took the card out of his shirt pocket. He didn't read it.

“No kids,” he said. “Wants them.”

I'm forty-four. I've got one. I'm done. He knew that.

“I've got one,” I said.

“I know.”

I didn't ask him who wanted that one. Not on an airplane.

I knew how that card worked by now. Every line he'd explained to me had somebody under it. If I asked about eight, he'd tell me. I'd have to hear it with my son asleep on his arm.

On the lanai I'd found the piano I'd been looking for since May. It was money, like I'd figured. I hadn't thought to look on the card for a second one.

He didn't move his arm.

Owen woke up over the coast with the fog under us. He took his phone out without a word and made a new list. He typed the title with one finger. *Volcanoes I Have Met.* There was one thing on it.

Then he opened his dogs and moved Pancake to the top.

He held the phone up to Cal and didn't ask him anything. He was showing him.

Cal read it for a long time.

“What happened to Truck Dog?” he said.

“I never met him,” Owen said. “It counts for everybody now.”
