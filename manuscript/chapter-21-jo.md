## Chapter 21

**Jo**

~~8. No kids. Wants them~~

On Thursday I packed a dead woman's kitchen in Westlake by myself, in fog so thick I'd driven over with my headlights on at seven in the morning. My nose was peeling from Maui. Every time I wiped it on my sleeve I was back on that lanai.

Her son had flown in from Phoenix. He sat in the garage with the door up, on a folding chair, with his phone, coming in every hour to ask if I needed anything. I've cleared a lot of houses for people's children. They'll go through their mother's closet and her jewelry and her desk with a box of tissues. They leave the kitchen to me.

I built two dish packs on her counter and taped the bottoms in an H. Plates on edge, cups rim down, glasses in their own cells. I can do a kitchen like hers in five hours if nobody talks to me. Nobody did. The house was so quiet I heard the furnace come on twice before noon, in July.

I was three days behind, with two trucks out and me on neither one. Vili had the white truck in Foster City. The other crew had a one-bedroom in San Carlos. Before I was out of bed that morning Denise had texted me the Friday payroll with nothing else on it, which is how she yells.

I had my phone in my back pocket all day. I took it out four times. In Denise's thread my *HOME* from Wednesday night sat right under her *MARRY HIM* from Monday. It didn't answer hers. She knew it didn't. Cal's was under that, with nothing in it since the terminal. I'd told him Thursday was a workday. He takes what I tell him to the letter.

The fourth time, I typed his name. Then I stood there with the phone in one hand and a stack of her salad plates in the other. I couldn't find a next word that didn't sound like a thank-you note. You don't send a man a thank-you note for a volcano. I put the phone back in my pocket and the plates in the box.

I wanted my hand on his back. I'd had it there three hours at the rim, through his sweatshirt. I could still feel his ribs going in and out under it. I stood in a dead woman's kitchen in the fog and missed that in my palm. I'd known the man since May. I'd slept with him once. I had no business missing a man's back.

The last cupboard was the one over the refrigerator, where people put what they don't use. I got the step stool. There was a roasting pan, a punch bowl with the cups stacked inside it, and a white CorningWare dish with blue flowers on the side.

I turned the dish over before I'd decided to.

There was a strip of masking tape on the bottom, gone the color of tea. Somebody had written on it in ballpoint, in capitals. NORMA ABAD.

Norma had brought something over in it when they found out she was sick. A lasagna, or the chicken with the potato chips on top. Nobody had taken it back.

I put it back on the shelf by itself, tape side down, and shut the cupboard. I didn't write it on the sheet.

Late in the afternoon the son came in and stood in his mother's kitchen and looked at the empty shelves for a long time. He didn't open the cupboard over the refrigerator. I didn't tell him about it.

I drove home in the fog with the heater on and my phone face down on the seat.

***

On Saturday I found nine more slivers of glass in the grout of his mother's hall with Cal holding the flashlight. He held it where I told him and didn't move it until I said. Most people can't do that. They get bored and point it at whatever they're curious about.

I hadn't looked at him since the milk.

He'd heard me tell my sister that nobody had ever sat down next to Owen. Me included. I'd never said that out loud before. He'd heard me say I wasn't going to marry a house, too. Denise hadn't sounded one bit surprised when she asked him how long he'd been standing there. I was going to kill my sister.

From the floor I'd heard Abby at the bathroom door. *Who's Owen.* Then, *You were here for his*, and Cal saying yes. I put my head down and went after the glass.

When I got up off the floor the back of his shirt was dark from the collar to his belt. The thermostat in the hall said ninety-four. It wasn't one o'clock yet.

He looked at the thermostat. Then he looked at me.

“I can have this house at seventy-two degrees by three o'clock,” he said. “May I do that?”

He'd asked.

He'd asked me on Sunday about a volcano. Now he was asking about air. Until him I don't think anybody in my life had asked me before they spent money on me.

I know what seventy-two by three o'clock looks like. I've seen it at estate sales in Atherton. Spot coolers on dollies, hoses run out the sliders, a man in a company shirt walking around with a meter. It roars like a truck idling in the next room. By three you'd have to shout to tell a man green or blue.

And I'd be standing in his cold air. The last two Saturdays I'd be working in it.

I wanted it so much my scalp itched. Sweat was running into my eyes. I'd put my hair up three times since ten.

“No,” I said.

He waited. Most people start talking.

“Your mother lived in it hot,” I said. “Every summer since 1960. We can do two more Saturdays.”

He looked down the hall at the glass.

“She opened every slider at nine at night,” he said. “She put a box fan in my window and took it out at six in the morning.”

“Then she knew what she was doing.”

“All right,” he said. He put his phone in his pocket.

I went and got the broom. My shirt was stuck to my back. I'd turned down seventy-two degrees from a man who'd asked me nicely. I'd be standing in ninety-four the rest of the day, knowing it.

***

After lunch Vili pulled the door down on the truck and came up the walk wiping his face with the front of his shirt.

“You coming?”

“I'm staying,” I said. “We're behind.”

Vili looked down the hall. The frames were done, the hall was swept twice, and Cal's room was half packed. We were ten minutes ahead, because Abby had taken the kite boy and saved me a box.

“Behind,” he said.

“Take the truck. Drop the load at the yard. I'll get the train.”

“The train.”

“Caltrain. It stops in San Bruno.”

“I've been to San Bruno, boss.”

He went back to the cab and got my cooler off the seat and carried it up the walk and set it on the step. He picked it up again, a couple of inches, and let it down.

“Heavy,” he said.

“Ice.”

He whistled his two notes. Then he looked at Cal in the doorway.

“Don't let her lift the desk.”

“I will not let her lift the desk,” Cal said.

“Desk stays till the fifteenth,” I said. “Go home.”

He went. Manny was asleep against the window before they got to the corner.

I'd packed that cooler at five that morning. My lunch, and then two turkey sandwiches, two plums, two bottles of water I'd frozen solid so they'd last. I hadn't told myself who the second of everything was for. I'd just made two.

Cal looked at the cooler.

“I will drive you home,” he said.

“I've got the train.”

“All right.”

I wasn't going to bill him for the afternoon. I'd decided that on the floor. I don't work for free for anybody but my father. I knew what that made it. I picked up the tape gun so I wouldn't have to think about it.

***

His room was the hottest one in the house. The sun came straight through the back glass onto the bed.

He'd said green for the ribbons and nothing for the bed. I'd left it. When a client can't call something, I leave it in the room until he can. The bed was made with the corners tucked under tight. I've stripped a lot of beds. You can tell one somebody slept in from one somebody made. This one had been made, over and over. I could smell the dryer sheet from the door. I'd seen beds like it on Monday, on Maui, made every week for a man who didn't come.

I sent Cal to take the ribbons down himself and wrap them while I did the desk.

You learn a lot about a person from a desk. I've packed a hundred boys' desks. There's gum stuck under the drawer, a note from a girl, a dead watch, something they'd die before they let their mother see. His had none of it. Mechanical pencils in a row, by lead size. Graph paper with the edges squared. A calculator with a label on the back in his mother's blue ballpoint, CALVIN B. A school calendar from 2003 with something written in every day through June. After June it was blank. I wrapped it and didn't read June.

The sweat ran down the insides of my arms. I could hear him in the hall, the paper and the tape, and then nothing for a while, and then the paper again. He was reading every ribbon before he wrapped it. I didn't go see.

Most days I like a room best when it's just me and the work. That afternoon it was me and the work and him on the other side of the wall. I kept losing my place in the drawer.

***

By the middle of the afternoon the hall said ninety-seven. I lay down on the floor.

I'd told his daughter everybody does. She'd lain on this slab when she was little. It was the coolest thing in the house, cool enough to come straight up through my shirt. I put my arms out. I hadn't been flat on a floor without a flashlight in my hand since I was seven, in an empty house in Millbrae.

Cal came out of his room with a ribbon in each hand and found me there. He stood over me for a while.

“The slab,” I said. “Everybody lies on it.”

He put the ribbons on the hall table. Then he lay down on the floor beside me, with his head by my head and his feet going the other way. Pancake came and lay down on his other side and sighed through his nose.

We lay there. A plane went over, high, going somewhere cold. I could hear Cal breathing and the dog breathing, and the house ticking in the heat.

“What were they for?” I said. “The ribbons.”

“Which one do you want to know about?”

“Pick one.”

“The red one from 1997 was second place,” he said. “A girl from the next street grew mold on eight kinds of bread. My design was better. Hers was disgusting. The judges were parents.”

I laughed. I couldn't help it. It came up out of me flat on my back and went down the hall. Pancake lifted his head to see what it was.

“You're still mad.”

“I am not mad. I have thought about it.”

“For twenty-nine years.”

“I think about it occasionally.”

“What was yours?”

“I put nine brands of battery in the same flashlight and timed each one until it died. The expensive one won by fourteen minutes. It cost twice as much. I wrote in my conclusion that it was not worth it.”

“How old were you?”

“I was eleven.”

I turned my head on the slab and looked at him. He was looking at the ceiling. His ears were red.

“You haven't changed a bit,” I said.

“My mother said that,” he said. “She meant it as a complaint.”

I didn't say anything to that. His mother's his business. I looked back up at the beams running out over the glass. His arm was an inch from mine on that cold floor. I could feel the heat coming off it. I didn't move mine. I lay there and wanted him, sweaty and red-eared on his mother's floor in the middle of a job. My whole body knew where his arm was. If I moved an inch I'd stop being on a job. I'd just told Vili we were behind.

“I do not know what to do about the bed,” he said after a while.

“Then it stays,” I said, “till you do.”

***

When the sun went off the back glass, air started to come through the house. I opened every slider, the way his mother did.

We ate on the bench in the atrium with the cooler between us. He took the second sandwich without asking whose it was. He ate it in four bites with his elbows on his knees, like a man on my crew.

“You brought two,” he said.

“I brought ice.”

He let that go. He's polite.

After that we worked. Once I told him how many boxes and what size, he built them on the hall floor and taped every bottom in an H without my saying so. I packed his closet. He brought me boxes before I ran out and set each one down where I'd reach for it. Nobody had to call anything. We didn't talk much.

By suppertime my back was gone from the floor and the hall and three days of making up. I sat down on the edge of his bed to write the count on my clipboard. Forty-one boxes, his room and the hall. I'd lie back for a minute. It had been made long enough. I'd unmake it a little.

I don't remember putting the clipboard down.

***

I woke up cold. That's how I knew I'd slept. The house had given up the heat all at once, the way glass does. The night was coming in every slider I'd opened. Somebody had pulled the bedspread up over me from the other side.

It was dark. The light from the hall came in at an angle. Cal was sitting on the floor by the desk with his back against the wall and his knees up. Pancake was asleep against his leg.

He wasn't on his phone. He wasn't doing anything.

“What time is it?” I said.

“I do not know.”

I'd never heard him say that about the time. He knows it to the minute. On a job he could tell you how long you were in the bathroom.

“Where's your watch?”

“It is on the desk.”

I could see it. It was face down.

I'd seen him sit like that once before, at the rim, next to Owen, for an hour and a half.

My phone was in my back pocket. I'd slept almost two hours. There was a text from Owen I'd slept through.

*Grandpa says it counts.*

Owen was sleeping over at my father's. My father had asked for him on Friday, for the videos. He doesn't say what he thinks until he's sure. He'd watched them seven times. Then he said it to my son, not to me. I lay there in a bed that wasn't mine with my son's text on my chest and my eyes stinging. I didn't wipe them. It was dark.

“There is still a train,” Cal said. “I looked.”

I didn't get up.

“You owe me one,” I said.

“Yes.”

“Eight.”

He didn't ask what I meant.

He'd heard me say something about him to my sister that I hadn't said to him. I was owed one of his. I'd found the piano on that lanai. I'd been afraid ever since that eight was a second one. If he wanted a baby, that was the end of it. I'm forty-four. I'm done having kids. You can't strike that one with a pencil.

“On the plane you did not ask me whose line eight was,” he said.

“I'm asking.”

“It was mine.”

“Why?”

He didn't answer right away. When Pancake moved his head on Cal's knee, Cal put his hand on it.

“Abby was four when her mother and I divorced,” he said. “After that I had her every other weekend.”

“Who picked her up?”

“Janice. On Fridays. Most Fridays I came home after she was asleep.”

I didn't say anything. I let him go at his own speed.

“I have a video of her at two years old at our kitchen table. She is counting to ten. She skips six. I watch it when I cannot sleep.”

“How many times?”

“I have watched it four hundred and twelve times.”

My stomach went over like a hand truck off a curb.

I've got a phone full of Owen at two. I hardly look at them. I was there.

“I wanted one I was there for,” he said. “I wanted to hear one skip six.”

“Then why not a woman with kids?”

“She would have been to the games,” he said. “She would ask me which of Abby's I went to.”

Kevin's been at every game Owen ever played. Same folding chair, same thermos. I've never said a word against him for it. I'd watched Cal now, too. At the rim. At a bathroom door with my carpenter's pencil. I'd already been comparing them. I hadn't known it was something a man could be afraid of.

“Do you still want a baby?” I said.

It came out smaller than I meant it to.

“I asked myself that on the plane,” he said. “I asked it again on Thursday at 4:58, when I turned my watch over. The answer was no both times.”

I believed him.

He didn't say Owen. He could have. It would have been easy. If he'd said he didn't need another one because he had mine, I'd have been off that bed looking for my boots and that train.

“Give me the card,” I said.

He took it out of his shirt pocket and got up on his knees and handed it to me. I turned on the lamp clamped to the desk. We both squinted. On the back, in my pencil, the lines went down in order, one through six, all struck. Seven wasn't on it. He still couldn't tell me why. I wrote eight under six. *No kids. Wants them.* Then I drew the line through it.

I handed it back. He looked at it for a while before he put it away.

I turned the lamp off.

He was still on his knees by the bed. In the dark he was mostly shoulders and the gray at his ears, where the hall light got it.

“What do you need?” he said.

Clients ask me that on the phone. *What do you need from us.* I've got it on a card. Parking for the truck. The elevator held. Somebody home who can sign. I could say that card in my sleep. I didn't have one for this.

He'd offered me the air today, and a ride home. I'd said no to both. Now he was on his knees by a twin bed with nothing in his hands, asking.

I knew the answer. I couldn't get it out of my mouth.

“Come up here,” I said.

He did.

It was a twin bed. There was no way to be in it except on top of each other. It took his weight with a sound like a screen door. I laughed. He laughed too, low, with his forehead on my collarbone. I'd been sweating since seven in the morning. I knew what I smelled like. He didn't care. He put his face in my neck and breathed in.

He took my boots off. He got down off the bed to do it, kneeling on his mother's floor to work the laces loose one eyelet at a time. I let him. Nobody's taken my boots off for me since I was small enough to be carried in from a car. He set them side by side under the desk with the laces tucked in, the way I keep them.

He didn't say anything at all for a long time. His watch was face down on the desk.

So I didn't run it either. I'd run it last time, all of it. I'd liked that fine. This time I let him take as long as he took. It was a long time. His hands were careful until they weren't. My shirt went somewhere. His went on the floor with the card in the pocket. The air through the slider was cool on my skin where his mouth had just been. Every time I noticed it, he put his mouth back. He got my jeans off. When he was inside me he went still and looked at me for a long time. I was the one who couldn't wait.

I kept count at first. I always do. What he'd given me, what I'd give back, how to even it. Somewhere in there I lost count. I didn't go back for it.

The bed hit the wall. The empty shelf over it rattled where the ribbons had been. His elbow found the wall twice. The second time he said my name into my hair, just my name. When it came for me it came up from my feet. I held on to his back with both hands, the way I'd wanted to since Thursday in the fog. I didn't let go.

After, we lay the only way that bed allowed, him on his back with his feet past the end and me mostly on top of him. Pancake came in and looked at us and lay down across the doorway. The night came in the slider. When a dog down the block barked twice and quit, Pancake lifted his head and put it down again.

“I have decided about the bed,” Cal said, at the ceiling.

“What about it.”

“It is green.”

“It's a twin.”

“I am not complaining.”

I laughed into his chest until it hurt. He let me. After a while he turned on his side toward the wall, the only other way that bed goes, and took me with him. His breathing went long. He was asleep.

I wasn't.

The truck notes, the mortgage, a nine-year-old, my father's knees. I know the number on every bit of that. I couldn't put a number on this.

I've emptied a lot of bedrooms where two people slept for fifty years. At the end one of them signs for the other one's things.

Eight was gone. The piano I'd found on the lanai was still there.

Two more Saturdays.

I put my hand flat on his back, where it had been at the rim. I left it there.
