## Chapter 27

**Jo**

At ten past four on Friday afternoon my father backed the sixteen-footer to the dock in one pass. Then he sat in the cab with the engine off.

I was on the dock with the clipboard. The four o'clock wind was coming over the hill off the ocean, hard enough to lift the corners of the pads on the stack, and I had my boot on them.

He got down sideways. He took the door in both hands and put his good leg down first. Then he brought the other one after it and stood on the asphalt holding the door until the knee would take him.

On Wednesday he'd done that in one move. On Thursday it took two. If a man on my crew got down out of a cab like that, I'd send him home and pay him for the day. I kept my boot on the pads. If I'd gone over there, he'd have let go of the door to wave me off.

He came across to the dock slow.

“Monday,” he said.

“Foster City. Two-bedroom.”

“One load?”

“On that truck it's two.”

He nodded. “Five-thirty.”

“I've got it, Dad.”

He looked at me. Then he looked at the white truck against the back fence with its hood up.

“I know you do,” he said.

He went to his chair by the office door, in the shade, and sat down. There wasn't any cushion on it.

I didn't have it. Vili had the big truck in Daly City on Monday, and somebody had to run the small one back and forth to Foster City all day while the crew packed. I could pack or I could drive. My father knew that before I did. He'd be in at five-thirty. He'd have the keys off the board before I got my coffee.

***

Fridays I do the trucks myself. Mileage, fuel, tires, and whatever's rolling around loose in the cab. I climbed up into the sixteen-footer with the clipboard.

His cushion was on the driver's seat. It was the one off his chair on the dock, the one I bought in May because of a message. He'd taken it off his chair and sat on it for three days of Burlingame and San Mateo and Pacifica, and it had gone flat in the middle.

His tide book was on the dash, open to the week. Down the margin, next to the tides, he'd written his loads in pencil. Wednesday, three. Thursday, two. Friday, two. He keeps the tides because he's a sailor. I don't know why he kept the loads. I've never seen him write down a load in my life.

The fuel receipt was under the visor, where we both keep them. A hundred and ninety-one miles since Wednesday morning. Front tires fine. The big bottle of ibuprofen from his kitchen was in the door pocket with the cap off, half gone. I put the cap on it. I left it in the door.

I wrote the mileage on the clipboard. I sat a minute in his seat, on his cushion, with my hands on the wheel at ten and two where his had been, and the wind rocked the truck on its springs. The cab smelled like his coffee and like the menthol rub he puts on his knees and says he doesn't. That was three days of my loads on his knees, so I wouldn't have to ask anybody for a driver. My throat was tight.

I took the cushion down, and the tide book. He saw me coming with them. He put his hands flat on the seat of the chair and lifted himself up off it so I could get the cushion under him. He sat back down and opened the tide book.

Denise came down the office steps with the payroll tote on her shoulder and her phone held out in front of her.

“Hillsborough stopped the CHECK.”

“Which Hillsborough?”

“How many Hillsboroughs do we HAVE? Wednesday. The big truck. Forty-eight sixty.”

She read it to me off the phone. Hillsborough had asked her bank to stop payment, *given what's being said about your company online*, until she'd had a chance to go through her things. Hillsborough had cancelled Saturday too. Saturday was her garage.

“Her things are fine,” I said. “Vili wrapped her mirrors himself.”

“I KNOW her things are fine. She means the picture.”

“Pay Vili and Manny for tomorrow.”

“She CANCELLED tomorrow.”

“They had it on the calendar. Pay them.”

Denise wrote it down. “Vili says THANK you, by the way. He's got his Saturday.” She looked over at our father in his chair. Then she looked at me. “CALL him.”

“No.”

“He'd have a truck in this yard by MONDAY.”

“Don't call him, Denise.”

“I didn't SAY I'd call him.”

“You were going to.”

“Who's driving Monday?” she said. I didn't answer. “Then who's paying for Dad's KNEES?”

I went and sat in my own truck.

On Thursday Cal had said he loved me on a rock with his heart going. Then he'd said it again across my hood with his heart going normal, because I'd asked him to. I'd kissed him with my headlamp on. I hadn't said it back.

Now I had a Saturday I hadn't asked for.

In 2018 I moved a retired dentist from Pacifica to a cottage in Carmel with a name on it instead of a number. *Dolores 4 NE of 12th*. It took Vili three passes to find it. On the way out we went down Ocean Avenue to turn around, and at the bottom there was a beach with dogs all over it and nobody holding a leash. Forty dogs, easy. I stayed in the cab.

I typed it before I could think about it.

*Tomorrow 6. I'll pick you up. Bring the dog.*

He answered before I'd put the phone down.

*Where are we going?*

*Down.*

*Understood. We will be at the gate at 5:55.*

***

On Saturday morning Cal was at his gate at 5:55 with the dog on the leash, a canvas bag at his feet and a fleece over his arm. He had his sleeves pushed up, and he looked like he'd been up since four. My stomach went over the hill. I sat in the cab with the engine running and let it.

He lifted Pancake into my back seat with both arms under him and set him down on my seat covers. Then he got in next to me and put the bag between his feet.

“Down is the coast,” he said.

“Says who?”

“You said to bring the dog. It will be ninety in Gilroy by two. You would not take him anywhere that hot. On the coast the fog stays until eleven. It is fifty-seven degrees in Monterey.” He held out the fleece. “This is for you.”

“I don't get cold.”

He put it on the seat between us.

We went over the hill on 84 and down through La Honda in the half dark under the redwoods, with my lights on. At San Gregorio the fog was sitting on the water and the road, and I turned south on 1. Nobody takes 1 on a job. On a job you take 101 and you're past Gilroy by eight. The dog put his chin on Cal's shoulder and kept it there.

He sat with his fleece on the seat between us and looked at the fog. Every time I opened my mouth he turned his whole head. All I talked about was the fog and how far it was to Santa Cruz.

I wanted to say it back. On the rock his heart had been going like he'd carried a dresser up three flights. I wanted it said with his heart going normal. At my hood I got that. I kissed him instead.

On Wednesday night I'd asked him for something, the first thing I'd asked anybody for since I asked Kevin to sign. Saying it back would be asking for all of him. Then Denise would call him about the white truck sitting in my yard with its hood up. He'd ask me nice. I'd say yes. There'd be a new one in its place by Monday with my father's name already on both doors.

I drove. Past Pescadero and Davenport, and through Santa Cruz before it was awake, and past the strawberry crews already bent over in the fields outside Watsonville at eight in the morning. The fog came and went on the road. Twice Cal reached back without looking and put his hand on the dog's head.

***

I parked in the lot at the bottom of Ocean Avenue, where everybody parks. On any job the first thing I find is the bathroom. This lot has one, and from the asphalt it's a straight shot down a slope of sand to the water. People stood at their tailgates with coffee. He had the fleece over his arm.

The sand on the slope was white even with no sun on it. The fog was down on the water. The cypress on the bluff were black against it. Out past where the waves broke, surfers in black wetsuits sat on their boards waiting, and I could hear them talking to each other in the fog.

There were dogs everywhere, jumping down out of back seats all over the lot and coming out of the fog in twos, and nobody holding a leash. I counted ten. I stood at the top of the slope with my truck keys in my fist.

Cal knelt and unclipped Pancake. “Go on,” he said.

The dog went down the slope at a trot and straight into the water. A wave came in, and he ran from it up the sand with the wind blowing his ears inside out. When it went back out he went after it, barking. A spotted dog half his age came and ran it with him, and then a little white one that barked at both of them. The three of them went in and out with the waves over and over, and every few waves Pancake came back to Cal and leaned on his shins, soaking wet, and went again. He never got farther than the length of my sixteen-footer.

“He has not been on a beach since Abby was twelve,” Cal said.

He sat down in the sand and took off his shoes and socks and rolled his jeans. He gave each leg two folds and made them match.

“Did you measure those?”

“Each one is three inches.”

I laughed out loud, and a woman with a poodle looked at us. His feet were as white as the sand. My face went hot over a man's feet.

We walked north along the wet sand with the dog running the waves beside us. The tide was low and coming in. I had my sneakers in my hand, and the water was cold enough to make my ankles ache. A loose V of pelicans came out of the fog, low over the water, and went back into it without one of them moving a wing. Past the surfers a seal had its head up, watching the dogs.

Cal stopped and bent down at the edge of the wet sand. He rinsed something in the next wave and held it out to me on his palm. It was a piece of glass about the size of my thumbnail, frosted on every side and blue all the way through.

“It is blue,” he said. “Blue is rare. About one piece in two hundred and fifty is blue.”

“What are you, a beach glass expert?”

“My mother bought me a rock tumbler when I was nine. You can tumble glass in one, so I read about glass too.”

On a job, blue means it goes on my truck. I don't take anything home from a job, not a pencil. I stood there with my sneakers in one hand and looked at it on his palm.

I took it and put it in the front pocket of my jeans.

A black dog about the size of a washer came out of the fog at a lope, straight at me.

My leg knew before I did. The back of my left calf went tight where the stitches were, and I stopped walking. I couldn't have told you what my hands were doing.

Cal took one step and was on my left, between me and it, with his hands loose at his sides. The black dog put its nose in his palm and went around him to Pancake. Somebody up the beach whistled, and it ran off into the fog.

He stayed on my left after it was gone.

I was shaking a little. I took the fleece off his arm and put it on. It came down past my knuckles and smelled like his neck.

***

At the north end the beach runs out under a bluff, and there was a driftwood log there as long as my truck. We sat on it. Pancake came back soaked and lay down in the sand against my left leg, on the scar, which is where he lies. I put my hand on his wet back.

“Kevin used to go quiet,” I said.

Cal waited.

“Not mad quiet. He came home. He ate dinner. He passed me the salt. He'd go from a Tuesday to a Sunday without a word to me, and then on Sunday he'd ask if I wanted the paper, like nothing happened.”

I watched the dog's ribs go up and down under my hand.

“I'd ask him what was wrong, and he'd tell me what time he'd be home Thursday. So I'd go looking for what I did. A job that ran long. Something I said to his mother. I never found it.”

“At his car,” Cal said, “Kevin said that you asked him for one thing.”

“You heard him.”

“Yes.”

“He was right,” I said. “Eleven years, one thing. When he went quiet, I painted the hall. I painted it twice in one year.”

I'd told Vili the rest of it once, in May, in the cab. I'd never told anybody about the hall. Cal let it sit there.

I didn't tell him when it started. The first time Kevin went quiet, Owen was five weeks old. I'd leaned on Kevin all that winter, with the baby on one arm and the phone on the other. On a Tuesday in March he stopped talking. I never leaned on him again.

He took the card out of his shirt pocket. The corners had gone soft.

“Line ten was mine,” he said. “*Never married.* Remy put it there because I was divorced at twenty-seven. I agreed with it in about a minute. I did not want a woman who had been married. She would know how a marriage ends. She would have known how mine did.”

“How did yours?”

“Stephanie told me in our kitchen on a Sunday night that she was lonely. I said I would fix it. On Monday I had Janice find the best counselor in Palo Alto and book us every Thursday at six. I went to two. Stephanie kept going without me. In March she filed.”

He said it to the water. His ears were red.

*I said I would fix it.* I thought about my yard, and the white truck against the back fence with its hood up.

My hands were cold, even in his fleece. I held one out.

“Card,” I said.

He gave it to me. One through six, struck, in my pencil, and eight under them. I had my pencil in my back pocket. I wrote ten under eight. *Never married.* Then I drew the line through it. The pencil went in deep where the card had gone soft.

I gave it back. He put it in his pocket and kept his hand over the pocket.

“What do you want?” he said.

I had my hand in my pocket, on the glass. I opened my mouth, and what was in it was the house, paid off by fifty-five. Since Friday night that was December. What I wanted was twenty minutes ago, him on my left without being asked, and no piano after. I shut my mouth on both of them.

The dog sighed against my leg.

While we sat there the fog came off the bay the way a tarp comes off a load, from one end. Under it the water was blue all the way across to Point Lobos, and the beach ran away south from us in one white curve, and the cypress on the bluff went from black to green. Right over our heads, at the top of the bluff, was the Pebble Beach golf course, green to the edge, with men in sweaters on it already. The surfers had sun on their backs.

Somebody up there yelled. A golf ball came down off the bluff, hit the log a foot from Cal's hand and dropped in the sand between his feet. Pancake didn't wake up.

A man in a sweater stood at the edge of the grass with his hands on his hips, looking down at us.

I held my hand out, and Cal gave me the ball. I threw it back up overhand, the way I throw a roll of tape to Vili on top of a load. It cleared the edge and landed on the grass a yard from him.

He looked at it. Then he lifted his hat to me.

Cal looked up at the edge of the bluff, and then at my arm.

“We'll walk Point Lobos on the way down and eat at Nepenthe after,” I said. “Neither one lets dogs in. Pancake's exhausted anyway.”

“All right.”

It was a long way back down the beach to the slope, on soft sand now that the tide had come in. Pancake walked in front of Cal the whole way, slow, with his head down. Halfway up the slope he stopped and shook on both of us. I came up behind them with my sneakers in my hand. Cal's shirt was stuck to his back between the shoulders where the dog had got him. I wanted both my hands there.

At the top Cal clipped the leash on. I dropped my tailgate. Friday night I'd put a towel back there, a gallon of water, a mixing bowl out of my kitchen and a bag of soft dog treats. He's old. I didn't know what his teeth were like.

I got down on the asphalt and started on his legs, like table legs before the pads go on. He wouldn't stand still for it. He wiggled the whole time, nose to tail. When I did his back he turned all the way around so I'd do it again. His tail got me in the face twice. I got the sand out from between his toes with him leaning on my shoulder. I'd never had my hands on a dog that long in my life.

I poured the bowl full. He drank all of it. Then I held a treat out on my flat hand. He took it without touching me with a tooth.

Cal had been standing there the whole time with the leash. He crouched down across from me.

“Thank you,” he said.

“He was wet.”

He held the dog's head in both hands while I wiped his face.

***

I took us out the long way, down Scenic Road. It runs one way, south along the water, and every house on it is a three-truck house with a name on it instead of a number. Half of them had nobody home, with every blind down to the same height.

Out on the point there was a house built of gray rocks off the beach, with a stone tower beside it, forty feet if it was an inch.

“One man built that tower,” Cal said. “It took him four years. He set every stone himself. Some of them weigh four hundred pounds. They came up from the beach below it.”

“Bet his knees were shot.”

Cal looked at me. I drove.

At the end of Scenic I cut over to Rio Road and went past the Mission. I crossed myself going by. Then we were on 1 going south.

Point Lobos won't let a dog past the gate, not even shut up in a car. I'd looked that up Friday night. I pulled into the turnout on 1 short of the gate, which saves the ten dollars too. Pancake was asleep on my seat covers before I had the engine off, with sand in his ears. Cal rolled the windows down a hand's width and filled the dog's water. The dog didn't open his eyes.

Cal took a trail guide out of the box at the gate and read it walking.

The trail out on the point runs between two wires on posts, and a sign says to stay inside them. Cal stayed inside them. Out there the cypress grow sideways off the rock, and every branch had orange on it, as bright as a safety vest.

“It is an alga,” Cal said, out of the guide. “The color is carotene. It is the same as in a carrot.”

“Are you going to read me the whole thing?”

“Yes.”

Where the trail came out of the trees at the edge of the point, I stopped walking.

The cove under us was the green of a beer bottle held up to the light, and so clear I could see the rocks on the bottom and the kelp standing up off them, leaning one way and then the other. Out past the cove the water went blue, and then a darker blue, all the way out to where the sky came down to it. A swell came in and hit the rocks under the point and went up white, higher than the trees, and I felt it in my feet before I heard it. In the kelp an otter lay on its back with its paws folded on its chest, asleep, going up and down.

“A painter called this the greatest meeting of land and water in the world,” Cal said, out of the guide.

I'd wrapped it. Every other house I do in Atherton has this over the couch in a gold frame, the cypress and the green cove and the water going white on the rocks. I've carried it down a hundred staircases in a mirror carton and figured somebody made it up.

Off the point the sea lions were piled on the rocks, barking. Every so often a big one shoved a little one into the water and took his spot. One on the top rock hadn't moved since we got there.

“That's Manny,” I said.

Cal looked at the rock a while. Then he laughed out loud on the trail.

Where the trail goes back into the trees, it's one person wide. Cal went first. There wasn't anybody on it but us. All the way up the slope at the beach I'd wanted my hands on his back. I put them there, both of them, flat.

He stopped. He didn't turn around. He reached back with one hand and held my wrist against him, and we stood there between the wires. I put my forehead between his shoulders. His shirt still smelled like the wet dog.

I waited for the piano. A family came around the bend behind us, and he let go of my wrist so they could get by.

South of Point Lobos the road climbs out onto the side of the mountains and stays there. On my side the hills went up gold, grazed short, with cows standing on them at angles I wouldn't park a truck at. On Cal's side there was nothing but air, and a long way down, the water. It was green over sand and dark over kelp, and every cove had a white edge where it broke. The fog had gone back out and sat on the ocean a few miles off, flat and white. Cal had his window down and his arm out in the wind.

I'd lived three hours north of this my whole life. I'd never driven it.

At Bixby Creek the road crosses the canyon on one gray arch, from one side to the other. I'd seen it sell me a car on TV a hundred times. Thirty people were stopped on the shoulder at the north end, taking the same picture. I pulled in with them.

“It is two hundred and sixty feet down to the creek,” Cal said.

I walked out to the edge. The arch went across in one piece, so thin from the side it didn't look like it could hold up the road. Under it the creek came out of the canyon and crossed a little gray beach into the ocean, and the water there was the color of a swimming pool, going white on the sand. South of the bridge the coast went on, one headland behind another, each one bluer than the one in front of it, until the last one was the same color as the sky.

Nobody takes 1 on a job. Every load I'd ever run south on 101, this was over the hill on my right the whole time.

Cal was on my left. I hadn't seen him get there.

Past the bridge the road climbs to a point where the wind came across so hard it rocked the truck on its springs, and Cal brought his arm in. The lighthouse at Point Sur went by on top of its own rock, white, out in the water. Down in the Big Sur valley the road went into the redwoods along the river, cold and dark after all that light, and came up out of them into the sun.

At Nepenthe I parked in the shade at the edge of the lot. Cal rolled all four windows down and put his hand flat on the back seat and then on the dash, and filled the dog's water, and Pancake lay down on my seat covers and looked at us.

The terrace hangs eight hundred feet over the water. I went to the rail before I went to the stand. The mountains come straight down into the ocean there, one ridge behind another, going south as far as I could see, each one a little bluer than the last, and the water at the bottom was so far down the waves broke white without making any sound. I've stood on a lot of decks in Hillsborough that thought they had a view.

The girl at the stand said forty minutes. We sat on the built-in benches by the fire pit with the sun on us. Twelve minutes in, he went down to the truck. He came back up.

“He is asleep. It is cooler in the truck than it is up here.”

A man across the fire pit had been looking at Cal since we sat down. He had his phone out. He stood up with it.

“Hey,” he said, loud, smiling. “Call your mother!”

Three tables looked over. Cal's face didn't do anything. His hand went flat on his knee and stayed there.

The last picture anybody took of us had cost me forty-eight sixty and a Saturday. I stood up and put myself between Cal and the phone.

“No pictures,” I said. “He's on his lunch.”

“Come on. One.”

“No.”

The man put his phone away. When I sat back down, Cal took his hand off his knee and put it flat on my back, between my shoulders, and left it there. My shoulders came down an inch.

We got a table on the edge at a quarter past two. I'd had coffee at five and nothing since, and we'd walked the beach and the point. Cal had been looking at the burgers at the next table since we sat down. I read the menu from the right-hand side.

On Thursday I'd bought him a burger with Wagyu in it. That was before Hillsborough.

“What can I get you?” the waiter said.

“The Ambrosiaburger,” I said. “Coleslaw. And a Red Seal.”

One beer. I was driving.

“And for you?”

Cal looked at me. “May I get lunch?”

“I've got it.”

On Monday at five-thirty my father would have the keys off the board.

He looked at me a second longer. “Then I will have the same.”

Two burgers and two beers came to eighty-one fifty before the tax. I could do eighty-one fifty.

The burger came on a French roll with their sauce on it. I ate it with both hands. The juice ran down to my wrist. I didn't stop for it. The beer was cold enough to hurt my teeth. The sun was on the side of his face and on the gray over his ear. He ate his in bites all the same size. A jay came down on the rail, as blue as a new pad, with a black crest, and looked at our plates. Cal set a piece of his roll out on the rail for it.

When the plates were empty he picked up the menu again.

“May I get the cake?”

Four-layer chocolate fudge, sixteen twenty-five. It was cake.

“Yes,” I said.

It came with two forks, four layers of dark cake with the frosting between them as thick as the cake. He cut it down the middle with the side of his fork and turned the plate so the half with more frosting was mine. I wanted my mouth on his neck in front of the whole terrace. I ate my half with my knee against his under the table.

He paid for the cake. I paid for the rest, five twenties, and didn't wait for the change.

“Thank you,” he said. He'd had his phone out under the edge of the table while I paid. He put it in his shirt pocket, behind the card.

“There is a hotel back up the road that takes dogs,” he said. “It is three hours home, and you have been driving since before six.”

I knew the place. I'd moved two couples who went there every anniversary. A night up there was most of Hillsborough.

“May I get a room?”

On Wednesday I'd asked him for one thing. A room up the road wasn't a truck with my father's name on the doors. It was one night. I could see the end of it from here. The dog had been asleep in my truck since Point Lobos.

“What do they charge for the dog?”

“It is one hundred and fifty dollars.”

“The dog's mine.”

“Understood.”

He took his phone back out. “I am having clothes sent down from Carmel. May I have some sent for you?”

“I've got a shirt behind my seat.”

“All right.”

***

The hotel is up a long drive off the highway, higher than Nepenthe. A boy in a vest held his hand out for my keys. I gave him the keys and told him the truck's seven foot ten with the rack.

At the desk Cal gave them his card for the room, and I gave them mine for the dog. The girl ran them both.

The room had wood walls, top to bottom, and a king bed I'd have put four men on. There was a dog bed by the fireplace with a bowl next to it. Pancake looked at the bed and lay down across the door.

There was a menu for the dog. Cal read it to him. “Chicken and rice, twelve dollars. Coco Patty, twelve dollars. Salmon bowl, fourteen dollars.”

“Chicken,” I said. “On my card.”

At six that evening the boy in the vest knocked with two shopping bags from a store in Carmel and a bag of the old-dog kind of dog food. Inside the shopping bags were a shirt, a pair of pants, socks, a sweater and a toothbrush, each one folded in tissue with a sticker holding it shut. Somebody had driven fifty minutes down 1 with a toothbrush. Cal set the dog food by the bowl for the morning. I went down to the truck and got my shirt out from behind the seat.

We ate our dinner on the terrace with the dog under the table. Dinner came with the room. I'd said yes to the room.

While we ate, the fog came back in off the ocean underneath us. It filled the canyons first and then everything below the hotel, until there was no road and no Nepenthe and no ocean, only white, flat, all the way out, with the tops of the ridges standing up out of it. The sun went down into it orange. The white went pink, and then gray, and then the stars came out over it, more of them than I knew there were.

Cal had his hand on the table. I put mine on top of it, in front of everybody. He turned his hand over and held on. He looked at me and waited. My face went hot. I passed him the bread.

In the room I lit the fire. The fire came in a paper bag. The bag said forty minutes.

Cal stood at the foot of the bed with his hands at his sides, waiting to be told where to be.

I told him. I stood in front of him and undid his buttons from the top, one at a time. His ears went red. My fingers went slow on the last two. Under my hand his heart was going hard.

“It's going again,” I said.

“Yes,” he said. “I am not going to say anything this time.”

I pushed him back onto that big bed and climbed on after him.

The fire burned its forty minutes. I looked at it once, over his shoulder, with his mouth on my collarbone and his hands spread on my back. It was most of the way gone. I shut my eyes.

In the dark I put my mouth on his neck, where the sun had been at lunch. Then I put my hand flat on his chest, where I'd put it on the rock. His heart was going normal, which is what I'd told him to wait for. I could have said it then. I had it in my mouth.

If I said it back, I'd be leaning on him with my whole weight.

I shut my mouth against his shoulder and kept my hand where it was until he was asleep.

***

In the morning the menu on the desk had no prices on it. Breakfast came with the room. I didn't know which side to read it from.

It came on a cart at eight, and the waiter set it out on our deck with the coffee in a pot and the orange juice with pulp on the side of the glass. We ate it in the hotel's robes. They were as thick as a moving pad. Below the deck the fog was still down in the canyons, white and flat, with the ridges standing up out of it in the sun. Past the last ridge the ocean had started to come out blue. Something in the oaks under us sang the same three notes over and over. Pancake had eaten out of the new bag at seven, and he lay on my bare feet.

I had eggs and bacon, and the lox on a bagel because it was there, and a warm roll out of a basket under a napkin. Cal had the same as me. Under the table he gave the dog one piece of his bacon and looked up to see if I'd seen.

Two gray squirrels chased each other around an oak trunk below the rail, up and around and down again. Pancake lifted his head off my feet to watch them. We watched them too, the whole time it took me to drink a second cup. Cal's robe had come open at the collar.

Twice he looked at me over his cup like he had something to say. Both times he drank his coffee.

When we were done I started stacking our plates. Cal put his hand over mine. I left them.

We left at eleven and came home up 1, the way we came. He fell asleep past Moss Landing with his head against the window. I'd never seen him sleep sitting up. I drove with the radio off. At San Gregorio I didn't turn up 84 for Woodside. I stayed on 1 and took him home with me for coffee. In my kitchen I took the sea glass out of my jeans pocket and set it on the sill over the sink. The sun came through it blue onto the faucet.

At half past two Cal was at my counter in the shirt from Carmel, with the fold lines still in it, and a mug of my coffee, standing where there's no room. Owen was due back at four.

My mortgage statement was on the counter by the coffee maker, where it lives till the next one comes. On Friday night, after the yard, I'd sat right there and written *skip Aug* in pen under the NOV 2036 I'd circled twice, for the check I'd mail in August. Skipping August moved the last payment to December 2036. I'd done the four months for the transmission in my head too, and stopped at February 2037, and gone to bed.

He read it upside down. I watched him find it. In July he'd done my month in his head before I could show him the spreadsheet.

He looked up at me. He didn't ask. He put both hands around the mug.

*I said I would fix it.* I could still hear him say it to the water.

I turned the statement face down.
