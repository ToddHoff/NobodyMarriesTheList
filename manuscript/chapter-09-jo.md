## Chapter 9

**Jo**

When we came out after the twelfth course there was a taco truck in the parking lot with its window up and every light on. It had come for one customer.

I had not said tacos. Neither had he.

It said TACOS ARREOLA down the side. The driver had backed it in across four spaces at the far end of the lot, which is how I would have done it, with a generator going. People were coming out of the club in good jackets and walking the long way around it.

Cal stopped on the walk and read the side of the truck.

“That is a taco truck,” he said.

“You didn't know?”

“I asked for food you could eat without a fork. It came back with an amount. I confirmed the amount.”

“You didn't read what you were buying.”

“No. I do not read most of what I buy.”

“That's how people end up with storage units,” I said, and went to the window.

I laughed the whole way over. He could have told me he'd picked it. I've had clients stand in their own kitchens and tell me they knew what was in a drawer they hadn't opened in thirty years. This one stood on the walk with his hands at his sides and let me laugh at him. I had to get my face straight at the window.

The man in it had an apron on and a towel over his shoulder. He looked at the dress.

“You the dinner?”

“I'm the dinner.”

“What do you want?”

“Four al pastor.” I looked at Cal, who had read the whole board by then, top to bottom. “Five.”

“What do I owe you?”

“Nothing. It's paid till eleven.”

I bill a truck and a driver by the hour. I knew about what two hours of that man's night had cost, a number I was never going to see.

I had it to within forty dollars. I wanted it on paper, in my hand, where I could pay it.

I took a twenty out of my purse and put it on the counter under the window.

“It is paid,” Cal said.

“The truck's paid. That's for him.” I turned back to the window. “There's a kid in there who brought the bread, and somebody on the pots. Feed them too.”

The man looked at Cal.

“Yes,” Cal said. “They do not know it is here.”

He went back up the walk and into the club. The man put the meat on.

Cal was back before he had turned it.

“There are nine of them,” he said. “They finish at ten.”

In twenty years I've had about five clients walk out to my truck to tell my guys something themselves. At dinner he had typed one line under the table and got a taco truck. He could have typed one more.

We stood at the window while he cooked. It was the first heat I had been near all night that smelled like food.

“How much does a dentist's chair weigh?” Cal said.

“What?”

“You said you moved a dentist's office today, with chairs. I have been trying to estimate one since the ninth course.”

“What did you get?”

“I got two hundred pounds.”

“Three hundred. They're bolted to the slab. The water and air lines come up through the floor. A plumber caps them before we can touch one.”

“How many were there?”

“Five.”

“That is fifteen hundred pounds of chair.”

“Before lunch.”

He stood on that for a while.

“And then you put on a dress,” he said.

“And then I put on a dress.”

“I did not say anything about it at your door,” he said. “Nothing I had was good enough.”

The man in the window asked me twice about onions and cilantro. On a job I can hear a dropped screw two rooms away.

I'd stood at my closet before I left and taken the dress off once and put it back on. At the door he had looked at it long enough that I raised my eyebrows at him. Then he didn't say a word about it through twelve courses. I had decided that meant he didn't think much of it.

Two paper plates came through the window. Cal took both. He carried them out in front of him, one in each hand, the way nobody should carry anything. I didn't say a word.

“Did Janice tell you to walk me to my truck?” I said.

“No.”

“She did the last time.”

“She did. Tonight her card is in my car. It has been there since 6:24.”

“There was a card.”

“There were four questions on it. I did not bring it in.”

“Why not?”

He took four steps on that one.

“I wanted to find out what I would say.”

“And the truck?”

“I wanted to walk you to it.”

He was looking straight ahead when he said it. His ears were red under the lot light. I caught my sandal on a curb stop I'd have seen in boots.

At the bar he had squared that card to the edge of the table every time I said something he had no answer for. At dinner he'd squared his knife. I took it for manners. The card had been in his car since before I sat down. When he told me what Dawn asked him, he hadn't had anything to read it off.

My truck was at the other end of the lot, nose out. We sat on the curb behind it with the plates on our knees. I have eaten a great many lunches on a curb. I had not done it in a dress.

I ate two before I looked up. He was holding his plate level with both hands, the one on it untouched.

“Eat that.”

“I have had twelve courses.”

“You've had twelve bites. Eat it.”

He picked it up and bit into it.

“You didn't brace,” I said.

“What would I brace for?”

“You braced for the shrimp. You braced for the linguiça.”

“This is the third thing you have told me to eat. I did not want either of the first two.”

“And?”

“You were right both times.”

I ate the other two. They were hot and they dripped, so I leaned out over my knees to keep them off the dress. He watched me eat them with his elbows on his knees.

On a job I eat away from the crew. Nobody watches me do it. I had grease running down to my wrist. I could feel him looking. I didn't sit up. I wanted him to keep looking.

“That's the something to eat,” I said. “You're down to two lines and half a linguiça.”

“That was the best thing I have eaten tonight.”

I got up and opened the cab. I keep a bag of clean rags behind the seat, next to the shirt. I gave him one and took one. We stood there wiping our hands like two guys at the end of a job.

That was when I saw it. With me up on the curb and him down on the asphalt, I was looking straight at his collarbone. Down on the asphalt with him, that's where the top of my head goes.

He saw it too. He looked at my sandals and then at the curb. I watched him add.

“You are sixty-eight and a half inches,” he said.

“Sixty-eight. That curb's five and a half. I've backed a truck up to a thousand curbs.”

He looked at the curb again before he took my number.

“That is five foot eight,” he said.

“Put it back on the list,” I said. “I'll bring the curb.”

He laughed, one syllable I felt more than I heard. He stayed where he was, down on the asphalt, with my rag in his hand.

“I told you I would not ask you to dinner again,” he said.

“You did.”

“I have kept to that.”

“I noticed.”

“This is a different question. I would like to ask it.”

“Then ask it.”

“May I kiss you?”

He didn't lean in. He stood there and waited for an answer. I have been asked a lot of things at the back of a truck. Whether it will fit. Whether I can come back Thursday. Nobody had ever asked me that one.

He'd take a no. He'd taken one on his mother's lawn. I didn't have one on me.

“Hold still,” I said.

He held still. He is good at it.

I put my hand flat on his chest, where I had put it in the hallway. In the hallway I had told myself I was making room. Up on my toes on a curb, I was still six inches short of his mouth.

He was holding still. I had told him to.

“Cal. Come down here.”

He came down.

He tasted like salt. For a second he kissed like a man following an instruction. Then his hand came up to the side of my face and he quit being careful. I felt it all the way down the backs of my legs. I have lifted with those legs for twenty years. They had not done that before.

His other hand still had my rag in it. I could feel it against my back. I hadn't kissed anybody since Kevin. Toward the end with Kevin it was something you did at the door with your keys already out. I kept my hand flat on his chest. I didn't trust it anywhere else.

His heart was going under my palm. His watch buzzed, close to my ear.

“Keep that one to yourself,” I said.

“It only taps when the number is high.”

“I said keep it.”

I came down off my toes. I didn't want to. That was reason eight not to like him, the last one I counted.

I had other things I could have said. I didn't want to stand on a curb and hear myself say them.

“Saturday,” I said. “Seven.”

“I will bring three lunches.”

“Three.”

I went around to the driver's side and got in. He was still at the curb in my mirror when I made the turn. He had my rag. I didn't go back for it. If I went back, I wasn't sure I'd leave again.

On the way out I passed the taco truck. Three people in aprons were at the window, one of them the kid who had brought the bread.

***

It is forty-five minutes home at that hour. I took 17 to 85 to 280 with the windows down, because the cab smelled like a taco truck and so did I. When the truck made its noise on the grades, I turned the radio up over it.

At the last light before the freeway I could still feel where his hand had been on the side of my face. I put my own hand there. The man in the next car saw me do it.

On Saturday morning I'd be in his mother's house at seven with a clipboard, saying green or blue, with Vili watching. I tried to work out how I'd keep my face shut for four hours. I didn't get anywhere.

I had gone to that dinner to find the piano. I went back through the night for it, one room at a time.

There was a taco truck he'd asked me about before he sent for it. He'd paid for it without reading what it was. I had laughed.

I went through it twice. No piano.

That should have settled it. I have cleared two hundred houses. Twice I've walked one and called it clean. Both times I was back the next morning for what I'd missed.

The reservoir went by on my left, flat and black. By my exit I had quit looking and still hadn't texted my sister.

Her car was in my driveway.

She was at my kitchen table with the payroll in front of her and my coffee maker on. It was an hour past my bedtime. She had moved my mortgage statement off the table to the counter where it lives.

“You didn't text,” Denise said. “I had Vili's number up. I had my thumb on it.”

“I was driving.”

“The checks need signing.”

“They need signing Friday.”

“Sit down and sign.”

I sat down and signed. She watched me do four of them.

“Your hair's down. Since when is your hair down?”

“It was dinner.”

“Is that GREASE?”

I looked. It was on the green, low on the left side, about the size of a dime.

“It's a taco.”

“They gave you tacos?”

“They gave me a pea in a spoon. He had a taco truck come to the parking lot.”

Denise put her pen down.

“Cal Brennan sent for a taco truck.”

“He asked me first. Then he wanted to know what a dentist's chair weighs.”

“Why?”

“I told him at dinner I'd moved a dentist's office. An hour later he was still on it.”

She looked at me for a while. She has been looking at me like that since she could stand.

“Did he kiss you?”

“No.”

“Your neck says he did.”

“I kissed him. He asked if he could. I told him to hold still.”

“You told him to HOLD STILL.”

“Then I had to tell him to come down. He's tall.”

Denise laughed out loud.

“Was it good?”

I signed the next check.

“That's the memo line,” she said.

It was.

“Joanna Marie,” she said. “An HOUR later he was still on the chair?”

“An hour.”

“Rob doesn't know what I do on Tuesdays.”

“He remembers everything. He writes it to me.”

“Vili says you read him one in the truck. I do your TAXES. Vili hears them first?”

“Vili talks.”

“Let me see.”

I don't hand my phone to people. I handed it to her and signed the next check.

I knew what she'd find. I had read every one of them at my counter, most of them twice, and the one about the footsteps until I had it by heart. I hadn't shown my sister any of them. I knew what her face would do.

She went up through them from the bottom. Twice she said “Oh.” Once she looked at me over the top of the phone. I looked at the payroll.

“Is this why Dad has a cushion on that chair?”

“I bought the cushion.”

“Because Cal Brennan told you to.”

“He said Dad should have one. I bought it.”

She kept going. Then her face stopped.

“‘Tell Denise nobody has ever announced me from a window,’” she read. “‘I liked it.’”

“You yelled ‘He called’ across my yard,” I said. “The Tuesday with the sandwich.”

“You never told me he wrote about it.”

“You'd have been unbearable.”

“How does he know my name?”

I had the pen on the next check.

“I've talked to that man twice on the phone,” she said. “I said ‘Silveira Moving,’ and I said ‘this is the sister.’ I didn't say Denise.” She turned the phone around to face me. “Did you?”

I went back through that too. The bar. Minna Street. A curb in Sunnyvale, a bench, my yard, a hallway, a table in Los Gatos, a curb an hour ago. I say “my sister.” I have always said “my sister.”

“I never told him your name,” I said.
