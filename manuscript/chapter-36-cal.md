## Chapter 36

**Cal**

At 10:34 on Tuesday morning, September 15, I walked to the front of the stage in the Civic Auditorium in San Francisco and let four thousand people wait for me.

The Civic holds eight and a half thousand. We had set it for four, with the floor in rows and the balcony closed past the third row, so it would look full on the stream. The stream had one million three hundred thousand people on it. In December 1968 Douglas Engelbart sat at a console on a stage in this building for ninety minutes and showed a thousand engineers a mouse. I read the transcript of it when I was nineteen. I had asked for this room every fall since we were big enough to fill it.

I had been on for thirty-four minutes. At minute nineteen Remy had sat on hold with an airline for eleven minutes while I talked about something else. When a person picked up, it moved a seat. Four thousand people cheered for a seat. My watch said sixty-one. It says sixty-one on a stage.

I can tell when a room is mine. It goes quiet from the back first. In the second row there was a woman from the Chronicle who had written about the photograph in July. She had come to see whether I was worse at this than I used to be. She was watching my board in the front row, not me. At minute twenty-six they were nodding. She put her pen down on her knee.

"We do not sell software," I said. "We sell an hour."

My voice went down and slowed. It does that on a stage. I let it.

"This morning, before most of you were up, forty million people handed Remy something they did not want to do." I gave them a second. "A refill. A form. The man from Ohio who keeps calling about the warranty on your car."

The laugh started at the back.

"Last month Remy waited on hold for you for eleven million hours." The number went up on the wall behind me, white on black, sixty feet wide. "That is twelve hundred and fifty-five years. We checked."

They laughed again. The woman from the Chronicle picked up her pen and wrote that down.

My phone moved against my left leg.

It was one minute and fifty-two seconds into the speech. The whole of it runs four minutes and ten seconds. Owen knows where every one of the seconds goes.

The phone was in my left front pocket. At 9:50 the stage manager had put her hand out for it at the foot of the steps. For seventeen years Janice had held it in the wing. Janice retired on August 28. I kept it.

On the third of September, a Thursday, Jo had put a card on her kitchen table between my plate and hers. It was green, from the school, EMERGENCY CONTACTS across the top and four lines under it. Her name and number were on the first line in her capitals. Kevin's were on the second. The third was empty. She had the pencil in her hand.

"Can I put you on Owen's card?" she said.

"What is the card?"

"Who they call. If he's sick and they can't get me or Kevin."

I looked at the empty line. She waited with the pencil over it.

My watch tapped against my wrist. I let it.

Janice had filled in Abby's card every August from kindergarten on. My name went on the second line, after Stephanie's, with Janice's number beside it.

"Yes," I said. "Put my own number on it. Not the office."

She wrote my name on the third line and my number beside it, from memory. On the fourth she wrote Denise.

That night I made a contact for the front office of Owen's school and set my phone so that four numbers could reach me at any hour: Jo's, Abby's, Owen's and that one. On Friday I drove to the school from my office and from my house and timed both. Remy would do nothing for any of them. I had set it that way in August.

It moved against my leg again. I finished the sentence about the years.

If I let it go, the school would call the fourth line. Denise was ten minutes from that school. Owen would be all right with Denise. If Janice had been in the wing, she would have answered it on the second ring. A car would have been at the school in twenty minutes with a nurse in the back seat. I would have come off at 10:38 and read her note about it in the car.

It moved a third time.

I took it out of my pocket.

Four thousand people watched me look at a phone. Somebody in the balcony laughed. They thought it was the demo again.

The screen said OWEN SCHOOL OFFICE.

"This is Cal Brennan."

It came back to me off the balcony a quarter of a second late. I had forgotten the microphone on my cheek. The room laughed harder.

"Mr. Brennan? This is Mrs. Fonoti in the front office. I'm calling about Owen Silveira." She had a radio going behind her and a child crying somewhere further back. "He's running a fever and he threw up after recess. He needs to go home. Mom's in Pebble Beach and we can't get Dad. You're next on his card."

"How high is it?"

"A hundred and one point six."

The laughing stopped. It stopped at the back first.

"Is he all right?"

"He's a sick kid. He's on the cot with an ice pack. He's okay."

"I am coming," I said. "I will be there at 11:06."

"Bring your license." She hung up.

I put the phone back in my pocket. On the wall behind me it was still twelve hundred and fifty-five years.

"I have to go," I said. "That was a school. I am on his emergency card."

Nobody moved. When a thing goes wrong on a stage, I have about four seconds before the room decides what it means. I used them to get to the wing.

Brandon was at the foot of the steps with his laptop against his chest. He had done the demo at minute nineteen. He had built the thing that waited on hold. I put the clicker in his hand.

"You have nine minutes," I said. "Do the hour. Start from the years."

He looked at the clicker. Then he looked at me.

"Do I tell them where you went?"

"They heard."

He was already looking past me at the second row. He had found the woman from the Chronicle before I had finished the word. He went up the steps with the clicker in his fist.

A woman from audio walked beside me down the corridor with her hand on my belt, unclipping the pack. She took the microphone off my ear without asking. I did not slow down for her. She kept up.

The health room was a closet with a cot in it and a poster about washing your hands. Owen was on the cot on his back with his backpack still on, both straps. He had an ice pack wrapped in a paper towel on his forehead. A pink plastic basin, curved like a kidney bean, was on the floor by his head. He was gray. His hair was wet at the edges.

He turned his head and saw me.

"You have makeup on," he said.

"I came straight from the stage."

I wiped my face with the back of my wrist. It came away tan. I wiped the other side.

He sat up slowly with the ice pack in his hand.

"I told her not to call you."

"I know. She told me. That is not how this works."

He looked at the basin. Then he looked at me.

"Did you finish?"

"Brandon is finishing it." I crouched down beside the cot, so that my face was lower than his. "How many times have you been sick?"

"Two." He thought about it. "Two and a half."

"When was the last one?"

"In here. Into that."

"Does anything hurt?"

"My head. And here." He put his hand flat on his stomach.

He answered each one as fast as I asked it. He had been waiting for someone to ask him.

Mrs. Fonoti came in behind me and picked up the basin.

"Take this for the car," she said. "Bring it back when he's better. He can't come back till he's fever-free twenty-four hours without medicine. Thursday at the earliest."

"Yes, ma'am. I will bring it back myself."

I held out my hand for his backpack. He looked at my hand. Then he took one strap off his shoulder and then the other. He gave it to me.

I put him in the back seat. He held the basin in his lap with a hand on either side of it. I put the car on self-driving and gave it Jo's address. Its record is better than mine. I turned around in my seat and watched him instead of the road.

At the light on San Bruno Avenue he went gray around the mouth and leaned over the basin. I reached back between the seats and held it under him with both hands, so he did not have to. He was sick into it all at once. I held it until he was done and he had sat back and breathed.

"Sorry," he said.

"You did not miss."

I set the basin on the floor behind my seat, upright. Then I opened the console. Jo's rag was in it, folded in fourths. I have had it since the curb in Los Gatos. I passed it back between the seats.

He looked at it before he took it.

"I never gave it back."

There is a gray lockbox on Jo's gate, the kind realtors hang on doorknobs. Owen got out of the car with the basin and the rag and went straight to it.

"Turn around," he said.

I turned around and looked at the street. A plane went out over the hill from the airport, climbing. Behind me four buttons clicked. The box let go.

When I turned back he had the key. He went up the walk and over her step. At the front door he touched the frame twice with two fingers, as he does at every door. Then he went in.

I took him down the hall to his room. It was his room, so I thought it was where he would want to be. He got into his bed in his clothes and turned to the wall. I let down his blinds. I put the basin on the floor by his head, where the school had put it.
