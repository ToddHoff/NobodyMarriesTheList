## Chapter 23

**Jo**

On Monday morning, before it was light, I drove under Cal's face with my headlights on.

The billboard is a quarter mile before the Oyster Point exit. They keep it lit all night. Forty-eight feet of gray shirt and CALL YOUR MOTHER, and nobody on 101 to read it but me and a bread truck.

It was my mother's birthday. She'd have been seventy.

I don't work on her birthday. It's the one day all year. I've never told the crew why. Vili worked it out in 2019 and put it in his phone so he'd know not to call me.

My phone was in the glove box. It stays there till the boat's in.

I took the exit and went out past the biotech buildings, dark except for their lobbies, to the end of the point. There were three cars in the marina lot. One had its front fender pushed in and no headlight on that side. I'd put that there on June 6. Cal had driven up from Woodside in the dark on one headlight.

At that hour the bay was black. The lights of the East Bay were strung along the far side of it from Oakland down past Hayward. To the south the runways at SFO were lit white and blue. The tide was going out. I could smell the mud. In the marina the masts stood up out of the dark, rocking together in the wind.

He was standing at the top of the gangway with his hands in his jacket pockets, looking down at the docks. He turned around when my door shut.

“You said a quarter to five,” I said.

“I said I would be here at a quarter to five. I am here.”

“Since when?”

“I have been here since 4:26.”

“Where's the dog?”

“He is at home. I walked him at 3:40. He was not in favor of it.”

I laughed out loud in the empty lot.

He'd shaved. At four in the morning, to go out on a boat, he'd shaved. I could smell it from where I stood. My stomach went over the hill.

I looked at his feet. Boat shoes, brown leather and white soles, without a mark on either one.

“You said shoes that grip,” he said.

“Where'd you get those?”

“I bought them for the board retreat in 2014.”

“The one where you sat where they put you.”

“Yes. They have been in a box since then.”

“Those are the cleanest shoes that have ever been in this marina.”

He looked down at them for a while.

My father's pickup came into the lot before five. He's early for everything. He parked next to Cal's car and sat for a minute with the engine off, looking at the fender. He'd have heard about the claim. I never told him I was looking at Cal in my mirror when I backed into it.

Then he got out, with his cap on and the cooler in one hand and the tide book in a bread bag in the other. He came across the lot slow, the way he walks in the mornings now.

Cal went to meet him. I stayed by my truck.

“Mr. Silveira,” Cal said.

“Frank.”

“Frank. I know two men who race sailboats. They could be on this dock before five-thirty. I would pay them for the day. May I do that?”

I'd known Cal would come up with something. In the car last night, when I told him about the boat, he'd had the look he gets right before he sends for a taco truck. I'd have bet money he'd ask me. He'd walked right past me and asked the man who owns the boat. I liked him for it more than I wanted to, at five in the morning, in front of my father.

My father looked at him for a while. Then he looked over at me, and back at Cal.

“No,” he said.

He held out the cooler. Cal took it.

My father's list has one line on it. Can he carry.

Cal held the cooler in both hands and waited to be told where it went.

The tide was nearly all the way out. Low at Oyster Point was a minus tide, right around sunup. The gangway went down to the docks as steep as a ramp off the back of a truck.

My father went down it backward with a hand on each rail. I went down below him, backward too, one step under his feet.

“I've got it,” he said.

I stayed one step under him to the bottom.

Cal came down last, facing front, with the cooler. He didn't offer my father a hand.

The boat is a Catalina 27, built in 1976, five years before I was. It's worth about two years of slip rent. My father's paid the slip since I was twelve. Most Sundays he comes down after Mass and sits in it at the dock with the tide book. He takes it out once a year.

Getting into the boat took my father a while. Cal and I looked at the water while he did it. He sat down at the tiller, reached into the locker beside him and held a life jacket out to Cal.

“Can you swim?”

“I can swim,” Cal said. “I cannot float. I tested it for an hour on Wednesday in Maui.”

I hadn't known that. On Wednesday I'd slept till after seven. He'd been out in the Pacific the whole time, trying to lie on top of it.

I laughed. My father didn't. He held the life jacket out until Cal put it on. Then he reached up and pulled the strap at the waist tighter himself.

“Jib,” he said.

The jib lives in a bag in the front cabin. It's bigger than the boat looks like it can carry, and it goes onto the wire at the bow with bronze clips, one at a time, from the bottom up. My father can't kneel up there anymore. That was the reason he gave. He could have put me on the bow and kept the tiller himself. He wanted a look at Cal.

Cal took the bag forward and knelt on the deck by the wire. I got the flashlight out of the locker and went up after him and held it.

“Bottom first,” my father said from the cockpit. “Tack.”

“Which end is the tack?” Cal said.

“Bottom.”

Cal found it and clipped it on. Then the next one, and the next, going up. He took his time with each one.

I held the light where he needed it. On Saturday he'd held one for me over nine slivers of glass and hadn't moved it until I said. I wanted to move this one up to his face. I kept it on his hands.

Halfway up I could see a twist in the sail. Everything in me wanted to say so. Clip, run your hand up the edge, then the next clip. It was my father's boat. I held the light.

My father came up the side as far as the mast, with a hand on the rail the whole way, and looked along the wire.

“Twisted,” he said.

Cal looked at the sail for a long time. Then he took seven clips off and did them over. When he was done he sat back on his heels and looked up the wire himself before my father could.

My father went back to the tiller and ran the blower four minutes before he started the engine. It's a gas engine as old as the boat. He hasn't skipped the four minutes in thirty-two years. Cal stood in the cockpit and watched him. I could see Cal's lips going. He was counting.

We backed out of the slip in the dark with the running lights on.

Outside the breakwater the wind came over the hills out of the west at fourteen knots. It was cold. My father put the bow into it. Cal went to the mast and hauled the main up, hand over hand, while my father told him when to stop.

Cal was still at the mast with both hands on the jib halyard when the first wave came over the bow. It went down his collar and came out of his sleeves. He didn't let go of the line.

The jib went up. My father turned us north, and the boat leaned over and took off.

Cal came back to the cockpit wet from his hair to the 2014 shoes. They squeaked.

“The water is cold,” he said.

“It's the bay,” I said.

“Locker,” my father said.

I dug out the yellow jacket that's lived in there since the nineties and gave it to him. It's my father's size, which on Cal meant it stopped short of his wrists. He put it on over the life jacket. I put him on the high side and he held on to the rail. Nobody sat in the corner by the hatch. Nobody does.

His teeth were going. He kept his mouth shut so we wouldn't hear them. Above the life jacket his wet collar was stuck to his neck. It was my mother's birthday, with my father four feet away. I looked anyway.

The sun came up somewhere behind the overcast. We never saw it. The gray over us got lighter. The water went from black to the gray of his mother's slab. Behind us the first planes were going up off SFO, one every couple of minutes. We went north past Sierra Point and Candlestick and Hunters Point.

I had the jib sheets. On this boat I'm crew. My father calls it, and I pull when he says.

He steered and gave Cal the names of things. Port. Starboard. Sheet. Halyard. Cal said every one back once, like a new man on my crew saying back an address. Every question he asked my father was about the boat.

We went under the Bay Bridge. I take loads to Oakland on its lower deck. The bottom of it is gray steel crossed every which way, a long way up, with rust in the seams. Then we were out the other side and the city was right there, lights on here and there in the towers, close enough that I could read the clock on the Ferry Building. It said four minutes after seven.

Cal had turned around on the high side to look at the city. The yellow sleeves had ridden up. His wrists were red with cold. I wanted to put my hands around them. I had the jib sheets.

Past the Ferry Building the wind went soft under the city. My father started the engine again and sent Cal forward to bring the jib down. Alcatraz went by on the right, a mile off. Gulls stood on every flat place on it, over rocks streaked white like the floor of an attic the pigeons have had for a year.

Past Alcatraz there was nothing in front of us but the bridge. It gets bigger the whole way in. You think you're close, and then it gets bigger again. Cal had quit saying the names of things back.

From the water it doesn't sit on anything. I move weight for a living. That's what gets me every year. Two towers stand up out of the bay seven hundred and forty-six feet, and the road hangs between them off two cables, four thousand two hundred feet of it. I looked that up the first year, when I was twelve. Under the overcast the orange was the only color anywhere. The water was gray, the sky was gray, the Marin hills were gray-brown, and the bridge looked lit from inside, like running lights on a truck at five in the morning.

Slack water at the Golden Gate was 7:44. My father had us under the middle of the bridge right on it, with the main up and the engine turning slow. He's done it that way every year since 1994.

From underneath, it's orange steel two hundred and twenty feet over your head. At the middle the big cables come down almost to the road on both sides and then go back up to the towers. You can hear the Monday traffic going across into the city, the tires knocking over the joints.

My father took his cap off.

He put it on his knee and held it there and steered with his other hand.

Cal was across from me in the yellow jacket. He looked at my father once. Then he looked up at the bridge and kept looking at it until we were out from under it on the other side.

I kept my hand on the winch and watched the water go by. Every year until 2019 my mother rode under this bridge in the corner by the hatch, with her eyes shut. Since then this is where my father takes his cap off. I don't look at him while it's off.

The year she died he didn't say ten words to anybody from the funeral in February to her birthday. He came to the yard at five-thirty, drove whatever was on the calendar and went home and sat in this boat at the dock till dark. Denise talked for both of them. I ran three trucks and didn't ask him for anything. I'd seen him go quiet once before, the winter I was eight, in a chair in our front room. That time he didn't get up till spring.

On her birthday I was on this dock before five without being asked, and I took the sheets like always. Under the bridge he took his cap off for the first time. Coming back in under it, he said she'd have been sick right there. It was the first thing he'd said to me since February.

All morning I'd been afraid of what a man who didn't know any of that would do right here. Say something nice. Ask me.

We came out the other side into the ocean. The first swell came in under us, long and slow, nothing like the bay, and the boat went up and came down. Out past the headlands the gray water went on until the overcast came down to meet it. Behind us the towers stood up over everything.

My father put his cap back on.

“Coming around,” he said.

My father said “Jib,” and Cal went to the mast and put it back up. With the wind behind us the boat went quiet, and the cold let go some. The flood had turned under us and was carrying us home.

My father had the jib sheet cleated and the tiller under his arm. He didn't need me.

Cal had gone forward again. He was sitting on the deck with his back against the mast and his knees up, holding on to a wire with one hand.

I left my father the sheet. I went forward along the side deck and sat down next to Cal.

I put my shoulder against his arm. He was shivering under the wet jacket. After a while he quit.

I've had the sheets on this boat since I was twelve. I'd left them with a man with bad knees, on my mother's birthday, to sit down next to Cal. I kept waiting to feel like I was stealing the time. I sat there from Alcatraz to the Bay Bridge.

My father didn't call me back.

South of the Bay Bridge the wind came up past twenty in the gusts. My father had Cal take the jib down, and we came the rest of the way on the main. By the time we motored in past the breakwater it was going on eleven. The overcast had lifted some.

“Bow line,” my father said. “Forward cleat.”

Cal stepped off onto the dock with the bow line when he was told. He put it on the first cleat he came to, which was the one at the back corner of the slip. A gust took the bow and pushed it out, and the whole boat swung. I went off the stern with my line and got a turn on before it hit anything.

“Forward cleat,” my father said.

“Which one is forward?” Cal said.

My father pointed with his chin. Cal moved the line and tied it off. My father looked at the knot for a while.

“I watched a video,” Cal said.

“Hm,” my father said.

Getting off the boat took my father longer than getting on. The tide had come up, and the gangway wasn't so steep. He went up it facing front, slow, with Cal one step behind him carrying the cooler, not touching him. At the top my father turned around.

“Six o'clock,” he said to Cal. “My house. Jo knows where.”

“I will be there at six o'clock,” Cal said.

My father hadn't asked me. Nobody new has been to that dinner since she died.

***

I washed the boat down by myself. Fresh water goes on everything the bay touched. I coiled the lines clockwise, the way my father likes them, and hung each one on the cleat it belongs on. I bagged the jib. I put the cover on the main and wiped the salt off the compass with the hem of my shirt. All over the marina the halyards were slapping on the masts in the wind, a few hundred boats nobody had taken out on a Monday.

The corner of the cockpit by the hatch was dry. Nobody had sat in it all morning.

I sat down in it.

The cushion was cold through my jeans. I've watched grown sons walk around their father's recliner for an hour rather than sit in it. I tell them it's a chair. It goes on the truck. Seven years I'd hosed this boat down around her corner.

She'd have been in it this morning, green, asking my father how much longer. She'd have asked Cal about his shoes before I could.

I sat there about a minute with the hose running on the dock. Then I got up and shut the water off and coiled the hose.

When I came up the gangway Cal was at the top. My father's truck was gone.

“How long have you been up here?”

“I have been here since your father left at 11:20.”

He'd stood at the top of the gangway for forty minutes in a gray shirt that had dried stiff with salt and watched me wash my father's boat.

“You've got a company,” I said.

“I have a call at two o'clock.”

“My father asked you to dinner.”

“Your father asked me,” he said. “May I come?”

Kevin would be there. He brings Owen every year and stays for cake. My sister would be there. She'd say something. My mother's name would be on the cake.

“Six o'clock,” I said.

***

My father's house is the one I grew up in, up the hill in South San Francisco. His kitchen table has had the same oilcloth on it since Denise was in high school.

Cal's car was at the curb that evening long before six. My father had linguiça and chicken on the grill in the yard, and Cal stood next to him and held the platter and was told when. Denise came next with the sweet bread and the cake box and her earrings in. She looked at Cal at the grill with our father, and then at me.

“He's holding Dad's PLATTER,” she said, and went in with the cake.

Kevin's gray sedan pulled up last. He got out and looked up the street and down it before he shut his door. Owen came out of the back seat with his phone in his hand and went straight to Cal.

“Did you go under the bridge?”

“Yes.”

“Did you fall in?”

“No. The water came in over the front of the boat.”

Owen thought about it.

“That counts,” he said, and went in to find his grandfather.

Kevin came up the walk with the same bottle of wine he brings every year. He put his hand out to Cal. One pump, looking at him.

“Kevin.”

“Cal.”

Cal held on a second before he let go. He'd had that handshake before. My son gave it to him in June.

“Owen says you sat with him,” Kevin said. “At the volcano.”

“Yes.”

“Thanks for that.”

“I did not do anything,” Cal said. “I sat.”

“Yeah,” Kevin said.

We ate at the kitchen table, the six of us, with the platter in the middle and the window open to the yard.

“Grandpa,” Owen said, “is Cal good at the boat?”

“No,” my father said.

“Is he bad?”

“He's new.”

Owen looked at Cal. “That's not a ranking.”

“It is accurate,” Cal said.

“He get the cleats?” Kevin said.

“Back one,” my father said.

Kevin laughed and shook his head. “Everybody does the back one. The forward cleat's the one by the dock box, Cal. The back one's loose.”

“It's fine,” my father said.

“It's been loose since 2016,” Kevin said. “He won't call the harbor office.”

“The forward cleat is the one by the dock box,” Cal said.

“Yeah.”

“Thank you.”

He put his fork down. His ears had gone red.

I've seen his ears go red at the Pied Piper and on his mother's slab and at the end of his ex-wife's table. I'd never seen it happen over another man, at my father's table, over where you tie up a boat.

I felt that one in my knees. I had to look at my chicken.

Denise put her glass down. “Dawn Pruitt called the office Friday.”

“Who?” my father said.

“The matchmaker. Jo's matchmaker.”

“She's not my matchmaker,” I said.

“She is mine,” Cal said.

Kevin looked at his plate.

“She wanted to know if Dad was SEEING anybody,” Denise said.

Nobody said anything.

“Does she have a dog?” Owen said.

My father tore a piece off the sweet bread. Since 2019 the ladies at church have asked him that about once a month. He says no before they're done asking. He passed Denise the bread.

After supper Denise took the cake out of the box. It comes from the same bakery every year, white, with blue roses on the corners. HAPPY BIRTHDAY CONNIE, in blue. One candle.

Owen blew it out. He's done it every year since he was two.

“Seventy,” Denise said. “She'd have HATED seventy.” She cut the first piece and put it in front of Cal. “Did he tell you why the bridge?”

“No,” Cal said.

“Mom got seasick in the BATHTUB. Twenty-five years he took her out on her birthday, and every single year she threw up over the side right under the Golden Gate Bridge. She said if she had to be sick she wanted to be sick someplace famous.”

Owen laughed. He hears it every year. Kevin laughed. Cal looked at my father before he did anything.

“She liked the bridge,” my father said.

Denise talks about our mother like she's out in the kitchen getting forks. I can't. I never could. I put a piece of cake in my mouth and chewed it a long time.

Under the table Cal's foot found the side of my boot and stayed there.

He'd seen the cap, and me in the corner by the hatch, and her name in blue frosting. He didn't ask me anything about her. I wanted to kiss him at my father's table, in front of my ex-husband and my son. I ate my cake.

My phone went while we were still on the cake. It was Vili. He's never called me on her birthday.

I took it out the back door into the yard.

“Boss,” he said. “I'm sorry.”

“What happened.”

“White truck. Transmission. She went out of second at one-thirty coming up 92 with a full load on, and she didn't come back.”

“Anybody hurt?”

“No. Load's fine. Manny and me got her towed to the yard. The other crew came by after San Carlos, and we put the load on their truck and finished. We were done at five.”

“Why didn't you call me at two?”

“It's your mom's day.”

The white truck had been slipping out of second since May. Vili heard it first. I'd told him to baby it till September, when the jobs slow down.

“Tomorrow's the three-bedroom in Belmont,” I said.

“On the sixteen-footer that's two loads.”

“Then it's two loads. I'll call the shop at seven.”

“Boss.”

“Go home, Vili.”

He didn't hang up. I heard him breathe out.

“Tell Frank hi,” he said.

I stood in my father's yard with the phone in my hand. A transmission for that truck was going to be about four months of the extra I put on the house. I had that done in my head before I'd put the phone in my pocket.

Through the kitchen window I could see Cal at my father's table, between my sister and my ex-husband, listening to Owen.

If I said transmission, he'd ask. He'd ask nice, like he asked about the air on Saturday. *I can have a truck at your yard by seven. May I do that?* And I'd say no. I'd said no to him twice on Saturday and gotten in his car on Sunday. I didn't know which one I'd say tonight, in my father's kitchen, on my mother's birthday, with Kevin passing the bread.

I went back in.

Cal looked up when I came through the door.

“Is everything all right?”

“Work,” I said.

He looked at me a second longer.

“All right,” he said.

I sat down. Across the table Kevin was watching me. He'd put his fork down.

***

When the cake was gone Kevin said Owen had camp in the morning. We walked them out to the car.

Owen got in the back and put his window down to show Cal *Volcanoes I Have Met*. It has one volcano in it.

“It's still one,” Owen said.

“Show me the picture again,” Cal said.

“You've seen it.”

“I would like to see it again.”

I went around to the driver's side with Kevin.

“I can keep him Sunday night too,” Kevin said, “if your week's bad.”

“My week's fine.”

“Okay.”

He put his hand on the roof of the car. He looked up the street and down it.

“In eleven years you asked me for one thing,” he said. “It was to sign.”

He meant the papers. I'd had them drawn up in 2023 and put them on the kitchen table with a pen on top, on a Sunday, after he'd started talking to me again.

Across the roof of the car Cal was looking at my son's phone. He'd heard. He kept his eyes on the volcano.

“You never asked me for anything either,” I said.

“I was at every game, Jo.”

“I know you were.”

“My dad never came to one. Not one.”

“I know, Kevin.”

He looked up the street and down it again. Then he got in and pulled out.

Owen waved at Cal through the back window until they were gone.

Cal came around and stood next to me in my father's driveway.

“You heard that,” I said.

“Yes.”

He stood there with his hands in his pockets.

Kevin wasn't wrong. When the water heater went, Kevin came home to a hot shower and never heard about it. I used to tell it at my father's table for a laugh, with Kevin sitting right there.

Now I had a dead truck in my yard. The man standing in my father's driveway didn't know it. I could have told him then. White truck, second gear, 92, Belmont. I didn't.

“You were jealous,” I said. “Of Kevin.”

“Yes.”

“Over a cleat.”

“He knew which one.”

I put my forehead on his chest and laughed.

“Jo,” my father said from the front step.

He was in the door with the light behind him and the cake plate in his hands. I went up the walk. Cal stayed where he was.

My father doesn't tell you what he thinks till he's sure. He has never said one thing to me about a man.

He looked past me, down the walk at Cal.

“Can't tell yet,” he said, loud enough for the driveway.

He handed me what was left of the cake.

“Bring him back.”
