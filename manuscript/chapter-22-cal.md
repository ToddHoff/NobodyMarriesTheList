## Chapter 22

**Cal**

I woke in my old bed with my feet past the end of it and Jo's hand flat on my back.

The room had gone gray at the slider. I did not know what time it was. My watch was face down on the desk where I had put it. I did not reach for it. Pancake was standing in the doorway looking at me. He does not need a watch. When he stands in a doorway like that, it is after five.

I lay still. Her hand was heavier than it is when she means it. She was asleep. Her knee was against the back of mine. Her hair was across my neck. It smelled like my mother's dryer sheet. The night had come in every slider she opened and was still in the house. I wanted to turn over. I wanted to put my mouth on the place under her ear and wake her up that way. I did not move, because if I moved she would take the hand back.

Pancake shifted his weight and looked at me harder.

Her hand changed. It got lighter until it was her hand again.

“Dog,” she said into my back.

“I know.”

“Go.”

She took her hand back. When I got up, she rolled into the place I had been and pulled the bedspread over her head.

My shirt from Saturday was on the floor with the card in the pocket. I put it on. I put the watch in my pants pocket without turning it over.

West Remington Drive was empty. The church on the corner had its doors shut and the time of the service on the sign out front. Across the street was the school. My mother went in by the side door at six every morning for thirty years. When I was a student there I went in by the front at eight, with everybody else. I did not walk over with her. I told her I could cross a street by myself.

Pancake stopped at the school gate and read the post for a long time. I let him.

Abby's party was at five, at X's house in Menlo Park. Abby was leaving in twenty-three days. Janice had bought the present on Wednesday. It was on the table in my front hall in Woodside, with a card in it in my handwriting. Janice had written the card.

I had meant to ask Jo to come. There had not been a minute on Saturday when she was not on a job, except the ones after eight. I had not used those for asking.

When we got back she was sitting on the front step in her crew shirt from Saturday, with her boots laced and the cooler beside her and her phone in her hand. She had found the last plum. She held it up to me. I shook my head. She ate it.

“My father's bringing Owen home at noon,” she said. “Kevin gets him at four.”

“Abby is having a party tonight,” I said. “It is at five.”

“I know. At her mother's.”

“How do you know?”

“She asked me. Yesterday, in the atrium, while you were holding a box you didn't need.”

“She did not tell me.”

“She told me.”

Janice had said that to me on Thursday, in the same three words. Now Abby was telling Jo things first. Jo watched me work it out. She took her time with the plum while I did.

“Abby said you'd get around to it,” she said.

“I was going to ask you after the walk.”

“What time is it?”

I looked at my wrist. There was nothing on it.

“Six-forty,” she said. “So you were going to ask me at six-forty.”

“I was going to ask you when I got back,” I said. “I am back.”

She waited.

On the walk I had built a sentence for it. It had the word *if* in it twice and left her a way out at the end. It was a good sentence. I have said a thousand like it to rooms. I did not use it.

“Will you come?”

She looked across the street at the school for a while.

I did not know what I would do if she said no. I had planned the sentence and not what came after it.

“Who's going to be there?”

“Abby's friends will be there, and some of their parents, and X.”

“Who's X?”

“X is Abby's mother.”

She turned her head and looked at me.

“You call her X.”

“I have called her X since 2012. It is what she is in my phone.”

“Huh,” Jo said.

She looked back at the school. I did not know what she had filed it under.

“What do people wear to that?”

“I do not know,” I said. “I have not been to one.”

She stopped with the plum pit in her hand and looked at me.

I went back over it to find what I had said. It had been the answer to her question. Abby has had fourteen birthdays since she was four. The parties were at X's house. Janice sent a present to every one of them. I was in another time zone for most of them.

Jo put the pit in her shirt pocket.

“Wear something that isn't gray,” she said.

“I own forty shirts. They are all gray.”

“Then wear the gray one.”

I laughed. I had not expected to.

“Four-thirty,” she said.

“I will drive you home,” I said.

On Saturday she had told me she had the train. Since May she had not been in my car.

She picked up the cooler.

“Okay,” she said.

Before she got in she looked at the front fender. It was still the way she had left it on June 6.

“You never fixed it,” she said.

“I did not want to.”

She looked at me over the roof of the car for a second. Then she got in and put her seat back two notches and her boots up on the dash. I did not say anything about the boots. Pancake put his head between the seats and laid it on her shoulder. She went still. I waited for her to tell me to call him back. After a mile she put her own head back against the seat.

She was asleep before Palo Alto.

I drove in the right lane at the limit. At that hour on a Sunday 101 is nearly empty. Everyone on it passed me. The sun was up over the bay on her side. Her mouth was open a little. Her hand was on her knee, palm up, with the callus from the tape gun across it. I did not want to get there.

At her curb she woke all at once and knew where she was. She put her hand on her purse. Then she took it off again.

She had been going to pay me for the gas.

“Four-thirty,” she said, and got out with the cooler.

***

The present was on the table in my front hall where Janice had left it on Friday. It was the size of a shoebox, in good paper, with ABBY on the envelope in my handwriting.

I opened the envelope. It was a good card. It said what I would have said if I had thought of it a day early. The present was a lamp for a drafting table. Janice has bought Abby a present for everything since Abby was four. She has not been wrong about one yet.

I took a pen and one of my blank cue cards out of the kitchen drawer. I sat at the counter with them for some time. Everything I could think of to put on the card was worse than what was on hers. I put the pen and the card back in the drawer.

I put on number thirty-nine and moved the card to its pocket. I left the watch on the counter, face down.

***

At 4:05 that afternoon, by the clock in my car, I parked across the street from Jo's house. I was twenty-five minutes early.

A gray sedan was pulling away from her curb. A boy in the back seat had his face at the window. He saw my car and lifted his hand.

It was Owen. I lifted mine. I saw nothing of the man driving except the back of his head, which turned once toward my car and once back. Then they were up the hill and gone. Owen would be at his father's for seven days.

Jo came out at 4:30. She had on the green dress. I had seen it once, across twelve courses in Los Gatos. Her hair was down past her shoulders. I had had my hands in it the night before. She had a paper bag in one hand. I got out to open her door. She had it open before I was around the front of the car.

I wanted my hands in her hair again. I got back in and put my hands on the wheel at ten and two instead. I had not held a wheel that way since my driving test.

“What is in the bag?” I said.

“A present.”

“I have a present.”

“That one's yours.” She held the bag on her knees. “Thirty-foot tape. From the yard. We buy them by the case. Every architect I ever moved had a drawer full of tapes that wouldn't go back in. This one goes back in.”

The lamp was in my trunk. Janice had picked it.

On 280 she asked how many times I had been inside X's house.

“I have been inside it four times.”

“In how many years?”

“It has been fourteen years.”

She did not say anything to that. She looked out her window at the hills the rest of the way down.

***

X's house is on a street in Menlo Park where the oaks meet over the middle. I had been inside it four times. All four were in the front hall.

By the clock in my car it was 4:56 in the afternoon. Under the oaks the light came down green. The trunks were older than any house on the block. The houses sat back behind hedges and old board fences. There was no sidewalk. Cars were parked up on the gravel shoulder for a block on both sides.

Jo got out under the trees and stood looking up into them. In that light the green dress went nearly black. We walked up the shoulder toward X's door. Her arm touched mine every few steps. I did not move my arm. I wanted her to keep doing it.

A girl opened the door with her phone already up. She was filming everyone who came in. She filmed the couple ahead of us for about a second each. She kept it on me for four. She had found the billboard.

X came down the hall from the kitchen. She had on a white shirt with the sleeves turned back twice, exactly. Her hair was the length it was in 2007. I do not know how she does that.

“Abby said you were coming,” X said.

She turned to Jo and held out her hand. “I'm Stephanie. Abby said you might. I set a place.”

“Jo,” Jo said, and shook it.

X looked at Jo for two seconds. Then she looked past Jo, down the hall, where Abby had come out of the kitchen. She watched Abby's face while Abby saw Jo. That was what she had come down the hall for.

Abby's face did what it had done on Saturday. She smiled with all of it.

“You came,” she said to Jo.

Jo held out the bag. “It's not wrapped. I wrap all week.”

Abby took out the tape. She hooked the end on the door frame, ran it across to the other side and read it.

“Thirty-six.”

“Thirty-six,” Jo said.

Abby laughed. She let the tape run back. It went in with a snap and did not stick. She clipped it to the pocket of her shorts.

I held out the box.

“Thanks, Dad!” Abby said. “The table's on the patio.”

I heard the exclamation point.

She went back down the hall with Jo's tape on her pocket. I took my box out to the patio and put it on a table with the others. There were twenty-two. Mine was the best wrapped.

***

There were forty-three people in the back yard. I knew six of them. X had set a long table on the lawn, with a card at every place in her handwriting. She had put Jo and me at the far end. The end is where I would have put me.

Jo went in to wash her hands and was back in a minute.

“Second door on the left,” she said, “if you want it.”

“How do you know?”

“I asked.”

I had not asked.

The man across from me said he had driven Abby to school on Thursdays for six years, in a carpool. I had not known there was a carpool. I asked him what she talked about in the car.

“She didn't,” he said. “She drew. Buildings, on the inside of the window, with her finger, when it fogged up. My wife used to make her wipe them off before we got to school.”

I had not known she drew on windows.

Jo was talking to two of Abby's friends about the dorms. She told them to put the heavy boxes on the bottom of the car and the lamps on top, and not to let anybody's father carry the little refrigerator up the stairs by himself. They had known her for twenty minutes. They wanted to know why.

“Sixty pounds and nothing to hold on to,” Jo said. “He got it as far as the landing in Daly City. Then he sat on it for thirty-five minutes and called his brother.”

They laughed. One of them asked whether she had moved anybody famous. Jo looked down the table at me.

“Not yet,” she said.

The girls looked at me. I looked at my plate. My ears were hot. Under the table Jo's foot found the side of my shoe and stayed there.

At ten to seven X stopped behind my chair.

“Will you tell Abby we're doing the cake at seven?” she said. “She's stopped hearing me.”

I went down the table. Abby was at the other end with her friends and her phone and a plate she had not eaten from.

“X wants the cake cut at seven,” I said.

Abby did not look up from her plate.

“Her name is Stephanie, Dad,” she said. “You were married to her for five years. You can say it.”

The girls on either side of her stopped talking. One of them had her phone up. She put it down.

I named her X in my phone in 2012, the week the lawyers started. I could not look at her name on the screen. I never changed it. Remy calls her X when it reads me her messages, because that is what I told it to call her. Abby has heard it in my kitchen for fourteen years. Stephanie is three syllables. I had not said them out loud since 2012.

“Stephanie wants the cake cut at seven,” I said.

Abby looked up at me.

“Okay,” she said.

Both ends of the table had heard it. When I sat down, Jo did not say anything. She moved her chair two inches closer to mine.

***

At seven Stephanie cut the cake. It was green and gold, with CAL POLY on top in white. Everyone stood around the end of the table where she was cutting. Everyone had a phone up.

“Green and gold,” Abby said. “School colors.”

“Green goes with you,” Jo said.

Abby laughed out loud. Her friends looked from her to Jo. Abby did not explain it.

Jo was standing beside me with her shoulder against my arm, laughing. The girl from the front door had her phone on the two of us. She was not taking it for Abby. Abby was at the other end of the table with the knife.

I could have asked the girl to delete it. It was my daughter's party. The girl was a guest at it. I did not want to be the man who took her phone. I let her have it.

Jo did not see the girl. She was watching Abby.

***

After the cake Abby opened her presents at the patio table, with her friends around her. They photographed every one.

When she was done she came down to our end with my card in her hand and sat across from us with a plate of cake. She put the card on the cloth in front of me and squared it to the edge of the table.

I have squared every card I have ever put down. I did not know she did it.

“The lamp's perfect,” she said. “Tell Janice.”

“It is from me.”

“Dad.” She turned the card so that it faced me and put her finger under the date at the top. *7/26*. The seven had a line through it.

“Janice crosses her sevens,” Abby said.

I had read that card at my counter at two o'clock that afternoon. I had not read the date.

“When did you know?” I said.

“When I was seven. My birthday card said seven with a line through it, and so did the note she left on your refrigerator about the dentist.” She ate a bite of cake. “And the videos are Remy. They're good. It always picks the right songs.”

She had been seven in 2015. She had thanked me for every card since, with an exclamation point.

She put her fork down.

“You weren't a bad dad,” she said. “You were a really good account manager.”

Jo's hand came under the table and took mine.

“I have paid your tuition since you were four,” I said. “I have not missed a birthday in fourteen years. I paid off your grandmother's house.”

I heard my voice while I said it. It was the one I use on a stage, warm and low, with the numbers lined up. I was presenting to my daughter.

“I know,” Abby said. She picked up the fork again. “That's three things, Dad. You did all three.”

“Abby!” somebody called from the lawn. “Picture!”

She took her plate and went. The card stayed where she had squared it.

Jo kept my hand and looked out at the lawn. On the lawn Stephanie was fixing the back of Abby's collar for the picture. Jo watched her do it, then looked up at the oaks.

My throat had shut. I kept my face toward the lawn. My watch was on the counter in Woodside. If I had been wearing it, it would have been tapping.

I have hired twenty-six account managers. The best one I ever had knew every client's birthday and the name of every client's dog. Her clients sent her flowers. None of them had seen her in a room in years.

Last night in my old room Jo had asked me whether I still wanted a baby. I asked myself a third time, at the end of a table in Stephanie's yard, with her hand in mine and my daughter on the lawn holding still for a picture.

It was still no. I had wanted to be at our kitchen table in 2010, when Abby counted to ten and skipped six.

The child I could still sit at a table with was on the lawn with Jo's tape on her pocket. She was leaving in twenty-three days.

I took my hand out of Jo's. She let it go.

***

Abby was by the fence with four of her friends. Somebody's father was taking their picture with all five of their phones, one at a time. I waited until he had done the last phone.

“May I talk to you?” I said.

She looked at me. Then she came. We stood by the side of the garage, where the trash cans were.

“Will you stay at my house the weekend before you leave?”

“Which weekend?”

“I mean Friday the fourteenth to Sunday the sixteenth.”

She looked at me for some time.

“Who told you to ask me that?”

She had asked me that at the refrigerator in June. In June the answer was a mover.

“Nobody did,” I said.

She looked past me, up the yard, at the end of the table where Jo was sitting by herself with her hands in her lap.

“She has not said a word,” I said.

Abby thought about that.

“You'd have to be there,” she said. “Not on a plane. Not at the office.”

“I will be home.”

“And you cook.”

“I will cook.”

“Not Rita.”

“I will not ask Rita.”

“Dad. You can't cook.”

“I have nineteen days.”

The corner of her mouth went up. She looked at the trash cans until it came down.

“Okay,” she said.

I listened for the exclamation point. There was not one.

***

At the door Stephanie told Jo it had been nice to meet her. She looked at Abby while she said it.

Jo did not say anything until we were on 280. The hills were dark. I drove in the right lane again.

I had sat for four hours at the end of Stephanie's table, among people who knew where the bathroom was. Line nine had been on my list since April. I had agreed with it.

“Line nine is good family,” I said.

She turned her head. She was quiet for a while.

“We're movers,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Why's it on there?”

I knew why. The reason was across the street from my mother's house. I had walked past it with the dog that morning.

“I cannot tell you yet,” I said.

She looked at me in the dark for a long time. The card stayed in my shirt pocket.

At the San Bruno exit she said, “My father's taking his boat out tomorrow.”

“Your father has a boat.”

“A sloop. It's older than me. He takes it out under the bridge once a year.” She looked out her window. “His knees can't do the front of it anymore. He said to bring you.”

I knew how to have two men who race sailboats on a dock at Oyster Point by five in the morning. I had the call half made before she finished the sentence. It was her father's boat. If I asked anyone, I would ask him.

“I have been on a sailboat once,” I said. “It was a board retreat in 2014. I sat where they put me.”

“Not on this one.”

“What time should I be there?”

“Five-thirty. Oyster Point. Shoes that grip.”

At her curb she leaned across and kissed me. It was not a short one. Then she got out and shut the door. Then she opened it again and leaned in.

“He'll be there at five,” she said. “He's always early.”

“I will be there at a quarter to five,” I said.
