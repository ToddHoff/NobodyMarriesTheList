## Chapter 20

**Cal**

At 4:58 on Thursday morning I turned my watch face up. It had been face in since the Saturday before, when Jo turned it over in her bed. The watch had my board meeting at nine.

Since Saturday night I had not known the time without asking someone. On Maui I asked Owen. He told me to the minute, then how many minutes it was until we could go back in the water. On the Big Island the time was whatever the volcano was doing. I would have predicted that not knowing would bother me. I had gone two whole days without wanting to know.

Pancake was waiting at the door. Rita had walked him for three days. He took me to the end of the road and back at a pace that made his position on that clear.

I had slept four hours and fifty minutes. Nothing had to tell me. On Monday night on Maui I slept six hours with the doors open to the water. On Wednesday I was up at four, because my body was still in Woodside with the dog. This morning, back in Woodside with the dog, my body wanted the water.

Today, on Maui, Gil would carry last week's food out to his truck for the pantry and fill the refrigerator again. I knew which drawer the sweet bread went in.

At 6:10 I opened my laptop at the kitchen counter for the first time since Monday. There were 1,904 unread messages. Janice had been through all of them on Tuesday and left eleven for me, flagged, in the order she wanted them answered. I read the eleven twice before I answered any of them. None of them needed me. Janice flags what needs me. That week nothing had. She had flagged eleven anyway, so that I would have something to do with my hands on Thursday morning. I answered them in her order. It took fifty-one minutes. Twenty-nine hours before, I had been sitting on the ground at four thousand feet.

Janice was at my door at 8:30 with her glasses pushed up into her hair.

“How was it?” she said.

“The fountain was fourteen hundred feet. Both vents went. It ran for three hours and twelve minutes.”

She waited.

“Calvin.”

I tried again. “Before it went, Owen did not talk for an hour. I sat on the ground next to him.”

Janice took her glasses down and folded them. She does that when she is pleased and would rather I did not see it. I have seen it nine times in seventeen years.

“Good,” she said. “Abby is coming to your mother's on Saturday. She told me Tuesday.”

“She did not tell me.”

“She told me.” She put a folder on my desk. “Nine o'clock.”

Abby has told Janice things first since she was twelve. Janice tells me what she decides I need to know. I have never asked her what she leaves out.

At nine I sat at the end of a table with eight people who wanted to talk about the quarter. Nobody asked about the volcano. On the second slide I found I was tapping the table with one finger. It was the song. Owen had played it from somewhere over the ocean until the fog. *Pancake. Make no mistake.* Our general counsel was looking at my finger. I put my hand flat on the table and kept it there.

Somewhere in the revenue slides I was back in the water on Maui with my arms out, Owen telling me not to do anything. I had done it. I had lain on top of the Pacific Ocean for a minute and a half and done nothing at all. It held me up. I did not know where to put that in a board meeting. I kept my hand flat until the quarter was over.

***

I had last seen her at 9:40 on Wednesday night, at the private terminal at SFO, with Owen asleep on my shoulder. She took her own bag and his and would not let me carry either one. She let me carry Owen to her truck. I put him in the back seat and did his belt while she watched me do it with her keys in her hand. Then she said, “Thursday's a workday,” and drove home to San Bruno. I stood beside the plane with nothing in my hands until the woman who looked after the cabin asked if I needed anything.

Jo did not write on Thursday. I had not expected her to. She had three days of work to make up and two trucks out.

At 8:52 on Friday night my phone lit up.

*Tomorrow, 7. It's going to be hot. Bring water for the dog.*

I read it three times. She had been thinking about my dog.

I wrote back that I would. Then I typed that I had missed her on Thursday. I looked at it for some time. Every day I approve about three hundred things that are better written than that. I sent it.

At 8:58 she wrote back.

*Go to bed.*

At 9:01:

*Thursday was long.*

That was as close as she was going to come. I took it. I went to bed.

***

On Saturday morning the car said seventy-one degrees on Remington at 6:52. I had parked across the street, where I have parked since June 6. I had on number thirty-eight. I had a gallon of water and a bowl for Pancake, and my lunch, which I had made at 5:40 with the butter out to the edge. I had decided in the car that I would not bring up Hawaii unless she did.

It was the first Saturday on Remington since June 27. July is her busy month. She had booked paying loads on the Fourth, the eleventh and the eighteenth.

Her truck turned onto Remington at 6:59. Vili was driving. He backed it in on one pass and got down. Jo came around the front with the clipboard already in her hand.

Vili went to the back and pulled the door up. He looked at the house, and at me on the curb with a gallon of water, and at her.

“Forty minutes down 101,” he said, “for a man who takes ten minutes on a lamp.”

“He's paying for the drive,” Jo said.

“He's not paying for my Saturday.”

He went for the runners. On Monday he had run both her trucks in Burlingame while she was over the ocean. On the first piece out she gave him the light end. In eight Saturdays I had never seen Vili on the light end.

I could have paid for his Saturday. I had the number before he reached the runners: a day at time and a half, and something on top for Burlingame. I kept it to myself. He had given up his Saturday for her, not for me.

Jo looked at the water.

“Good,” she said. “Put it in the shade. Your old room, then the hall.”

“Where is Owen?” I said.

“At my father's. Owen's showing him the volcano videos. My father's watched them six times. He still hasn't said what he thinks.”

Her nose was peeling. It had been in the sun on Maui for two days. She had on the gray crew shirt and the boots, her hair tied back tight. On Tuesday night it had been loose in the wind at the rim. She had put her hand flat on my back and left it there. I felt it through the sweatshirt for three hours. Now she was on a job. On a job she talks in colors and directions. She looks at you for as long as a color takes. I wanted to put my mouth on the peeling place anyway.

“Green or blue?” she said, in the door of my old room.

I looked at the room. It held a twin bed, a desk with a lamp clamped to it, and a shelf of my science fair ribbons, blue and red, in the order I won them. The bed was made, with the corners tucked. I had not been in the room since 2003. My mother had put the ribbons up as I won them, one at a time, for nine years. I did not remember ever looking at the shelf.

“Green,” I said, about the ribbons.

She put green on them without a word and went on to the desk.

My mother's house is glass on three sides. It was built in 1960. Nobody ever put air conditioning in it. By ten the thermostat in the hall said eighty-eight. The slab under our feet was the coolest thing in the house. Pancake lay flat on it in the hall with his legs out behind him.

***

At 10:20 that morning a white sedan pulled in behind my car. It had a dealer frame from Colma on the plate. A woman got out with three iced coffees in a cardboard tray. She had on earrings and lipstick and shoes that had never been near a ramp. She was taller than Jo by most of five inches. She was dressed for a different day than anyone else on the block.

I had heard her voice twice on the phone and once from a window.

She looked at the house first, from the roof down, the way an appraiser does. Then she looked at me.

“Cal Brennan,” she said.

“You are Denise.”

“I'm the sister.” She held out the tray. “One of these is yours. I drive under your billboard every morning. I figured I owed you a coffee.”

There were three coffees. She had planned for me. Mine had milk and sugar in it. I drink coffee black. I drank it.

Before she went in, she stopped at the truck and asked Vili how Burlingame had gone on Monday. He told her forty minutes late and a woman who wanted every lamp wrapped twice. She asked him two more questions about Burlingame. She did not ask him one about Hawaii.

She had driven forty minutes on a Saturday to see whether the business ran without her sister. It did. She looked at the truck for a while, then at the house. Then she went in.

Jo came out to the atrium with tape on her wrist.

“What are you doing here?”

“You texted me HOME,” Denise said. “One word. After HAWAII. That's not a REPORT.”

“I was tired.”

“You were in a ZEPPELIN.” She handed Jo a coffee. “I'll wait.”

“Lunch is at eleven.”

“Then I'll wait till eleven.”

She sat down on the bench in the shade with a tote bag and a calculator, took a stack of mileage sheets out of the bag and went to work on her knee.

***

At eleven Jo called lunch. She went out to the bench. Denise was on my end of it.

I took my lunch to the kitchen counter by the slider. The slider was open for the air, with the screen shut. Pancake came and lay on my feet. From the counter I could see the backs of their heads and hear every word.

I could have gone to the living room. I told myself there was no chair left in the living room, which was true. I told myself my lunch was on the counter, which was also true. I stayed at the counter because I wanted to know what she would say about the trip when I was not there to hear it.

“Start at the plane,” Denise said.

“Eight seats. A woman whose whole job was the three of us. Owen asked her where she slept.”

“Where DID she sleep?”

“He never found out. It's been bothering him all week.”

“The house.”

“No front door.”

“How do you LOCK it?”

“There's a gate. And a man named Gil.”

“What's in the refrigerator?”

“Milk. Every Thursday for six years. The old goes to a food pantry.”

“Six years of MILK.”

It was the third time I had heard that sentence in a week. Her sister had said the other two.

“Did you ride it?” Denise said. “The blimp.”

“It's not a blimp. It's got a frame inside.” I heard Jo's ice. “I rode it.”

It was my sentence. I said it in her kitchen on Sunday, when she called it a blimp.

“And?”

“He got sick.”

Denise laughed so hard she had to put her coffee down on the bench.

“Cal Brennan threw up in a ZEPPELIN.”

“He didn't throw up. He went gray and sat in the back with his eyes shut and my rag. He's had my rag since June.” A pause. “Owen had his whole head out the window. You could hear a dog bark a thousand feet down.”

She told it better than it was. I had not known she would tell it at all.

“And the volcano.”

Jo did not answer right away.

“It didn't go,” she said. “All afternoon. The scientist said maybe tomorrow. We were flying home tomorrow. Owen quit talking.”

“Like Kevin.”

“Like Kevin.”

“What'd Cal Brennan do? Call the volcano?”

“He sat down on the ground.”

Denise waited.

“That's it?”

“That's it. He sat down next to him in his good pants and didn't say a word for an hour and a half. He didn't look at his watch once.” Her voice went lower. “Kevin goes quiet in the next room. I say *next time, bud*. Nobody's ever sat down next to him, Denise. Me included.”

I put my sandwich down. My watch tapped against my wrist. I turned it face in without reading it.

I had shipped an airship across an ocean. I had made a promise to a governor. I had been sure the airship was the thing.

I had sat down at the rim because I could not think of anything to buy. In June I had asked whether there was anyone to call about a volcano. There was not. For three days I had thought of it as the part of the trip I had failed to arrange.

I wanted to go out through the screen and sit down on the bench between them. I wanted to tell her I had not known what else to do. I stayed where I was, with Pancake on my feet. If I went out there she would stop talking. I wanted to hear the rest of it more than I wanted to be seen.

“Then it went,” Jo said. “You see it first. You count to nine before you hear it. Fourteen hundred feet. Two of them. I had my hand on my chest like a lady in church. I didn't know I'd put it there.”

“You slept,” Denise said. “I can see it on you.”

“Till ten past seven. Wednesday.”

“You did NOT.”

“Ten past seven.”

I had been in the water since six, failing to float. I had thought she was up on the lanai the whole time, watching me fail. She had been asleep.

Denise did not say anything for a while. Then she said, “Mom would've stood at that rope all night.”

Jo did not answer that. Denise let it go.

Jo has told me about her mother once, on the first night, in two sentences. Denise said one in thirty seconds and went on to the next thing.

“You sent me the house,” Denise said.

“I sent you a picture.”

“You didn't answer my text.”

“Which one?”

“You know which one.”

It took Jo a while.

“I'm not going to marry a house, Denise.”

“Nobody said the HOUSE.”

I had a list for a wife. I had a plan to have one before Abby left. Eleven women had been on that plan. I had never heard any of them say the word to anybody. My watch tapped again, face in, against the bone of my wrist. I could feel it counting.

Neither of them said anything after that. Pancake got up off my feet and went to the screen and put his nose against it.

Denise looked over at the screen. Then she looked through it, at me.

“How long have you been standing there?” she said. She did not sound surprised.

“I have been here since the milk.”

Jo turned around on the bench. Her face went red from the collar up. I had seen it do that once before, at a thousand feet.

“Hall,” she said. She stood up and folded her foil into a square and went in past me without looking at me.

Denise had looked straight at the kitchen at 11:06 and smiled into her coffee. She had asked about the text at 11:21. I had been arranged. I knew what it looked like. Janice has done it for me since 2009.

At 11:45 I walked Denise to her car. She put the tote on the passenger seat and stood with the door open and the heat coming out of it.

“Two more Saturdays after this one,” she said.

“Yes.”

“Then what?”

“I do not know.”

It was true. Remy had a plan for what came after. I had not read it. What I had was a house to empty and two Saturdays to empty it in. When it was empty, there would be nothing in it that needed a mover.

She looked at me for some time. She has her sister's eyes. Hers move more.

“Find out,” she said, and got in.

At 11:48 that morning I sat in my car across the street with the engine off and called Dawn Pruitt. She had called me twice since May. I had not called her. For eight years Janice had made my appointments with her.

She picked up on the second ring. When a door closed behind her, the street noise went away.

“Cal.”

“Denise Silveira just asked me what happens after the house is empty,” I said. “I did not know. You are the one who closes things for me.”

“I've done it seven times with you. The four before me I can't speak for.”

“Then tell me the seven.”

She poured. I waited for it.

“What did they say?” I said. “When you closed their files.”

“You want to know that?”

“Yes.”

“You've never asked.”

“No.”

She was quiet long enough that I looked at the screen to see whether the call had dropped. It had not. Across the street Vili came out of my mother's house with a lamp in each hand and looked at my car.

“I call the women when I close the file,” Dawn said. “Not you. I write down the first thing they say. I've got all seven here.”

She had pulled them before I asked. I have sat across tables from people like that since I was twenty-three. I raised money from some of them.

“Read them to me,” I said.

“The first one said generous. Then lovely. Busy. Generous. Very generous. Kind. The last one said generous. Then she said, he sends the most beautiful things.”

I counted while she read. Three of them had said generous, a fourth very generous. Not one of them had said anything I could not have put on a slide.

“None of them were unkind,” I said.

“None of them. Not one of yours needed a minute before she answered.”

“Is that good?”

“Cal. You know what a woman means when the best thing she can tell me about a man after four months is that he was generous?”

“No.”

“She means she never met him. She met the packages.”

The car was hot. I had left the windows up. Across the street Vili had put the lamps on the truck and gone back in, leaving the front door of my mother's house open on the atrium.

I went down the seven. I could not see their faces. I could see the inn in Napa, and the boxes. Remy had chosen the boxes. I had approved them in about a second each. Each of the seven had written to thank me for them. I had approved the replies.

“What do I do with that?” I said.

“You don't have to do anything with it. You asked me what they said.” She poured. “Let me ask you one. What does Joanna do when you're wrong?”

I did not have to look for it.

“On the first Saturday I used her tape gun wrong and cut my thumb,” I said. “She said, let me see it. She taped it and gave me thirty-six more boxes to build.”

“And then?”

“She stayed in the hall until I had built them.”

Dawn did not say anything for a while. I heard her set the cup down on something hard.

“Which of the seven ever taped your thumb?”

“None of them.”

“No.”

I could name nineteen times since May that I had been wrong in front of Jo. She had never sent me a thank-you.

“Two more Saturdays,” I said. “Then the house is empty. Denise asked me what then.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That I did not know.”

“Good answer.”

“It is not an answer.”

“From you it is.”

“Why do you call them at the end?” I said. “The women. You said not me.”

She took a while with that one.

“Because nobody else does,” she said. “When it's over, everybody calls the man.”

Then she was gone. It was 11:58. I sat in the car with the windows up until I could not breathe in there. Then I went back across the street.

***

Jo was in the hall with her tape measure, measuring the frames. The numbers were already on her clipboard. She was measuring them again.

I stood at the end of the hall.

“I should have gone to the living room,” I said.

“Yeah.” She wrote down a number she already had. “You should have.”

“I wanted to hear what you would say when I was not there.”

She let the tape run back into the case. It went in with a snap.

“How much did you hear?”

“I heard all of it from the milk.”

She looked at the frames instead of me. Her face was still red at the top of her collar, with sweat at her hairline. The hall was the hottest place in the house. It was three feet wide. I was close enough to see the peeling place on her nose and the freckles under it that had not been there two weeks ago.

“Then you heard me say you threw up.”

“I heard you say I did not throw up.”

“You went gray.”

“I went gray,” I said.

One corner of her mouth went up. She got it back down. She put the pencil behind her ear.

“You owe me one,” she said.

“What do I owe you?”

“I'll tell you when.”

I already owed her half a linguiça sandwich. I put this one under it.

Vili came through the far end of the hall with the dolly, looked at the two of us, and went out again the way he had come. I wanted to kiss her. She was on a job. Her crew was in the next room. She had just told her sister she would not marry a house. I did not.

“Frames,” she said. “Green or blue.”

***

Abby's car pulled in at 12:05, where Denise's had been. She came up the walk in shorts and sandals with her sunglasses on her head and stopped when she saw Jo in the doorway with the clipboard.

“You're Jo,” she said.

“You're the one on the door,” Jo said. “Three to fourteen.”

Abby looked at her for a second. Then she smiled with her whole face. I had not seen that smile aimed at an adult since she was eleven.

“Grandma did it every birthday,” Abby said. “With a ruler on my head.”

“My father used his hand,” Jo said.

They were in the atrium for ten minutes. Abby told Jo she moves into her dorm at Cal Poly on the eighteenth of August, for architecture. I had it as September. It was on my refrigerator. I had not read it. It was twenty-four days. I had been counting on September without knowing I was counting on anything.

She wanted to know about the house. Jo has moved more Eichlers than Abby had been inside. Jo showed her where the posts carry the roof and where the beams run out past the glass. She told her the heat was hot water in copper pipes in the slab, so never to let anybody drill the floor.

“Grandma said the floor was warm in winter,” Abby said. “I used to lie on it.”

“Everybody lies on it,” Jo said. “I've found kids' drawings under the couches in four of these.”

I stood in the hall with a box I did not need to be holding. My daughter was telling a woman I had met in May more about this house than she had told me in eighteen years. She was asking her questions. She was listening to the answers with her head on one side, which is how she listened to her grandmother.

Abby had come for the kite boy. She told Jo so in the hall. There were nine frames on that wall. Six held Abby. One held me at a podium. Two held people nobody knew, a couple laughing on a beach and a boy with a kite. They had come with the frames.

“Grandma kept him,” Abby said, “because she said he looked like Dad at seven.”

I had never had a kite.

Abby lifted the kite boy off his nail. She looked at him for a while.

“When I was seven I asked Dad how computers think,” she said to Jo.

“Did you want to know?” Jo said.

“No.”

“Did it work?”

“He talked for an hour.”

I remembered the afternoon. I had counted it as our best hour. I had thought she wanted to know.

I went back through the other hours I had counted. There were not many. There was a dinner when she was twelve, and a drive to look at a school when she was sixteen. I did not want to check them against what she would say about them.

When Abby took the kite boy down, the wire on the frame beside it let go. It was the podium. It hit the hall floor face first. The glass went everywhere.

“Don't move,” Jo said to Abby. Abby was in sandals.

Pancake came down the hall at the sound, the way he comes to anything that falls. Jo put her flat hand out at him.

“Stay.”

He stayed.

A week ago I found the scar on the back of her calf. She got down on her knees on the floor eighteen inches from my dog, with her back half to him so that she could see the grout. She tore off a length of green tape, wrapped it around her fingers sticky side out and went over the floor with it, square by square, lifting the slivers out of the grout so he would not cut a foot. When the tape was full she folded it on itself and tore another. She did not look up at me.

I did not say anything. If I had, she would have had to answer me.

She picked up my photograph out of the glass and held it up to me from the floor.

“Green or blue?”

“Blue,” I said.

Abby looked at me.

***

Abby went down the hall to wash the glass dust off her hands. I heard the water run in the hall bathroom. Then it stopped. She did not come out.

I went down the hall. She was standing in the bathroom with my mother's robe in one hand, off its hook. The door was still on its hinges, with green tape on its edge. Her lines went up the right side of it in my mother's blue ballpoint, ABBY 3 to ABBY 14. Mine were on the left, CALVIN 4 to CALVIN 12.

On her side, half an inch above her nine, a short flat line in carpenter's pencil said OWEN 9.

“Who's Owen?” she said.

“He is Jo's son. He is nine.”

She looked at the pencil. Then she looked at her own nine, in pen, half an inch below it.

“You measured him,” she said.

“Jo put her hand flat on his head. I wrote it.”

She hung the robe back on its hook over all of it.

“You were here for his,” she said.

“Yes.”

I could have found out where I was for every one of her lines. It is all on a calendar. Janice would have it by the end of the day. I did not need Janice for her nine. On most days in 2017 I was somewhere with a podium.

My phone was in my hand. I do not know when I had taken it out. It lit while she was looking at me.

I had been on that weekend eleven times. It was the same inn each time. I could not have told you its name. Remy knew the name. Remy knew which room.

Abby read it upside down. She was four the first time. She has watched eleven of these.

“The usual,” she said.

She did not say anything else about it. She went back down the hall for the kite boy. At the front door she stopped.

“Dad,” she said. “Don't do the thing you do.”

Then she went out to her car.

I stood in the hall with the phone.

“Cancel the weekend,” I said.

*Removed,* the screen said. *Twelve items remain. Review?*

I did not open it. I knew what it would show me. It would show me eleven women and four point one months, and the plan it had made from them, and where I was in it. I put the phone in my pocket.

Jo was still on the hall floor with a flashlight, lying almost flat, sighting along the grout for anything that caught the light. Pancake was lying where she had told him to.

I got down on the floor beside her. The slab was cool through my pants. She moved over to make room. Her shoulder came up against mine. She smelled like tape and sunscreen and the cab of her truck. I did not ask her anything. She did not tell me anything. I held out my hand. After a second she gave me the flashlight.

“Lower,” she said.

I held it lower.
