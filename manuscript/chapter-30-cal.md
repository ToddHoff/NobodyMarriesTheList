Abby's car came up my drive at 4:58 on Friday afternoon, two minutes before she had said. I had the oven on at 350 and my mother's tin open on the counter.

“Why are you awake at midnight?”

It was a long time before she answered. In eight years I had not asked her one thing about herself.

“I don't sleep much,” she said. “I haven't since my divorce. I pour coffee and I read files, and every so often a client calls me at midnight because somebody didn't do what they expected. When I practiced I billed four hundred an hour for this. Now it comes with the package.” I heard her pick the cup back up. “Go play your mother's message, Cal.”

Then she was gone.

“Play my mother's voicemail,” I said. “October 6.”

“Darlene Brennan. October 6, 2:14 PM. Forty-seven seconds. Summary: asks you to call when you have a minute. Not urgent.”

“Play it,” I said.

There was a hiss first, and a click. Then a room. I knew the room. It was her kitchen. I could hear the refrigerator.

*Calvin, it's your mother.*

She waited. On the telephone she always left a space for the other person to say something.

*I never know with these if it's you that gets them.*

Her voice was lower than I had it.

*It's Monday. Nothing's wrong. Pat says hello. I'm making pot roast. Enough for two.*

Behind her the cord knocked against the wall. She was walking with the phone, as far as it would go.

*Call me when you have a minute. You, Calvin. Not Janice. Janice is very nice.*

There was a pause.

*It's not urgent.*

Then she did not hang up. For seven seconds there was the kitchen. The refrigerator. Her breathing, close to the phone. Something outside, a car on Remington going by slowly, the way cars go past the school.

*Okay,* she said, not to me.

The receiver knocked against the hook twice before it went down.

The hall was quiet. Pancake had lifted his head at her voice. He kept it up, with his ears forward, looking at the phone in my hand. I scratched his neck.

Remy had not been wrong. Every word in the summary was hers. She had asked me to call when I had a minute. She had said it was not urgent. A summary leaves out whatever does not change what the person reading it will do. I built it to do that. It had left out *You, Calvin. Not Janice.*

I thought of the shoebox on my desk. *Calvin, house.* She had not been paying off a house. She had been waiting for me to come and ask her why. Every first of the month for thirteen years she had written my name on the front of it, in case I did.

I had a minute on the ninth. She died on the eighth.

I sat on the floor of my hall with my dog. I did not get up. I did not know how long. I did not look.

I played it again. Forty-seven seconds. Then I played it again.

By the second time I could smell the pot roast. By the fourth time I knew where the cord hit the wall. By the sixth I could hear the car on Remington coming before she said *Okay*. On the ninth I heard something I had missed every time before it. Before *Calvin* she took a breath in, the way a person does before she knocks on a door.

I played it until I lost the count. Remy would have the number. I did not ask.

Pancake put his head in my lap. Each time she said *Calvin* and waited for me to answer. Each time the space she left was the same length. Nothing I had would fit in it.

At 12:38 in the morning the screen lit in my hand.

```
REMY · 12:38 AM
Jo Silveira: unanswered, 3 days.
No action queued.
```

I read it. I had queued nothing.

I left it there.
