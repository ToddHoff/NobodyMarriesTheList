## Chapter 15

**Jo**

When Cal Brennan asked on Sunday night if he could work a load, I put him on my payroll.

*May I work a load with your crew this week? I will do what Vili tells me.*

That was the whole message. There was no semicolon in it and nothing about anybody's mother. Janice would have given me a reason. He hadn't.

He'd written to me himself once since the fight, on the Wednesday night. *I still owe linguica.* Reading it in my kitchen in my socks, I knew who wrote it by the last word, because Janice would have looked it up. He sent four words. One was spelled wrong.

I didn't answer it for three days, because I didn't trust what I'd say. On Saturday night I fixed his spelling and sent that. It was the most I could manage.

I read Sunday's at my kitchen counter with a dish towel over my shoulder. Owen was in the bath, telling his phone about a dog he'd seen. I read it twice before I went through it the way I go through a contract, looking for the piano. A man who could buy my company before lunch wanted to carry boxes for it. There had to be something in that he wasn't saying.

I couldn't find it. That didn't mean it wasn't there.

A client doesn't carry. I tell every one of them on the first morning, because of my insurance and because there are two men standing right there that I pay to do it. A man on my payroll carries whatever Vili hands him.

*On my truck you're on my payroll,* I wrote. *Workers' comp. Thursday, 5:30, at the yard. Bring gloves.*

*Yes.*

It came back before Owen had let the water out. One word. Owen answers me like that when he's scared I'll change my mind.

I wanted to see what he did with no card, no Janice and nobody to send. I wanted to see him tired, too. I didn't tell Denise that part.

Denise came over the night before the load to sleep on my couch, because Owen was with me that week and I had a six o'clock. She had a new-hire packet from the office in her purse.

“You're HIRING him.”

“For a day.”

“At what rate?”

“Starting.”

She wrote his name on the folder and handed it to me. “I'm going to frame his pay stub.”

After Denise took the couch, I sat up at the counter with the Delgados' estimate and walked their stairs in my head, the turn at the landing and who'd be under what. I put Cal on boxes, where a new man can't hurt anybody. Then I went through the whole house again from the top and put him on boxes again.

***

He was in my yard before me the next morning. I'd planned to beat him there.

He was standing beside his car under the light over the dock, in the gray shirt, with a pair of work gloves in one hand. They still had the cardboard on them.

“Good morning,” he said.

“You're early.”

“You said 5:30. I did not want to be late on the first day.”

I put the packet on the hood of the sixteen-footer. Two tax forms and the one where you show ID. He filled them out standing up, in small square capitals, and showed me his license without being asked. He was done in under four minutes. Then he squared the forms to the edge of the hood, the way he squares Janice's cards.

I'd seen those capitals once before. He'd drawn a line on his mother's bathroom door along the side of my finger and written OWEN 9 beside it. He'd taken a long time over the O.

I handed him a crew shirt from the box in the cab, gray, with SILVEIRA across the shoulders.

“Everybody on my truck wears one,” I said. “Clients like to know who's in their house.”

He took his own shirt off right there in my yard.

I'm not going to pretend I looked at the truck. He's built like a man who works out four mornings a week, which he does. The light over the dock was not kind to anybody but him. There was gray in the hair on his chest. There was a number on the label of the shirt in his hand, in black marker.

He put on my shirt. It said my name across his back.

It's my father's name. He had it painted on the first truck in 1987. I've put it on the back of every man I ever hired. I quit seeing it a long time ago. On Cal it went from one shoulder blade to the other. I was still mad at him. I wanted to put my hand flat on it anyway, between his shoulders, and leave it there. I picked his forms up off the hood instead.

Vili pulled in next and looked at Cal in that shirt for a long time.

“So he's helping,” Vili said. “Or he's a client.”

“Today he's payroll.”

Vili took the gloves out of Cal's hand and pulled the cardboard off them. Then he gave them back.

We were two trucks. Vili rides with me. He has for nine years. That morning I gave him the big truck by himself and put Cal in my cab with Manny.

If Vili had done that, moved a crew around so he could sit next to somebody, I'd have docked him.

Vili looked at me over the hood of the big truck. He whistled two notes and got in.

I had two reasons ready if he asked. Manny sleeps better against a door. Vili backs the big truck better than anybody I've got. Both were true.

Vili didn't ask.

Manny was asleep by the airport. He always is.

That left Cal in the middle of the bench with his knees up, because the middle seat in a sixteen-footer is built for a man with no legs. His thigh was against mine from the yard to Millbrae. There isn't four feet anywhere in that cab. I'd thought about that when I put him there.

“Two-story,” I said. “Mr. and Mrs. Delgado, going to a condo in San Mateo. What won't fit in a condo rides with me to their son's garage. The stairs turn at the landing. There's a room upstairs that's nothing but books. You're on boxes.”

“How do I carry one down the stairs?”

“On your shoulder, not in front of you. That way you can see your feet.”

“The box goes on my shoulder,” he said, “so that I can see my feet.”

He says everything back. He's putting it in a file. I've had men on my crew for three years who never asked how to carry a box down a staircase. I'd have hired him on that alone.

“Did you eat?” I said.

“I ate at five.”

“Eat again at nine. You'll want it.”

“I did not bring anything.”

Manny, asleep against the door, said, “I got you.”

He didn't say anything else until we got off at Millbrae Avenue.

The last time I'd kissed him, I had a fistful of his shirt in his mother's hall and my son was thirty feet away. That was twelve days ago. Since then I'd found out who wrote his texts. I'd been careful with him for nine days. Now I drove with both hands on the wheel. His leg was warm through my jeans. When I changed lanes it pressed into mine. It was still there when I straightened out.

“Owen asked whether my shirts were numbered,” he said.

“I know. I'm the one who told you.”

“They are. I did them myself, last Thursday night.”

“I saw the label.”

He looked straight out the windshield. “I would like him to know.”

“Then you tell him,” I said. “He'll have more questions.”

***

We padded the banister first. Mrs. Delgado watched us do it from the kitchen door with her arms crossed until we were done.

The book room was at the top of the stairs. Books go in small boxes, because a big box of books will put a man on the floor. Cal carried them down one at a time on his shoulder, so he could see his feet.

He went too fast. Men who lift in a gym all go too fast at first, because nothing in a gym has corners or a turn at the landing. By the second hour he came out of the book room with a box on each shoulder.

“One,” Vili said from the landing.

“Noted.”

“Seven,” I said.

Vili looked at me. “Seven what?”

“Get the dolly.”

He'd said this one to Vili with a box on each shoulder, like a man who didn't know he'd said anything. The number was out of my mouth before I'd looked at it.

When the book room was half empty, the Delgados' son came out of the kitchen with a coffee and stopped at the bottom of the stairs. He was about thirty-five. He'd come to help his parents move, which so far meant standing in doorways. He watched Cal come down with a box on his shoulder and got his phone out.

“No pictures of my crew,” I said.

“That's Cal Brennan.”

“He's new. He's doing fine.”

The son put the phone in his pocket and followed Cal out to the truck.

“Mr. Brennan. If you have five minutes at lunch, I've been working on something.”

Cal set the box on the ramp. He didn't drop his shoulders or change his voice. He said what he'd have said about the weather.

“I work for her today.”

He went back in for the next box. The son stood on the lawn a while before he went back to the kitchen, where his mother put him on the lamps.

I heard all of it from the back of the truck. I'd been watching him all morning for the piano. A man doing this for my benefit would have checked whether I'd heard. He never looked at the truck. Manny had been standing on the ramp with a box for I don't know how long.

Then Vili put him on the high end of a dresser. On stairs the low man takes the weight, so the low man calls the lift. Vili called it. Cal lifted when he was told and not before. At the turn on the landing he said “Wait.” They waited, then brought it around with about a pencil's width between the corner and the wall. I was at the bottom of the stairs holding my breath. I don't do that.

His watch tapped on every landing. I could hear it from the truck. After a few trips he took it off, put it in his pocket and kept going. After that I watched his neck instead.

His neck was wet at the hairline and red where my collar rubbed it. When he bent for a box, the shirt went tight across his shoulders. I lost count of the boxes twice and had to start over.

Later I went up to check the book room while he was coming down. The landing on those stairs is about three feet square. I should have waited at the bottom. I went up. We turned sideways to get past each other, him with a box on his shoulder, me with my face an inch from his collarbone. He smelled like cardboard and his own soap. His breathing was loud. Mine wasn't any better.

I went on up and stood in an empty room full of book dust until my face cooled off.

The Delgados had kept books in that room a long time. You could read it in the carpet. Along three walls, where the shelves had stood, it was still the brown it came in. The middle had gone pale from the window. There were screw holes in the plaster at shoulder height where the shelves had been fastened to the wall.

I did what I do in every empty room. I opened the closet and ran my hand along the top shelf to the back, where people put the thing they mean to come back for. I got down on my knees and looked along the baseboard for anything that had rolled. There was nothing but dust in the light from the window.

I stayed on my knees a minute and listened to him on the stairs. He was going down slower than he had first thing and coming back up slower than that. My face still hadn't cooled off. I told myself it was the heat. All the heat in that house had come up the stairs by then. I waited until I heard him on the bottom step before I went down.

My shirt was dark down Cal's back, under my name. He'd stopped talking at all except to say “Yes” to Vili. He took the stairs slower now, stopping on the landing to turn his whole body instead of his shoulders. Nobody had told him that. He'd watched Vili do it twice.

I worked the truck. I load my own trucks. I stood in the back with the clipboard and the pads and called out where each piece went. Every time he came up the ramp with a box, I told him where and he put it there. He never asked me twice.

He set the last box from the book room on the ramp just before lunch, but he didn't pick up the next one.

He was looking at his hands. They wouldn't close all the way. I've seen it on every new man I've ever hired, usually by the third hour. He'd lasted five hours.

“Sit,” I said.

He sat down on the ramp. A new man always argues with me about that. He didn't.

I wanted to sit down on the ramp beside him and open his hands for him, a finger at a time. The first summer I rode with my father's crew, I couldn't close my hand around a sandwich and wouldn't say so. Dad didn't say anything either. He opened my fingers in the cab and put the sandwich in them.

I didn't touch him. Today he was payroll. Manny was coming across the lawn with a chair. I got a water out of the cooler and set it on the ramp beside Cal. I went up into the truck and restacked boxes that didn't need it.

***

Lunch is at eleven on every job I run. We ate on the Delgados' curb in the shade of the big truck, the four of us, with our backs against the wheels.

He'd made his own lunch. I checked the bread before I could stop myself. Both pieces, out to the edge.

When Manny offered him a pickle out of a jar, he took it and said thank you. Then Manny asked him if it was true he had a robot that answered his phone. He said yes, but that it was not answering it today. Vili ate and watched me. I didn't look at Vili.

Vili had seen me check the bread. There isn't much Vili has to see twice. I looked across the street and ate my sandwich.

I was halfway through my sandwich when Cal put his down and stood up.

My guys both looked at him, because nobody stands up at lunch. He was a little gray in the face, my shirt stuck to him.

I thought he was going to be sick. New men get sick sometimes, the first day, from the stairs and from eating at nine because I told them to. I had my hand on the cooler lid.

“Jo,” he said.

His shoulders came down as his voice dropped and went smooth. I'd heard that voice in Los Gatos, at the sixth course. He heard it too. He stopped with his mouth open.

Manny stopped chewing.

I sat on the curb with my sandwich and waited to see what was under that voice.

Cal started again. This time it was his own voice, which isn't any good at this.

“I would like to take you somewhere on Saturday,” he said. “After one o'clock.”

“Where?”

“I do not know yet. I have not booked anything. I have not told Remy. Or Janice.” He looked around at Vili, Manny and the Delgados' son, who had come out onto the porch. Then he looked at me. “That is my whole plan.”

His hands were shaking at his sides. It could have been the boxes.

I looked for the piano anyway. I'd been at it since Sunday night at my counter. This had even less in it than his text.

Then I counted Saturday. We'd be done at his mother's house by one. There was nothing after that. My heart was going. I'd forgotten I was holding half a sandwich in my lap.

Vili set his sandwich down on his knee. Vili, who answers everything, had his mouth open to answer this.

“Yes,” I said, before he could.
