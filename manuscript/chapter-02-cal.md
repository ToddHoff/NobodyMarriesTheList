## Chapter 2

**Cal**

I have turned my phone face down on a table once in my life, and I can give you the time. It was 8:45 on a Thursday night in May.

I cannot give you the reason. I have looked for it.

The forty-five minutes is not rudeness. It is a test condition. Same bar, same table, same order, same length. By minute forty I know. At forty-five Remy sends the car and a nine o'clock that does not exist, and nobody's feelings are hurt, because nobody is told.

Remy wrote the list too. A woman at the bar that night had called it she. About half of our users do, and the other half say he. I built it, and I call it it.

In April I asked it to read fourteen years of my history and describe the woman I would choose. It took nine seconds and came back with twelve lines. I read them. They were right. I approved them.

The woman across from me fit none that I could see.

She was beautiful. The list never used the word. It asked for tall and thirty-six and well dressed, and I had assumed those added up to it. She had dark hair tied back for work and coming loose from it, brown eyes that went straight at whatever she was looking at, and a wide mouth that had not decided yet whether I was funny. She had been in the sun. She wore no makeup that I could find, and I looked.

She had tucked a carpenter's pencil behind her ear without noticing she had done it, and she was eating my shrimp.

“How old was I supposed to be?” she said.

“You were supposed to be thirty to thirty-six.”

“I'm forty-four.”

“I know. It is on the card.”

“It isn't, actually.”

She took the pencil down and pulled the card over. Under the first line she wrote *AGE 30 TO 36* in square capitals, the kind people use on boxes. She drew a line through it.

“Why thirty-six?”

“It is arithmetic.”

“Whose?”

I did not have an answer. Remy does not show its work unless you ask, and I had not asked.

In April I had approved it at my desk in about a minute. Now I did the arithmetic at the table, with her waiting. Thirty-six was five years under me. Thirty was eleven years under me and twelve over my daughter.

She slid the card back with two lines on it, both dead. She was forty-four. There was gray coming in over her left ear that she had done nothing about, and there were lines at the corners of her eyes from years of looking at things in full sun. On a billboard we take those out first. When she smiled, I looked at them more than at anything else in the bar. I had specified against all of it. Sitting there, I could not have told you what I had against it.

My watch tapped my wrist. *Heart rate 104. No workout detected.*

I put my hand in my lap.

I did not want her to read it. At 8:31 she had corrected my card in pencil. She had been right about every line she touched. I did not know what she would do with my pulse.

“Tell me about the widow,” I said.

“What widow?”

“You were late because of a widow and a recliner.”

She looked at me for a second before she answered. I do not think she had expected me to remember.

Then she told me. A two-bedroom in Daly City, forty-one years in it, going down to 640 square feet near a daughter in Elk Grove. The recliner was 38 inches wide and needed two feet behind it. It would fit if the widow gave up her dresser. The daughter wanted the chair gone.

“The daughter signs the check,” I said.

“The daughter signs the check. The mother owns the chair.”

“So which one is the client?”

“Both of them.” She picked up a shrimp tail and put it down again. “Whoever owns the thing is in the room when it gets decided. It's in my contract.”

“And what happened to the chair?”

“Rode to Elk Grove. The dresser went with me.” She turned her glass a quarter turn. “There was thirty-one hundred dollars in her husband's coat.”

“It was in his coat.”

“Inside pocket, in a bank envelope. He'd told her for years they couldn't afford a cruise.”

“What did you do with it?”

“Counted it twice, called a witness, and put it in her hand.”

“You gave it back.”

She frowned at me. It was the first time that night she had not followed something I had said.

“Back to who? It was hers.”

I ran it anyway. I cannot not. A dead man's coat. An envelope nobody living knew about. No camera, and a crew that works for her. The odds that anyone ever asks her about that money are zero. I know a great many people, several of them on my board, and I could not name five who would have skipped the calculation.

She had not skipped it. It had never loaded.

My watch tapped me again. I took it off and put it in my pocket.

I did not want it reporting on me while she talked. It had done it twice. I could guess the number without it.

“Does that happen often?” I said. “I mean the money.”

“Coats. Freezers. Bibles. Once it was taped inside a closet door frame.” She shrugged. “You get so you can feel it in a house.”

“And you have never kept any.”

“I don't take anything home from a job. Not a pencil.” She touched the one behind her ear. “This one's mine.”

She had put a second twenty on the table by then, and later a third. I have a tab at that bar that my assistant settles by the quarter. Jo Silveira paid cash for every round and left the change every time.

“What do the others do?” she said. “At minute forty-five.”

“They do not know it is minute forty-five.”

“The last one. What did she do?”

“She asked whether I would consider rehoming my dog.”

“On a first date.”

“She asked at minute thirty-one. I asked for the check at thirty-two. It is the only one I have ended early.”

“You have a dog?”

“His name is Pancake. He is a cream golden retriever, and he is eleven. My daughter named him when she was seven, over my objection.”

I did not tell her that he can no longer manage the last three steps to my door, or that I carry him up them every night. I have not told anyone that.

“I'm not good with dogs,” Jo said. “I got bit on a job when I was twenty-six. I'd want him on the other side of a railing from me, and I'd tell you so.”

I waited for the rest of it. I had heard the rest at minute thirty-one.

“Then you would have asked the same thing.”

“No.” She said it flat. “He was there first. You go stand where the dog isn't.”

The woman at minute thirty-one had told me she loved dogs. She had two. She wanted mine gone because he is old and he sheds.

This one was afraid of them, and she had just given him the house.

I had nothing in fourteen years to file that under.

“The things you sent your mother,” she said. “What were they?”

“I do not know. They were chosen.”

“By who?”

“Someone good at it chose them.”

“Where are they now?”

“They are in her house, I assume.”

“You assume.”

“I have not been in it.”

“Since October.”

“I have not been in it since October.”

She did not say anything kind. I was grateful. People say kind things to me and then I have to do something with them.

“I can tell you what's in it,” she said. “I've never seen it, and I can tell you. A drawer of bread ties and rubber bands. Every card that ever came with your name on it. And one dish.”

“There is a dish.”

“Every family has one.”

“Why the dish and not the silver?”

“Silver stays in a drawer. The dish came to every Thanksgiving.”

“There is nobody to fight. There is only me.”

“Then you'll have to pick it up yourself.”

I went through who I could send. My assistant would have a firm by nine the next morning. It would be a good one. It would pack a kitchen in an afternoon. I tried to think what I would tell it to keep. I had lived in that house for eighteen years and eaten out of that kitchen most nights of them. I could not name one dish.

I looked at the card. I squared it to the edge of the table. She watched me do it.

“That house needs clearing,” she said. “Seven months empty, something's leaking and nobody's there to see it. Call my office.”

“Is that an invitation?”

“It's a job. I don't date, and this wasn't one. Somebody has to go through that house, and whoever does, you're in the room for it.”

“Noted.”

“Three,” she said.

She was smiling when she said it. I had been waiting most of an hour to see that again, and I had not known I was waiting.

“The daughter who named the dog,” she said. “How old is she now?”

“She is eighteen. Her name is Abby. She got into Cal Poly for architecture. She came off the waitlist.”

I had said that sentence four times that week, once in a board meeting. At that table my eyes filled. I felt it start and could not stop it, the way you cannot stop a sneeze.

“It is the horseradish,” I said.

“Sure it is.”

She pulled the napkin out from under her beer and passed it across. She did not look away and she did not look harder. She waited until I had used it.

The four times before, somebody had said congratulations. I had said thank you, and we had gone on to the next thing. Jo had asked about the girl who named the dog. Abby was seven when she named him. From seven to eighteen was eleven years. I could have told Jo what the company did in every one of them.

“What does she want to build?”

I opened my mouth. My daughter spent four months drawing a portfolio, and I had not seen a page of it.

“I do not know.”

“Ask her before she goes,” Jo said. “They go fast.”

She was leaving for San Luis Obispo in the middle of September. That was four months off. I had assumed that when the drawings were finished, somebody would show them to me.

“How old are you?” she said.

“I am forty-one.”

She did the subtraction faster than I would have liked. “You were twenty-three.”

“Yes.”

“You were a kid yourself.”

“I was married, and I had a company with four people in it. I thought I was behind.”

“I was thirty-five when I had mine,” she said. “I had three trucks by then, and I kept meaning to get to it.”

“That averages to twenty-nine.”

She looked at me to see whether I was joking. I was not. She laughed anyway.

The laugh was low. It went on in her shoulders after the sound had stopped. My ears went hot. I went back over the sentence and could not find the joke in it. I wanted her to do it again. I did not know what had caused it, so I had no way to repeat it.

The bartender came to the table with a telephone on a tray.

“A Ms. Louie for you, Mr. Brennan. She said she would hold.”

I took it. “Janice.”

“Calvin. Are you hurt?”

“No.”

“Your car has been at the curb for an hour and forty minutes. Remy thinks you've been taken.”

“I am having a drink.”

There was a pause. Janice has worked for me for seventeen years, and I have heard her pause perhaps four times.

“A second drink?”

“It is my third.”

“With the same woman.”

“Yes.”

She paused again. That made six.

“Then listen to me. When she leaves, you walk her to her car. All the way to it. You ask her to text when she gets home. And you do not say 'noted.'”

“She is counting those already.”

“Good for her,” Janice said, and hung up.

I would not have thought of her car. At minute forty-five my car comes for me. I had never stayed at a table long enough to see how anyone else got home. I put the receiver back on the tray.

Jo was looking at the phone. “Is that who crosses her sevens?”

“Yes.”

“What did she want?”

“I am to walk you to your car.”

“It's a truck.”

The bar closed at eleven. I had not known that. I had never been in it past 8:45.

She stood up before I did. She had a six a.m. load in Pacifica, she said, and she put the pencil in her purse and was halfway to the door while I was still finding my jacket. I did not want her to go. I noted it, which she would have counted.

The truck was on Minna Street in a yellow zone, white, with a steel rack over the bed. The door said SILVEIRA MOVING & ESTATE, and under that SOUTH SAN FRANCISCO SINCE 1987, a license number and a telephone number.

“That is your name,” I said.

“It's my father's. He handed me the keys and the work.”

She got her keys out. We were standing closer than the table had let us. The top of her head came to my collarbone. I knew how tall she was to the quarter inch, because I had put my hand on her hair to find out, and I could still feel it in my palm.

I wanted to put my hand back where it had been. I put it in my pocket instead, on the watch.

“I am supposed to ask you to text when you are home.”

“I sent my sister your name before I walked in, and where I'd be.”

“Why did you?”

“Because you're a man I'd never met.” She said it without heat. “She didn't need the picture. She drives under it.”

Her sister had known my name and the place since before Jo walked in. It was a sound procedure. I had come with a car at minute forty-five and a call that would get me out. I had not thought of myself as the risk at that table.

“Will you text?”

“I don't have your number.”

“I can have it sent to you.”

“I bet you can.” She opened the door of the truck. “Call my office about the house. The number's right there.”

She put out her hand. The cut on her thumb had a strip of green tape over it now. I held on longer than I had the first time, and this time I knew I was doing it.

“Good night, Cal,” she said.

She had not used my name all night. She left first. I stood in an alley behind a museum and watched a truck with her father's name on it go around the corner.

I did not go to the car. It had been out front since 8:45. Getting into it would end the evening. I stood in the alley instead. I do not know for how long. I did not look.

I read the fourth line twice. Remy has fourteen years of me. It has every dinner, every flight, every night I slept under five hours. It had gone looking for another evening like this one and come back with nothing.

“Cancel the introductions,” I said.

“All three?” Remy has a pleasant voice. We tested forty.

“Cancel all three.”

“Done. Dawn Pruitt's office will be told in the morning.”

Each of the three matched all twelve lines I had approved. I did not ask for their names.

“Book an estimate with Silveira Moving and Estate in South San Francisco. It is an estate clearing at my mother's house in Sunnyvale. Take the first opening they have.”

“Booked. Tomorrow at 7:00 a.m., at the house. I gave them your name and the address. Their office runs Remy Starter. It confirmed at 11:52.”

I had not been inside that house in seven months. I had just agreed to stand in front of it in seven hours.

I had asked for the first opening because it was the soonest I could see her again. I pictured the walk up to my mother's front door and got as far as the door. My mouth went dry. I put it down to the third drink.

I took her card out of my shirt pocket. There were two lines on the back in carpenter's pencil, both struck. I tried the second one with my thumb. It stayed.

The phone rang. It was Janice.

“Well?” she said.

The reservoir was going by on the right, flat and black.

“Eleven of the twelve are wrong,” I said. “When is she free?”
