## Chapter 31

**Jo**

On Sunday morning I was on my back under my own kitchen sink with the trap off and a flashlight in my teeth. I heard the deadbolt. Then I heard heels on my floor.

Denise is the only one with a key. She's dressed by six on Sundays too.

“Is that the DISPOSAL?”

I took the flashlight out of my mouth. “It's the trap.”

“Why is your trap OFF?”

The sea glass Cal picked up out of a wave at Carmel had sat on the sill over the sink since that Sunday. On Wednesday morning, when I left for Westlake, it was there, black against the window. On Thursday morning it was gone. Friday night I took the trap off with a bucket under it and found a twist tie and a week of coffee grounds. I put it back. This morning I took it off again, in case. I find things in other people's houses for a living. I'd been through my own kitchen three times.

“I dropped something,” I said.

Denise put two coffees on the counter, one on either side of my mortgage statement, which was face down. She looked at Cal's fleece on the back of the kitchen chair, where it had been since Wednesday. Then she looked at me on the floor.

“Get up. Wash your hands.”

I got up and washed them. She stood behind me with a little white box from See's in her hand.

“Turn around.”

“Denise.”

“Joanna Marie. Turn AROUND.”

I turned around. She opened the box.

My glass was in it, on a square of cotton, wrapped in copper wire. The wire went around it four times and came up into a loop at the top, with a thin chain through the loop. The wrap was lumpy. It was tight. I pushed the glass with my thumb, and it didn't move.

“You took it off my sill.”

“Thursday. I came by for the Westlake folder you left on the counter. You'd have said NO.” She held up the chain by both ends. “Rob's needle-nose. Twenty minutes of a girl on YouTube. I ruined two of the craft-store ones first.”

I opened my mouth.

“You don't keep a pencil from a job,” she said. “So I know where THIS came from.”

She put it over my head and lifted my hair out from under the chain with both hands. When she was nine she used to do my hair for Mass, because she'd decided it was her job. Her fingers were cold from the coffee. The glass came down on my breastbone, over my T-shirt.

“Mom wore her Saint Christopher till the chain turned her neck green,” Denise said. “In the SHOWER.”

I put my hand flat over the glass. It was cold. Then it wasn't.

Denise watched my face. Hers was doing everything.

“Vili says you made him a SANDWICH.”

“I made four.”

“You made the CLIENT lunch.”

“He's not the client.”

His name was on that contract, on the line under my father's.

Denise didn't say a word. She drank her coffee, looking at the fleece.

Her phone went. She looked at it and answered.

“The BIG one,” she said. “With the handle.” She listened. “Yes. And grapes.” She put it face down by mine. “Costco. He'll call twice more before the registers.”

“Go home,” I said. “It's Sunday.”

“I'm going.” She took her coffee and left mine. At the door she turned around. “What did he DO?”

“Thank you for the necklace.”

She looked at me a second longer. Then she went.

I put the trap back on and ran the water to check it. It held. I put the glass inside my shirt.

***

Kevin's sedan pulled up on Sunday afternoon on the dot. It always does. He looked up the street and down it before he let Owen out.

Owen came up the walk with his backpack on one shoulder and his phone already out.

“Fourteen,” he said. “Wednesday night. Dad saw nine. He kept checking his email.”

“I saw one.”

“I know. It was a good one. It had green.” He sat down on the front step to show me his. They were dots. “Did Cal see it?”

“I don't know.”

“You didn't send it to him?”

“No.”

“He gets up at five. He'd have been out with Pancake.” He was already working it out. “From Woodside, northeast is over the bay. He'd have a better sky than you. Can I send it to him? From your phone.”

If it came from my phone, it came from me.

“No,” I said.

His face came down like a roll-up door, from the top. He didn't argue. He looked at the dots on his screen and didn't say anything. He gets that from his father. I've watched for it in him since he could walk.

At the volcano, when he got like this, Cal had sat down beside him and waited. I sat down on the step next to my son and put my hand on his back.

A man went by with a stroller. The sprinkler came on across the street. Owen's back went up and down under my hand. The screen went dark in his hand. He didn't touch it awake.

It took eleven minutes.

“Pancake's eleven,” he said.

“I know.”

“Golden retrievers live ten to twelve years. I looked it up.”

“He's fine.”

“You don't know that.”

I didn't.

He got up and went in and hung his backpack on its hook.

***

When Owen was asleep I got into bed with my phone.

On the first of September I wasn't sending the bank three thousand nine hundred ninety-one dollars and eighty-one cents. It was in my checking account anyway, where it sits the last two weeks of every month, waiting to go.

I opened my credit union's app and opened a savings account. It took four minutes. It wanted a security question. I picked my mother's maiden name and typed it in.

It asked what I wanted to call the account. I typed HOUSE.

I moved the three thousand nine hundred ninety-one dollars and eighty-one cents into it. It asked whether I wanted to make that a repeating transfer. I said the first of every month. It asked me until when. There was a little calendar. I scrolled it forward to November 2036.

Confetti came down the screen. *You're all set!*

In July I'd sat on a slab in his mother's hall and watched him open a hundred and fifty-six envelopes. Thirty twenties, a ten and two ones. *Calvin, house.* She'd done hers with a pen on the first of the month for thirteen years. I did mine in bed in four minutes, with my thumb.

I put the phone face down on the nightstand and turned off the lamp. The glass was warm under my shirt.

***

On Monday after lunch a gray Volvo wagon came in through my gate and parked in the one spot in the yard where nobody would ever back a truck into it.

It had been washed that morning. Nothing on it had ever been dinged. On the hill behind it, in white letters taller than a house, it said SOUTH SAN FRANCISCO THE INDUSTRIAL CITY.

I was on the dock by myself pulling pads for Tuesday. Pads come off the shelf in twelves and go on the truck in twelves, and you check every one for tears before it goes, because a torn pad on a dresser is a claim. I had three dozen across the dock in stacks. My shoulders were warm from it. It was the first hour since Wednesday I hadn't thought about my bank.

The forty-eight from that Thursday were on the second shelf. Thirds, then half. Every edge lined up. I'd watched them get folded through the office window, my father calling it, and turned away from the glass. I took a dozen off the top. Not one of them had a tear.

A woman got out of the Volvo and looked up at the sign before she looked at anything else. White shirt, sleeves turned back twice. I knew her from the end of a table in Menlo Park. She'd set me a place.

She came across the yard in flat leather shoes that were wrong for a yard. She knew they were wrong. She'd picked the flattest ones she had.

“Ms. Silveira,” she said, at the bottom of the dock steps. “I'm Stephanie. Abby's mother. I'm sorry to come without calling. Do you have a few minutes?”

Behind me the office window had Denise's face in it.

“Office,” I said.

Denise was at her desk with the payroll tote, pretending.

“Denise. Lunch.”

“I ATE.”

“Eat again.”

Denise looked at Stephanie, and at me. She took her purse and went out slow, and left the door open an inch. I shut it.

Stephanie didn't sit. I didn't either. My desk was between us with the week's work orders on it.

“Abby leaves tomorrow morning at seven,” she said. “Her father's driving her. She asked him to.”

I waited.

“She told me on Saturday that he paid off your house.”

The glass was under my shirt. I kept my hands on the desk.

“I was married to him for five years,” she said. “I've watched the rest since. There have been eleven. Around the second month there's a weekend in Napa. Around the fourth there's something very large. Then she writes him a thank-you. Every one of them did. Somebody in his office answers it, and that's the end. Nobody fights. He's on to the next thing on his calendar.”

Every answer I'd had for him since the pads started with thank you. I hadn't sent one.

She shifted the bag on her arm.

“You're thinking they'd stay for the money. They have their own. Careers. Houses they paid for themselves. That's who the matchmaker finds him. Nobody stays for a present, Ms. Silveira. Not one that's the last thing he gives you.” She looked at me. “Abby liked two of them. Neither one said goodbye to her.”

She said it in order, without any heat. She'd had it ready a long time.

Around the fourth. We'd had a beer at the Palace on the seventh of May. The wire went in on the tenth of August. *He always gives a present before the end,* Janice had said, with her tape gun on the desk.

He'd never taken me to Napa. He'd taken me to a dog park in Los Gatos. I'd driven him to Carmel in my truck.

“If it's going to end,” she said, “I'd like it ended before she goes. I don't want her in a dorm in San Luis Obispo, waiting to find out. I'm asking you to let it end the way the others did. Quietly.”

She took a manila envelope out of her bag and set it on my desk. She squared it to the edge. I'd seen her daughter do that, and her daughter's father.

“And if it doesn't end,” she said. “When I married him, nobody asked me to sign anything. There was nothing to sign. He's known you three months. He paid off your house. The next thing he pays for is a ring.”

At *ring* my stomach went over the hill. It always does, with him. I didn't want it doing that for a word out of her mouth. I put my hand flat on the envelope to keep it still.

I opened the envelope. It was forty pages, stapled at the corner, with tabs. PREMARITAL AGREEMENT across the top, and his name, and a blank where mine went.

“I had it drawn in July,” she said. “After Abby's party. My lawyer had most of his side from 2012.”

I read it standing up.

I read every contract that crosses this desk. You read the parts in capitals first, and then you go back for the rest. She stood on the other side of the desk the whole time with her bag on her arm. She didn't look at her phone once. I've had clients who couldn't stand still for one box.

Everything he had before the wedding was his. So was everything he made after it. If it ended there was no support, and on page eleven I WAIVED it, in capitals. If he died I got nothing, and a trust with Abby's name on it stayed whole. I looked for Owen. He wasn't in it anywhere. He didn't need to be. He's mine.

It was the Millbrae wife's morning, written up by the other side. A woman who married that man would walk out with what she walked in with. I'd been planning on that since I was seven.

Schedule A was his. Four pages. Recall Technologies, a number of shares with more commas in it than I wanted to read twice. A house in Woodside. A house on Maui. A trust holding a house on West Remington Drive in Sunnyvale. I'd emptied most of that one.

Schedule B was one page. It said *Separate Property of* and my name, and under it one line, blank.

“That's for you to fill in,” Stephanie said.

I took the pencil from behind my ear.

“It ought to be in ink,” she said.

“This won't come off.”

I printed it on the line. *The house in San Bruno, and what I owe on it. Silveira Moving & Estate, three trucks, my father's name on the doors since 1987.* It didn't fit. It went off the end of the line and down the margin.

I turned it around so she could read it. She read it twice. She didn't ask me about the middle of it.

I put the forty pages back in the envelope without signing anything and held it out to her across the desk.

She looked at it. She didn't take it yet.

“You agree with it,” she said.

“Every page.”

“Then sign it.”

“Nobody's asked me anything,” I said. “If he does, he can bring it himself.”

She took it then. She held it against her chest, the way I carry a clipboard.

“Abby says your son ranks dogs,” she said. “Is he ranking Cal?”

“He asked me yesterday how long golden retrievers live.”

She looked at me for a while.

“Will you let it end?”

“I'll talk to him,” I said. “Not tomorrow at seven.” I could have stopped there. “You drove up from Menlo Park for your kid. I'd have done it.”

“He'll let you go now,” she said. “He always does.”

“He hasn't.”

That stopped her.

“He was at his mother's house on Saturday before my truck was,” I said. “He'll be there this Saturday.”

“How do you know?”

“It's on the contract.”

I took a roll of green tape off the corner of the desk and held it out.

“Give her this,” I said. “Tell her good luck from me.”

Stephanie looked at the tape a long time. Then she took it.

“She clipped your tape measure to her pocket at the party,” she said. “She wore it till everybody went home.”

She went to the door and put her hand on it. Then she turned around.

“I told him once that I was lonely,” she said. “By Monday he'd ‘fixed’ it.”

She went down the dock steps. Denise was sitting on the bottom one, with no lunch. Stephanie told her good afternoon. The Volvo went out through my gate without touching anything.

***

When the Volvo was gone I sat in my pickup with the door shut and called Dawn Pruitt.

I'd never called her. In eleven years she'd always called me.

She picked up on the first ring. I heard a cup go down on something hard.

“Joanna.”

“If one person signs a prenup and the other one doesn't,” I said, “does it hold?”

She didn't answer right away. I heard her pour.

“No,” she said. “You both sign it. Then the state makes you sit with it a week.” She drank. “Who brought you a prenup?”

“His ex-wife.”

“Stephanie.” She said it like a case name. “Did he ask you to marry him?”

“No.”

“Then what is Stephanie doing with a prenup?”

“She had it drawn in July. In case.”

Dawn laughed, once, low.

“I drew those for fifteen years,” she said. “I had a mother in Los Altos pay for her son's. Came in by herself with her checkbook. He didn't know she was there. Did you read it?”

“All forty pages.”

“Of course you did. And?”

“It's fair.”

“To who?”

“His daughter.”

Dawn didn't say anything for a second.

“That's who it's for,” she said. “She's a good mother, Joanna. That's all that is. Did she ask you to let it end?”

My hand went to the glass under my shirt. “How'd you know that?”

“It's what I'd have asked, if I were Abby's mother and I had his file.” She set the cup down. “I talked to him Saturday night.”

I had the wheel in my other hand. I held on to it.

“I'm not going to tell you what he said,” Dawn said.

“I didn't ask.”

“You were going to.”

Out the windshield Manny was carrying flat wardrobe boxes up the dock steps, two at a time. He'd found the lightest job in the yard. He always does.

“I call the women at the end of a file,” Dawn said. “I ask one thing.”

“Is that what this is?”

“You called me.” I heard her pick up a pen. “But I'll ask. How was it?”

“It's not over.”

It came out of me the way the meteor came over the yard. No warning, and the whole way across.

“That isn't what I asked you,” Dawn said.

“It's the first thing.”

I heard the pen go.

“Then that's what I'll write down,” she said. I heard her put the pen down. “You know how my marriage ended?”

“No.”

“The dishwasher.”

I waited.

“Every night for a year we talked about the dishwasher. Whether he was loading it wrong. I had the real conversation all worked out. I was getting it right first.” She laughed, not much. “I got it right in March. He moved out in February.”

I had my thumb on the glass through my shirt. I kept it there.

“When I was practicing, I sent you some of my clients to move,” she said. “Wives, mostly. The week the papers were filed.”

“The one in Hillsborough with the bracelets. She wouldn't let my guys carry the shoebox.”

“That one,” Dawn said. “She was fine. Mad, but fine. California gives them half.” She poured. “I had one in Atherton. Thirty-one years. I asked her what she wanted out of it, and she asked me what he was offering.”

I didn't say anything.

“I asked her again. She said she'd have to think about it.” There was nothing on the line for a second. “She never did tell me. I don't think she knew anymore.”

“The Millbrae wife got half,” I said. “Half went under a tarp in her sister's garage.”

“What did she tell you she wanted?”

“She put her hand on everything that was hers.”

“That's what she had, Joanna. I asked what she wanted.”

*In eleven years you asked me for one thing,* Kevin had said at his car in July. *It was to sign.*

“You don't have to call him tonight,” Dawn said. Her voice had gone down. “Or this week. Nobody gets to tell you when. Not Stephanie. Not me.”

“I wasn't going to.”

“You could text him two words,” Dawn said. “‘Not yet.’ Then he'd know you're still there.”

“Then he'd send me something back.”

“He might.”

I looked down at my phone. I didn't pick it up.

If I called him, I didn't know which word would come out first. On the rock I'd had one in my mouth. In his office I'd had it again, behind all the rest, and I'd shut my mouth on it.

Dawn poured. Half a cup.

“Has he stopped calling you?” she said.

“No.”

I said it before she'd finished asking. Then I heard it.

My phone was in my lap. I didn't have to open his thread. I knew what the last thing in it was, and whose.

Dawn didn't say anything. She let me sit there with it, the way I'd sat in my truck for ten minutes in her mother's driveway while she cried in the garage.

“I've got a truck coming in,” I said.

“Go get your truck, Joanna.”

She hung up.

***

At the end of the day my father backed the sixteen-footer to the dock in one pass and sat in the cab with the engine off.

He got down sideways. Both hands on the door, his good leg first. He stood holding the mirror until the knee would take him. He'd done Daly City and back twice.

I had his cushion on his chair already. He came across the dock and sat on it.

“Denise says a Volvo came,” he said.

“His ex-wife.”

“Hm.”

He took his cap off and looked inside it and put it back on.

“I'll drive tomorrow,” I said. “You stay on the dock.”

“I've got it,” he said.

It's what I say. I said it to Cal over a beer the first night, and on this dock the Thursday he came about the truck. I'd never had it said to me.

I sat down on the edge of the dock next to his chair with my boots hanging over. The four o'clock wind was still coming over the hill. It lifted the corners of the pads on the stack.

“You ever let anybody take the heavy end?” he said.

“You taught me to take it.”

“I taught you how.” He looked at the white truck by the back fence with its hood still up. “I never told you to take it every time.”

I picked a pad off the stack and folded it in thirds.

“After your mother,” he said, “I didn't talk to you till July.”

“I know, Dad.”

“That was too long.”

In seven years he'd never said one word about it. He didn't look at me when he said it.

My throat had gone tight. I looked at my boots.

“Anybody can carry a gun safe down the stairs,” he said. “Once, with everybody watching. They tip you.” He rubbed the knee with the heel of his hand. “It's boxes that do your knees. Forty years of boxes. Nobody watches you carry a box.”

He nodded at the second shelf.

“That fella folded with me the other Thursday,” he said. “Forty-eight. I didn't have to fix one.”

Then he put his hand out to me, palm up.

I didn't know what he wanted. He'd never held his hand out to me for anything but keys.

I took it and pulled. He came up out of the chair on it, all his weight, and stood. He let go when the knee would take him.

“Five-thirty,” he said, and went to his truck.

I sat on the dock with my boots over the edge.

I hadn't said a word to Cal since Wednesday. That was six days.

Kevin's longest was Tuesday to Sunday. Six.

***

By the time I'd done the dishes Owen was asleep, with his door open a foot.

I sat at my counter with the light over the stove on and nothing else. The fleece was on the back of the chair. I put it on. It still smelled like his dog and the cold at his gate. I wanted him at my counter so bad my stomach hurt.

On the terrace at Ventana he'd turned his hand over under mine and held on, and looked at me, and waited. My face went hot. I passed him the bread.

Kevin used to pass me the salt.

I took my phone into the bedroom. I didn't open his thread. I plugged it in on the nightstand face up and turned the ringer all the way up.

I lay down in his fleece with the glass in my fist and left the lamp on.

Sometime after two the phone went off on the nightstand. It said CAL.

I had it before the second ring.

“It is Pancake,” he said. His voice was wrong. “I did not know who else to call.”

“Where are you.”
