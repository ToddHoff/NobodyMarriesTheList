## Chapter 25

**Jo**

I'd turned my phone face down on his mother's floor so I'd stop looking at myself laughing.

It didn't help. I'd looked at it for about a minute. I could still see all of it. My head was back. My mouth was open. My shoulder was against his arm. He was looking at me, not at the girl with the phone. Nobody takes my picture unless I'm carrying something, a box or Owen. In this one my hands were empty.

I'd have paid for a print.

Under it were two words. *Brennan's mover.*

The slab was still cool under my back. Cal's head was against mine the wrong way round, his feet toward the kitchen and mine toward the bedrooms. The dog was snoring in the closet where the boxes had been. Cal's mouth was in my hair. I could feel him breathe in it. I'd been lying on his mother's floor for twenty minutes wanting to roll over and put my mouth on his neck. I still wanted to.

“I know who to call,” he said. “It could be gone by midnight. Do you want that?”

He was holding still to ask it.

“No,” I said.

“It is your face, on a page that sells pictures of me.”

“It's a good picture.”

“It is a very good picture. Then what is wrong with it?”

“Read me the two words.”

“Brennan's mover,” he said.

“I've been Silveira's mover for twenty years.”

He didn't say anything for a while. Out in the driveway the sixteen-footer had my father's name on both doors. My father painted his name on his first truck himself in 1987, on a Sunday, with a brush. I pay for the paint now.

“I can have them take the caption down,” he said.

“And put up what?”

He thought about it.

“They would put up whatever you told me to,” he said.

“On their page.”

“Then I would like the whole thing gone.”

“I know you would.”

I knew why, too. In June I'd written line six on his card myself and struck it. *PRIVATE. NO SOCIAL MEDIA.* A woman he'd been seeing had sold the story of his custody hearing, Abby's school and all. Abby was four. Now there was a picture of me with my head back, laughing, on a page that sells him.

I didn't say any of that. It was his.

“Tell me what you can stop,” I said.

“I can have that page take it down. I cannot stop the copies. There are copies already.” He was quiet a second. “By morning people will have found your company from it. I cannot stop that.”

“The company page is mine.”

“Yes.”

“Don't touch it.”

“I will not touch anything of yours without asking you first.”

He'd said that once already, in July, at his own sink, about money. He'd kept it. On Saturday he'd asked me about air.

His hand came across the slab and found mine without looking. His fingers were cold. I held on to them.

The picture was still up, with the two words under it. His hand stayed tight on mine.

At eleven I sat up.

“I've got Millbrae at seven,” I said.

I put the phone in my back pocket without turning it over.

***

Denise had two coffees and two hundred and six reviews at ten past six on Wednesday.

She was on the office steps in her good shoes with her phone out in front of her, the way you'd hold a dish somebody else cooked. The fog was down to the tops of the trucks. My father's pickup was parked by the fence. He doesn't come in on Wednesdays.

“We had four hundred and thirty-one yesterday,” Denise said. “Four hundred and thirty-one, EVER. We got two hundred and six since last night.”

I took my coffee.

“Read them to me.”

“You don't want me to read them to you.”

“Read me three.”

She scrolled. “‘Five stars. Get it, girl.’ That's Kimberly from Rob's office. We never moved her. ‘One star. Gold digger.’ ‘One star. She'll be gone by Christmas.’” She scrolled again. “‘Five stars. Jo and her team made our move absolutely seamless, from the initial consultation to the final box.’ Who had a CONSULTATION?”

“Nobody. They get an estimate.”

“Is she lying? Or is she a ROBOT?”

“I don't know.”

Some of them read like people I could have moved. I couldn't tell which.

I looked at the street. There was nobody on it, no van, no camera. Denise had already looked twice.

The white truck was up against the back fence where the tow had left it Monday, hood up. I'd called the shop at seven on Tuesday. Seventy-six hundred dollars and three weeks for the part. That's four months of what I pay extra on the house. Denise would have the invoice by Friday. What it did to my house was mine.

The keys to the sixteen-footer weren't on the board. My father was in the cab of it with the window down and the tide book on the dash, reading the tides like he was going sailing instead of to Burlingame with Manny.

Burlingame was a four-bedroom. On the sixteen-footer that's three loads. Somebody has to drive the truck back and forth all day while the crew keeps packing. Vili had the big truck in Hillsborough. That was a driver I didn't have.

I should have walked over there and told him to go home. His knees had done the gangway on Monday. He'd come in at five-thirty so I wouldn't have to ask him. By the time I got there he had the keys. I drank my coffee and let him sit there.

My phone went at 6:21. Denise saw the name before I did. Her eyebrows went up into her hair.

“Good morning,” Cal said. “Remy can report the reviews that were not written by customers. Most of them would be gone by tomorrow. Do you want that?”

“No.”

“May I ask why?”

“Everybody who writes about us gets an answer from me. I sign it.”

“There are two hundred and six.”

“Then I'm behind.”

He was quiet for a second.

“Understood,” he said.

I hung up. Denise was looking at me with her whole face.

“He ASKED,” she said. “Cal Brennan called you at six in the morning to ask.”

“Go do payroll.”

“It's Wednesday.”

“Then go do Friday's.”

She went up the steps with her phone and her coffee and didn't stop reading till the door shut.

***

The house in Millbrae belonged to a man who'd died in May. His son had flown down from Spokane for the week to empty it. He'd told me on the phone that his father didn't have much. His father had a den with a fold-out couch and four file cabinets.

My two guys had the bedrooms. The son sat at the kitchen table with the found sheet. I did the den by myself, every drawer, front to back, and brought him what I found one thing at a time. Bank envelopes in a tackle box. A Christmas card from 1994 with a twenty still in it. Thirty years of property tax bills, paid, in order, with the stubs stapled. He called them, and I wrote them down. I brought him a drawer of rubber bands somebody had saved for forty years. He looked at it a long time.

“Blue,” he said.

My phone buzzed on my hip with every new review, every couple of minutes. After an hour I took it out and put it face down on his desk. It buzzed against the wood instead.

I worked till the den was four stacks and a swept floor. When the cabinets were empty I pulled each drawer all the way out and looked behind it, because once in Burlingame there was cash taped to the back of the bottom one. There was nothing. I put the drawers back. I like a room right before I tape the door, when it's down to what goes and what stays and nobody's arguing.

At 3:41 the phone buzzed different. Kevin.

*Got him at 3:30. A mom at camp asked Owen if you're dating Cal Brennan. He told her Cal's his friend so he's allowed to ask him things. Then he told her about the volcano. I didn't say anything. I can keep him off his phone tonight if you want. Whatever works for you.*

I read it twice.

I sat down on the dead man's fold-out couch with the phone in both hands. A mom at his day camp had asked my son about me at pickup. I didn't know her name. There was no page for her where I could sign mine. Owen had told her the truth and then told her about lava.

*Let him have his phone,* I wrote. *Thanks, Kev.*

I put the phone in my pocket and taped the den door.

***

At 11:58 I was at my kitchen table with my glasses on and three hundred and twelve to go.

By then there were four hundred and three new ones. The office laptop was in front of me with Denise's password on a sticky note under it in her capitals. I'd made a sentence for the ones I couldn't place. *We don't have a record of moving you. If we did, call me at the yard. Jo Silveira, owner.* I'd typed it sixty-something times. The ones I could place I wrote to by name.

The trouble was the ones in between.

*Jo Silveira moved my mother out of her house in San Mateo and it was the most professional experience. The crew wrapped every piece with care. They did not stop for a single break. Highly recommend Silveira Moving & Estate to anyone.*

I've moved a lot of mothers out of San Mateo. I could have moved this one. Except we stop at eleven. Every job, every day. You don't put a man who hasn't eaten since five under a dresser at two.

So that one was nobody. The one under it said Burlingame and a piano and a woman named Carol. I'd moved a Carol in Burlingame with a piano, I was almost sure, in 2021. Or 2019. The one under that was a man who said I'd saved his marriage. I didn't remember his marriage.

At midnight I got up and cleaned the knobs on my stove. All six. They didn't need it. I sat back down.

My phone lit at 12:04.

*Are you awake?*

I looked at it a while. *Yes,* I wrote.

He called.

“How many are left?” he said.

“Three hundred and twelve.”

“How many have you answered?”

“Ninety-one.”

“Read me one.”

I read him the San Mateo mother. He listened to the whole thing.

“A model wrote that,” he said.

“How do you know?”

“It is exactly the right length. It has no mistakes in it. It does not want anything from you.” He thought for a second. “And it is wrong about lunch.”

“I got that one.”

“I know. I could hear you get it.”

I looked at the screen. Millbrae again at seven. My father in the sixteen-footer in the morning because I hadn't told him not to. I couldn't tell the Carol in Burlingame from nobody. I'd been at it six hours. Whichever she was, she was getting my name.

“Can you?” I said.

“Can I do what?”

He was going to make me say the whole thing.

“Tell. All of them. Which ones are people.”

“Yes.”

“Not your robot.”

“No. I would read them.”

“Not anybody you pay.”

“I would not pay anyone.”

“May I come over?” he said.

“Bring nothing.”

I put the phone down on the table.

I'd asked him for something. I hadn't sent him a bill for it. In May I'd billed him two lines for a dinner I wanted. On Monday in my father's driveway Kevin had told me that in eleven years I'd asked him for one thing. Cal had heard it across the roof of the car. I'd heard it myself every day since, at red lights, in a dead man's den. Tonight I'd said can you, at midnight, at my own table, to a man with eleven billion dollars. I'd asked him to read. I sat there and waited for it to cost me something. My face was hot.

One headlight came up my street at 12:31.

I watched him from the front window. He parked behind my pickup, went around to the back door of his car and lifted the dog down off the seat, sixty-four pounds, the way you'd lift a box you've been told is glass. The dog came up my walk and over my front step on his own.

“I said bring nothing,” I said.

“He is not a thing.”

I let them in. The dog went past me into my kitchen like he'd been there before.

Cal sat down at my table and looked at the laptop. Then he pushed it square to the edge of the table before he touched a key.

“The trackpad sticks,” I said. “Use the arrows.”

He used the arrows.

The dog went under the table and lay down on my feet. He was warm all the way through my socks. In May I'd wanted that dog on the other side of a railing from me. I left my feet where they were.

He read on the laptop. I answered on my phone. He'd read one, say “No,” and go to the next, and I'd paste my sentence under it and sign it. Every so often he'd stop and read one twice.

“This one is a person,” he said.

“How do you know?”

“She spelled Silveira wrong. She is angry about a mirror. She wants something.”

He was right. Glenda Ruiz, 2018. We cracked her mirror coming down her stairs. I bought her a new one. She wanted the old one. I answered her by name.

He had my estimate pad next to him. When he hit a person, he wrote the name on it in small square capitals and pushed it across to me. I'd write that one myself and push the pad back.

I watched him more than I watched my phone. He reads with his whole face still and his mouth a little open. He'd shaved at some point today and missed a spot under his jaw on the left. His forearms were on my table. I wanted my hands on them. I wanted to take the glasses off and go around the table and sit in his lap in my own kitchen at one in the morning with three hundred reviews open. I typed my sentence instead, and signed it, and typed it again.

At 1:52 he stopped and didn't say anything.

“What,” I said.

He turned the laptop around.

It was an old review with a new line on top. *Rowena B., Elk Grove. Edited.* Under it were the eleven sentences she'd put up in May, about her mother's recliner and her father's coat. Over them she'd added one more.

*Saw the picture. Silveira Moving is still the only company I would let in my moms house. Good for you Jo.*

The widow with the recliner, the night I was late to meet him. He'd told me about those eleven sentences in May, at the fender of the white truck, the day I found out he'd sent her mother to Alaska and told him next time, ask.

I read it twice. *Silveira Moving,* she'd written. My father's name. She'd spelled it right.

Most of the four hundred and three had his name in them somewhere. I took my glasses off and pressed my thumb and finger into the bridge of my nose until I could see the screen again.

I didn't say anything for a while. Cal left the laptop facing me and waited.

I wrote Rowena back. It took me longer than any of the others.

At 2:20 he stopped on another one and turned it to me without a word.

*Booked for 8. Showed up at 10:40. Nobody called. Everything got there fine. One star for the morning I took off work for nothing. Saw this company in the news and remembered.*

Gary Roth, Pacifica, March. I'd put a two-hour job ahead of him in San Bruno. It ran four. I didn't call him, because I kept thinking we were almost done.

“That one is real,” Cal said.

“I know. I earned it.”

I answered Gary Roth before Cal could go on. *You're right. I put two jobs on that morning and I didn't call you. I should have. Jo Silveira, owner.*

Cal read it upside down on my phone and went back to the laptop.

In the three hundred and twelve there were nine people. He finished at 2:41. The pad had nine names on it in his capitals.

He stood up. The dog came out from under the table and leaned on his leg.

“I walk him at five,” he said. “And you have Millbrae at seven.”

“How long's his walk?”

“It takes forty minutes.”

I walked them out to his car. He lifted the dog back onto the seat and shut the door. Then he looked at me across the roof of the car.

He could have stayed till half past four. I wanted him to stay. I wanted him in my bed till my alarm, with the dog on the floor, and nobody reading anything. I'd already asked him for one thing tonight. I didn't know how many asks I had in me. I wasn't going to find out in my own driveway at a quarter to three.

“You've got one headlight,” I said. “Somebody's going to write you a review.”

“I will answer it myself.”

I watched the one headlight go down the street.

***

The courier came at 12:50 on Thursday. I wasn't there. Denise signed for it.

I got back to the yard at 1:20 for the afternoon load. Denise was on the office steps with a white envelope in both hands. She'd held it up to the window. I could tell by her face.

“From HIM,” she said. “A courier. In a little gray car, with a tablet for me to sign.”

The envelope had my name typed on it. In the corner it said *Office of C. Brennan*.

I'd seen that on Tuesday night. It was on the labels of thirty-four boxes in his mother's closet. The same office had sent her the same cashmere wrap seven times.

I took it to my pickup, got in and shut the door. Denise stood on the steps and watched me through the windshield like it was television.

Fourteen pages, stapled in the corner.

CONFIDENTIALITY AND NON-DISCLOSURE AGREEMENT. Calvin Brennan, the Disclosing Party. Joanna Silveira, the Recipient. It was the first one of these I'd ever seen. My address was on it, my house in San Bruno, and the yard's. The date we met was on it, May 7, and the Pied Piper.

I read contracts. I read every page of every one, because the piano is always in the part nobody reads. I took the pencil from behind my ear and read this one the same way.

I wasn't to say anything about him to anybody. Not about his health, his money, his house, his company, his daughter or his dog. Not to post a word or a picture of him, anywhere, including any business listing. That one was for my company page. I wasn't to say anything about him ever, as long as I lived, whether we were seeing each other or not. If I did, I'd owe him two hundred and fifty thousand dollars for each time.

On page five it said the Recipient would see to it that her employees, agents and family members did the same.

Family members. My father, out there in the sixteen-footer. Denise on the steps. My son, at day camp, who'd told somebody's mother at pickup that Cal was his friend.

Nothing in it asked a thing of him.

I'd sat at my own table at midnight waiting for asking him to cost me something. Here it was, fourteen pages, by courier, with a tablet to sign for it.

At the bottom of every page, in small gray type, it said the same thing.

*Prepared by Remy from 3 prior agreements. Approved by C. Brennan.*

Three. Three women had signed this before me.

I found out one of their names on page nine. Everywhere else it said the Recipient, or Ms. Silveira. On page nine, in the part about giving back anything that belonged to him, it said *Ms. Holcomb shall return all materials within ten days.*

I sat there with the pencil. Ms. Holcomb. She'd had ten days.

I sat in my own truck with Denise watching through the windshield and wondered whether she'd met him at the Pied Piper too, at the same table, for forty-five minutes, and whether she'd been on time.

The fourteen pages were the San Mateo mother again. The right length, clean, sure of themselves. Wrong about my name.

Denise knocked on the window. I rolled it down an inch.

“What is it?”

“Paperwork.”

“From HIM? What kind of paperwork?”

I started the truck.

***

I parked on the same level of his garage where I'd sat for forty minutes in June, nose out.

*Garage,* I wrote. *Level 2.*

He came out of the elevator three minutes later. He was in a gray shirt and no jacket, walking fast. When he saw my face he slowed down.

I got out and let the tailgate down and put the pages on it.

“Did you read this?” I said.

He looked at the first page. Then he looked at the bottom of it, at the gray type. I watched him read the line with his own name in it.

“No,” he said.

He'd been caught. He stood there with it. I liked him for that. My stomach went over the hill, mad as I was.

“Page nine,” I said.

He turned to page nine. He read it standing up, with one hand flat on my tailgate. His ears went red from the top down.

“That is not your name,” he said.

“No.”

“I will have it withdrawn this afternoon. You will not see another page of it.”

“No.”

He looked up.

“Three,” I said. “It says three.”

“Yes.”

He kept his eyes on me.

I put my hand on the pages. I'd had a speech ready since the on-ramp in South City. I left it in the truck.

Every estimate I've ever written has two lines at the bottom, one for the customer and one for me, and nobody on my crew lifts a thing till both are signed. This had one.

All the way down I'd thought I would tear it in half on this tailgate. Standing there, I wanted it fixed. A mom at camp had asked my son about me. A stranger had put his name over mine. His robot had my house and my yard and the night we met. It had put some other woman's name on page nine of me. If there was going to be paper, I wanted his name on it too, where it would cost him the same as me.

“You sent me one of these,” I said.

“I do not want you to sign anything.”

“I want one.”

He waited.

“Two pages,” I said. “Your name in every place mine is. Your people in it the same as my people. Your robot too.”

He looked at me across the tailgate for a long time.

“Mutual,” I said. “Or nothing.”
