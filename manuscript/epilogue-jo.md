## Epilogue

**Jo**

The ring was in the soap dish by the kitchen sink. That's where they always are.

The family had been through the house in March and taken what they wanted. The daughter had gone through the jewelry box on the dresser twice. Nobody looks in the soap dish. A woman takes her ring off at the sink to do the dishes. She puts it back on when she's done. One night she doesn't.

It was a plain gold band, worn thin on the palm side. The house was a ranch in San Mateo. My crew had taken the last load to the yard in the middle of the afternoon. I'd stayed to walk it by myself, every door and every drawer.

I wrote the ring on the found sheet and took a picture of it on the counter. I texted the picture to the daughter, Carol. Then I put the ring in a coin envelope with her name on it. The pencil was down to an inch and a half. I'd been sharpening it with my box knife since the night I met him. That was a year ago tonight.

Every line I'd ever struck on his list had come out of that pencil. The list was on my fridge, under the magnet from the yard. It was the printout he'd mailed me in August, with *I did not write this* across the top in his blue ink and his pencil down the side. Eleven of the twelve had a line through them in my pencil. He'd told me eleven himself, the afternoon we stayed home with Owen, at my table with Owen asleep on the couch: he'd wanted a wife who could leave when he left, so nobody would be at home waiting on him to call.

Twelve was *Says what she wants*. I'd left that one alone.

Under it, in my printing, there was a thirteenth.

*SAYS WHEN HE WAS WRONG.*

I wrote it in February, the week after Owen turned ten. While Owen was at his dad's, Cal went into the dog app Owen wrote himself and made it faster. Owen came home and opened it. He shut it again. Then he quit talking, to all of us, for two days.

On the second night Cal sat down on the hall floor with his back against the wall outside Owen's door. I was at the sink. He talked to the door.

"I changed your app without asking you," he said. "That was wrong. I have put it back the way you wrote it."

For a while the door stayed shut. Then it opened. Owen stood there in his socks with the phone.

"Show me how you made it fast," he said.

Cal got up off the floor and went in. I dried my hands. That night I wrote the thirteenth line under the twelfth.

I locked up and left the keys in the lockbox with Carol's envelope. She was picking them up from the realtor in the morning.

***

On the way to the freeway I stopped at the hardware store for a box of pencils, the flat red ones. The man ahead of me in line was buying one hinge. He had the old one in his other hand so he'd get the same one.

I called Cal.

"I'm at the hardware store," I said. "You need anything?"

"I do not need anything."

I hadn't thought he would. I didn't have anything to ask him. I was in a store.

I stayed on anyway. So did he. Behind him somebody laughed. A glass went down on a table.

"You're there already," I said.

"I am at the table."

"It's at eight."

"I came at seven."

"What's your heart doing?" I said.

"My watch says ninety-four."

"What's it say on a stage?"

"It says sixty-one."

My stomach went over the hill. It always does, with him. I stood in line with a box of pencils and tried to think what a man does for an hour at a table that gets him to ninety-four. Last August Stephanie stood in my office and told me the next thing he paid for would be a ring. I'd had a ring in my hand that afternoon, out of a dead woman's soap dish.

"What are you buying?" he said.

"Pencils."

"How long is the one you have?"

"Inch and a half."

"It was five and a half inches in May."

"You measured my pencil."

"You had me measure you."

I paid and went out to the truck with the phone at my ear. He was still on it. I put him on the speaker and got on 101.

He stayed on all the way up. Neither of us had anything to say. I could hear the bar behind him. He could hear the noise my truck still makes on hills.

***

I put the truck on Minna Street. The curb's a loading zone until six. It was after. The clean shirt stayed in its bag behind the seat. I went in with San Mateo down the front of me.

The Pied Piper was still walking the children out of town over the bar. Cal was at the corner table with his phone at his ear. He stood up when he saw me.

"I'm here," I said into mine, ten feet from him, and hung up.

He put his phone face down by the shrimp. Two of the six were gone.

"You started without me," I said.

"I have been here an hour." His ears were red. "I left you four."

He had on the gray shirt. The gray was coming in over his ears. I wanted my hands in it.

The bartender was there before my chair stopped moving. I asked for whatever was on draft. He said it would go on Mr. Brennan's tab.

"Thanks," I said.

There was a twenty in my purse. It stayed there. Cal looked at my purse. Then he looked at me.

He had Stephanie's envelope on the seat beside him. He put it on the table first. Abby's green tape was still across the flap, with DAD on it in her printing.

I turned to the last page. My printing still ran off the end of the line and down the margin. He hadn't added a word to it. I took the pencil from behind my ear and drew a line through *and what I owe on it*.

He looked at the line a long time. Then he put the forty pages back in the envelope in their order.

I put the pencil back behind my ear. My hand wasn't steady doing it.

He took a card out of his shirt pocket. There were two in there. The other one was the old one with my lines on the back, gone soft at the corners. He laid the new one on the table between us, face up, and squared it to the edge with one finger.

It was his printing, in blue ink.

```
JO SILVEIRA
Silveira Moving & Estate, South S.F. Est. 1987.
Divorced. One son, 10.

ASK: her.
DON'T: say "noted."
```

The seven in 1987 went straight down. Nobody had crossed it.

He'd taken every one of my corrections. Jo, with no question mark after it. *& Estate*, where I'd put it in for him a year ago in pencil. Owen at ten. The bottom of the card was blank.

I read DON'T and laughed out loud. The couple at the next table looked over.

I read ASK again.

"Then ask," I said.

He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the card, where he puts them when he wants them to stay put. He said it in the voice he uses in my kitchen.

"Will you marry me?"

My face went hot from the collar up. I looked for a box. On the table there was his card, Stephanie's envelope, four shrimp and my beer.

Carol's mother had taken her ring off at that sink every night for fifty-one years. That's eighteen thousand six hundred mornings she put it back on.

I took the pencil from behind my ear. At the bottom of the card, where the car used to go, I printed what I wanted, in capitals, so he couldn't read it two ways.

*YES. IN MY DRIVEWAY.*

I turned it around and slid it across to him.

He read it twice. He put his thumb on it and held it there.

He stood up. I got up too.

"Come down here," I said.

He came down. I put my hands in the gray over his ears and kissed him at the corner table. He tasted like horseradish. The couple at the next table looked over again.

We sat down on the same side.

"Chairs off the truck," I said. "Abby squares them up. Denise brings a sheet cake. Dad sits in his chair, with his cushion. Owen times it."

"I did not bring a ring."

"Good. They catch on the straps."

"Which Saturday do you want?"

"Whichever one the sixteen-footer's free."

At a quarter to nine his phone buzzed face down on the table. I had my purse in my lap before I knew I'd moved. He turned it over.

```
OWEN · 8:45 PM
Did you ask her yet
```

I put the purse back on the floor.

"Owen knows?" I said.

"I asked him on Sunday, before Kevin's."

"What'd he say?"

"He asked whether Pancake comes too."

Cal lifts him up my front step every time, front end and then back.

"Then we need a ramp," I said.

"I will ask your father."

He typed his answer with one thumb. I read it over his arm.

*Yes. She wrote it down.*

Owen answered before he'd put it down.

*In pencil?*

I still had the pencil. There wasn't enough left of it to sharpen. I put it in his shirt pocket, with the cards.
