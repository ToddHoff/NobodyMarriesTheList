## Epilogue

**Jo**

On Sunday night Cal dried my roasting pan and put it away where it doesn't go.

"Will you meet me at the Oak on Friday?" he said.

"I've got a house in San Mateo Friday."

"At eight," he said. "At the corner table."

Friday was a year to the night.

"San Mateo'll be done by six," I said.

After he'd gone home I stood at my fridge and read his list. It was the printout he'd mailed me in August. Every line I'd struck on it had come out of the same carpenter's pencil. I'd been sharpening it with my box knife since the night I met him. It was down to an inch and a half.

Eleven of the twelve had a line through them. He'd told me why eleven was there the afternoon we stayed home with Owen. He'd wanted a wife who could leave when he left, so nobody would be at home waiting on him to call.

Twelve was *Says what she wants*. I'd left that one alone.

Under it, in my printing, there was a thirteenth.

*SAYS WHEN HE WAS WRONG.*

I wrote it in February, the week after Owen turned ten. While Owen was at his dad's, Cal went into the dog app Owen wrote himself and made it faster. Owen came home and opened it. He shut it again. Then he went to his room and shut the door.

That night Cal sat down on the hall floor with his back against the wall outside Owen's door. I was at the sink. He talked to the door.

"I changed your app without asking you," he said. "That was wrong. I have put it back the way you wrote it."

For a while the door stayed shut. Then it opened. Owen stood there in his socks with the phone.

"Show me how you made it fast," he said.

Cal got up off the floor and went in. I dried my hands. Then I went to the fridge and wrote the thirteenth line under the twelfth.

***

On Friday I finished a house in San Mateo and stopped at the hardware store on the way to the freeway, for a box of pencils, the flat red ones. In line I called Cal.

"I'm at the hardware store," I said. "You need anything?"

"I do not need anything."

I hadn't thought he would. I didn't have anything to ask him. I was in a store.

I stayed on anyway. So did he. Behind him somebody laughed. A glass went down on a table.

"You're there already," I said.

"I am at the table."

"It's at eight."

"I came at seven."

"What's your heart doing?" I said.

"My watch says ninety-four."

"What's it say on a stage?"

"It says sixty-one."

I knew his ears had gone red. They still do, with me. I stood in line with my pencils and tried to think what a man does for an hour at a table that gets him to ninety-four.

"What are you buying?" he said.

"Pencils."

"How long is the one you have?"

"Inch and a half."

"It was five and a half inches in May."

"You measured my pencil."

"You had me measure you."

I paid and went out to the truck with the phone at my ear. He was still on it. I put him on the speaker and got on 101.

He stayed on all the way up. Neither of us had anything to say. I could hear the bar behind him. He could hear the noise my truck still makes on hills.

At Oyster Point I went under his billboard. CALL YOUR MOTHER, forty-eight feet wide. The rest of him was on my speaker, saying nothing.

On the eighth of October, a year to the day, he'd played his mother's message on my couch. When she said *Calvin*, he said, "Thank you, Mom," into the space she'd left him.

***

I put the truck on Minna Street. The curb's a loading zone until six. It was after.

The oak was still behind the bar.

I stopped inside the door. One tree on a gold hill, with the long limb going out low and setting its elbow down in the grass. A year ago I'd slowed down for it on my way to being late.

It was his tree. I knew that limb.

In July I'd stood under the loving tree in the dark with my palm on the bark and his beside it. Where his little finger touched mine, something had come across from him. I felt it come again in the doorway, in my fingertips, with my hands at my sides and nobody touching me. It came in quiet, the way it had the first time.

Mrs. Hollis and her husband put their hands on the tree the night he proposed. They were married fifty-one years.

My mother believed in signs. I never have. I stood there long enough that the hostess asked me if I had a reservation.

Cal was at the corner table with his phone at his ear. He stood up when he saw me.

"I'm here," I said into mine, ten feet from him, and hung up.

He put his phone face down on the table. At my place there was a shrimp cocktail, six shrimp hung off a glass of ice around a cup of red sauce. Beside it a bottle of champagne sat sweating in a silver bucket.

"Those are yours," he said. "You ate five of mine the first night."

I took one. The sauce went up the back of my nose and cleared out San Mateo.

He had on the gray shirt. The gray was coming in over his ears. I wanted my hands in it.

The bartender was there before my chair stopped moving. I asked for whatever was on draft. He looked at the champagne. Then he said it would go on Mr. Brennan's tab.

"Thanks," I said.

There was a twenty in my purse. It stayed there. Cal looked at my purse. Then he looked at me.

"That's your tree," I said.

He looked over at it, like he'd forgotten it was there.

"It is the loving tree," he said.

"Who painted it?"

"Mrs. Hollis's grandmother, in 1919. It hung in the front hall. Mrs. Hollis left it with the house. She said it went with the tree."

"So what's it doing here?"

"This place needed something for that wall the week it opened. It was the only thing I had that was long enough."

"Who owns this place, Cal?"

He moved his glass a quarter inch.

"I do."

"You never said."

"I own a number of restaurants. I have only been inside this one. I did not think about it until you stopped in the door."

"You named it."

"There was a form. It needed a name by Friday. I looked out my kitchen window."

"Why not the Loving Tree?"

"It was too close to the old Nut Tree."

I laughed into my beer. "You thought about it."

"For about a minute."

He had Stephanie's envelope on the seat beside him. He put it on the table between us. Abby's green tape was still across the flap, with DAD on it in her printing.

Stephanie had put it on my desk last August. *If there's a ring,* she'd said, *it comes with this.* I'd told her that if he ever asked me anything, he could bring it himself.

I looked at it a while before I opened it.

I turned to the last page. My printing still ran off the end of the line and down the margin. He hadn't added a word to it. I got the pencil out of my purse and drew a line through *and what I owe on it*.

He looked at the line a long time. Then he put the forty pages back in the envelope in their order.

He took a card out of his shirt pocket. There were two in there. The other one was the old one, gone soft at the corners. He laid the new one on the table between us, face up, and squared it to the edge with one finger.

It was his printing, in blue ink.

```
JO SILVEIRA
Silveira Moving & Estate
South S.F. Est. 1987.
Divorced. One son, 10.

ASK: her.
DON'T: say "noted."
```

Nobody had crossed the seven in 1987.

He'd taken every one of my corrections. Jo, with no question mark after it. *& Estate*, where I'd put it in for him a year ago in pencil. Owen at ten. The bottom of the card was blank.

I read DON'T and laughed out loud. The couple at the next table looked over.

I read ASK again.

"Then ask," I said.

I looked for a box. On the table there was his card, Stephanie's envelope, the shrimp and my beer.

Cal looked over at the bar and lifted one hand.

The kitchen door swung open. The chef came out in his whites, carrying a plate with a silver dome on it, up in front of him like it was hot. The room went quiet one table at a time. A woman at the bar put her phone down.

He set it on the table in front of me. Cal got up. He came around to my side and went down on one knee on the floor of his own restaurant.

Low man calls the lift.

I held on to the table.

He lifted the dome. There was a ring box on a napkin folded in thirds and then in half, the way I fold a furniture pad. His hands shook getting it open.

I'd put the stone at half an inch across, a soft blue with a little fog in it, like somebody had breathed on it. It was cut square, with the corners rounded off the way a wave rounds them.

It sat low in a plain gold band. The gold came up smooth all the way around it. Down each side, small diamonds were set into the band flush, like rivets. Nothing on it would catch on a strap.

I've wrapped the good jewelry out of a lot of estates. I'd never wrapped anything like it.

I took the glass out from under my shirt and held it next to the ring. It was the same blue, with the same fog. The corners had gone soft the same way.

"Jo Silveira," he said, in the voice he uses in my kitchen. "Will you marry me?"

Behind the bar a man had stopped with a glass halfway to the shelf.

I didn't trust my voice. At the bottom of the card I printed what I wanted in pencil, in capitals, so he couldn't read it two ways.

*YES. IN MY DRIVEWAY.*

I turned it around and held it down to him.

He read it twice. Then he took the ring out of the box and put it on me. It went over my knuckle on the second try. It was heavier than it looked. Good gold is.

Somebody at the bar started clapping. Then the whole room did. The chef was clapping too.

He stood up. I got up too.

"Come down here," I said.

He came down. I put my hands in the gray over his ears and kissed him in front of all of them.

When I let him go he turned around to the room. "Dinner is on the house tonight," he said, in the voice from the billboard. They loved him for it.

He sat down next to me and was the one with the card again.

He took the champagne out of the bucket and opened it. It went off like a nail gun.

"How'd you get it to match?" I said.

"I looked at three hundred and six stones. Most sapphires are clear."

"This one isn't."

"It is from Kashmir. The fine ones have something in them that scatters the light. They all came out of one valley, between 1881 and 1887."

"How do you know it's from there?"

"A lab in Lucerne. Then a lab in Basel. Nobody ever heated it."

"You had it tested twice."

"It is ten point four carats. I wanted to be sure."

"And you didn't buy forty."

"It was the only one that matched."

I took it off and turned it to the light, the way I do on a job. Inside the band, engraved small, it said 62¼.

"Sixty-two and a quarter," I said.

"Without the boots."

I put it back on. On the truck it would go on the chain, with the glass.

"Chairs off the truck," I said. "Abby squares them up. Denise brings a sheet cake. Dad sits in his chair, with his cushion. Owen rules on whether it counts."

"Which Saturday do you want?"

"Whichever one the sixteen-footer's free."

At a quarter to nine his phone buzzed face down on the table. I had my purse in my lap before I knew I'd moved. He turned it over.

```
OWEN · 8:45 PM
Did you ask her yet
```

I put the purse back on the floor.

"Owen knows?" I said.

"I asked him on Sunday, before Kevin's."

"What'd he say?"

"He asked whether Pancake comes too."

Cal lifts him up my front step every time, front end and then back.

"Then we need a ramp," I said.

"I will ask your father."

He typed his answer with one thumb. I read it over his arm.

*I did. She wrote YES.*

Owen answered before he'd put it down.

*In pencil?*

The pencil had about one more line left in it.

I reached into his shirt pocket for the old card and turned it over. Eleven lines in my capitals, eleven lines through them. Under the last one I printed the twelfth.

*SAYS WHAT SHE WANTS.*

I didn't put a line through it. I put the card back in his pocket.

"I want Monday off," I said. "You too."

He took out his phone and cleared Monday himself.
