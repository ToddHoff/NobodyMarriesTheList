## Chapter 32

**Cal**

At 2:06 on Tuesday morning I was sitting on the floor of the lobby at SAGE, on Charter Street in Redwood City, with my phone in my hand. My dog was on the other side of a door with a keypad on it.

There were eleven chairs. I was not in any of them. I had carried him in from the car at 1:52 and put him on the front counter, because there was nowhere else to put him. A woman in blue scrubs came around the end of the counter with a gray stretcher and said his name before I had told her what it was. It was on his tag. Two of them took him through the door. I sat down on the floor beside it. That was as close as they would let me go.

The room smelled of disinfectant with something sweet sprayed over it, and under both of them, dog. A television on the wall was showing a cooking program with the sound off. I had shoes on and no socks. I did not know when I had put the shoes on.

Remy had found the hospital before I was out of my gate. It had the route on the dashboard and the front desk knew I was coming by 1:41. I had not had to ask it twice. It would have called anyone I named.

I went through the names.

Janice would come. She would come anywhere I said, at any hour, with a folder on her knee, and by three o'clock there would be nothing left for me to do. She had ten days left.

Abby was at her mother's in Menlo Park, asleep, with her bags by the door. At seven I was to drive her to San Luis Obispo. Abby had named him. I would call her when I had something to tell her. I had nothing to tell anyone.

I opened Jo's thread. The last thing in it was mine. *Your father and I folded forty-eight pads.* August 6, 10:02 PM. There was nothing under it.

I did not type anything. Anything I typed, she would have to answer.

I pressed her name.

She answered before the second ring. I heard her breathe in. She did not say hello.

“It is Pancake,” I said. My voice came out higher than I have ever heard it on a recording. “I did not know who else to call.”

“Where are you.”

“SAGE. On Charter Street in Redwood City.”

“Twenty minutes.”

She hung up.

I put the phone face down on the floor beside me and looked at the keypad.

***

Abby had come at ten past six on Monday evening, with the back seat of her car full to the windows.

She had been at her mother's since Sunday. She wanted the car loaded the night before, so that we would not be fitting her boxes into my trunk at seven in her mother's driveway. She told me so in one sentence, coming up my steps with the first box already in her arms.

We loaded it in fourteen minutes. She had the boxes numbered on the tops in black marker and the numbers circled. She had packed the heavy ones small. She put the drafting-table lamp in last, on the back seat, with a towel around the shade, and belted it in.

Then she went to her own car and came back with a manila envelope and a roll of green tape.

She walked past me into the front hall and put the envelope on the table by the door.

On Friday night I had left a card in an envelope on that table. On Sunday morning it was gone. She had not said anything about it. I had not asked.

She squared the envelope to the edge of the table.

“Mom went to Jo's yard today,” she said.

I stood in the doorway with my keys in my hand.

“Why?”

“To ask her to let it end before I leave.” She did not look up from the envelope. “Quietly. Like the others.”

“What did Jo say?”

“She wouldn't promise.”

My face went hot. I put my keys in my pocket so that I would have somewhere to put my hand.

“Then Mom gave her that.” Abby put one finger on the envelope. “It's a prenup. Her lawyer drew it in July, after my party.”

“Did Jo sign it?”

“She read every page. Standing up.” She squared it again. “She didn't sign it.”

“What did she say?”

Abby looked at me then. She has my exactness.

“She said, ‘Nobody's asked me anything. If he does, he can bring it himself.’” She said it in a lower voice than her own, flat. “Mom said it twice for me. The same both times.”

I read the sentence back to myself. Then I read it again.

“If he does,” I said.

“If you do,” Abby said.

I put my hand flat on the hall table beside the envelope.

She pulled a strip off the roll of green tape, tore it against her thumb and laid it across the top of the envelope, flat, with no bubbles in it. She took the marker out of her pocket and printed DAD on the tape in her square capitals.

Green goes with the owner.

“Does your mother know you have this?” I said.

“She will.” Abby held up the roll. “Jo sent me this. Mom said she said good luck.”

She put the tape in her bag.

At the door she stopped with her hand on the frame. She looked at the table where her box always was, and at the robe hanging on my mother's door against the wall, and at me.

“I'll call you,” she said. “When I want to.”

Then she went down the steps to her car, and I stood in my doorway and watched her taillights go down the drive and through the gate and right, toward her mother's.

***

At 11:52 that night I took the envelope to the kitchen counter and read the agreement.

I read all of it, from the first page to the last, the way Jo had, standing up.

Stephanie's lawyer had my share count as of June 30. It was correct to the share. He had the house in Woodside, the house on Maui, and the trust that holds the house on West Remington Drive, which Jo had spent nine Saturdays emptying for me. Page eleven waived support, in capitals. Page nineteen kept Abby's trust whole. It was a careful document. Everything in it that was mine was right.

Schedule B was the last page. *Separate Property of,* and her name. Under it there was one line. It had been left blank for her.

She had written on it in carpenter's pencil, in her square printing. *The house in San Bruno, and what I owe on it. Silveira Moving & Estate, three trucks, my father's name on the doors since 1987.* It did not fit. It went off the end of the line and down the margin, and the last four words turned the corner at the bottom of the page.

She had not signed it.

I read the line three times. *And what I owe on it.*

I took my phone out and opened the payoff confirmation. *Balance: $0.00.* I looked from the screen to her pencil and back.

She had made a mistake. In three months I had not seen her make one on paper.

I did not understand it. I put my thumb on it. The pencil was soft enough to smear, and I took my thumb off before it did.

*If he does, he can bring it himself.*

I put the forty pages back in the envelope in their order and laid it on the hall table where Abby had squared it, with DAD up.

Pancake was asleep under my desk in the study, on his side, snoring. At 12:40 I woke him and walked him up to bed. He took the stairs one at a time with me behind him. He lay down on his bed by my door and sighed through his nose, the way he does when the day has met his specifications.

***

At 1:26 in the morning I woke up to a sound I had not heard in eleven years. It was his nails on the wood floor, scraping and not catching.

I turned the light on.

He was trying to stand up. His back legs came under him and went out to the left. He went down on his side. He tried again, and the same legs went out the same way, and he rolled. He came up against the dresser and lay there with his head tipped hard to the left.

His eyes were going. Both of them, side to side, fast, the way a man reads a line of print and jumps back to the start of it and reads it again. He could not hold them on me. He tried. I watched him try.

I got down on the floor. I said his name. His tail moved once on the boards.

Then he was sick on the rug. He was ashamed of it. He tried to get up and away from it, and he fell again.

Golden retrievers live ten to twelve years. I had read that the week Abby named him. He was eleven in March.

I was sure it was his brain.

I had a hand flat on his side. His heart was going faster than mine. Mine was going faster than I have seen it on my watch.

I carried him down the stairs and out to the car. He is sixty-four pounds. Every morning I carry him up the last three steps from the gate, because he cannot do them anymore. I had never carried him farther than that. At the bottom of the stairs he was sick again, down the front of my shirt. I put him on the back seat beside Abby's lamp, on the towels Rita keeps in the hall closet, and he lay with his head tipped and his eyes going and looked at the roof of the car.

The fog had come over the ridge. It was on my windshield in drops.

Woodside Road goes down the hill in curves. I took them faster than I have ever taken them. At every curve I heard him slide on the towels, and I put my right arm back between the seats and kept it on him. Nobody was on the road. The lights at the bottom were all red. At each one I slowed, looked both ways and went through, with my arm between the seats. I had never run a red light in my life.

***

At 2:41 that morning the glass doors opened, and Jo came in.

It was thirty-five minutes after I called her. She had come from San Bruno.

She had my fleece on, the one I handed her at my gate on the way to Carmel. It was zipped to the chin, with the sleeves pushed up. She had slept in it. I could see where it had creased across the front and down one arm. Her hair was down and flat on one side. She had her work boots on, unlaced, and the laces were knocking on the floor.

She stopped inside the doors and found me on the floor. Then she looked at the door with the keypad, and back at me.

I did not get up. I did not think I could do it well enough to be worth doing.

She came across the room and sat down on the floor beside me with her back to the wall. Her shoulder was four inches from mine. I could feel the warmth off her through the fleece, and I could smell my own dog on it, and her sleep. My shirt still had him down the front of it. She did not move away. I wanted to put my face against her neck and leave it there. I kept my hands on my knees.

“What happened,” she said.

I told her. The nails on the floor. The legs going out to the left, twice the same way. His head. His eyes.

“They go side to side,” I said. “Fast. He cannot stop them.”

“Is he hurting?”

“They have not told me.”

She looked at the door. Her face was gray in that light. I did not know what mine was.

“What'd he eat?”

“Two cups of his food at six o'clock. The same as every day.” I thought about it. “On Saturday Abby gave him the last of the pot roast when she thought I was not looking.”

“That wasn't it.”

“No.”

“Has he done anything like this before?”

“No. Never.”

She nodded. She pulled her knees up and put her arms around them. On the television someone was folding egg whites into something with great care.

“Where's he sleep?”

“On his bed by my door. I walked him up at 12:40.”

“The stairs okay?”

“One at a time. He did them.”

I knew what she was doing. She does it in a house before she touches anything. She walks it first and asks the owner where the heavy things are and what is wrong with the stairs. I had watched her do it in my mother's house in May with a clipboard against her chest. She was doing it with my dog.

I did not say anything about the envelope on my hall table.

“Owen?” I said.

“Denise.”

The woman in blue scrubs came through the door with a clipboard. She looked at the two of us on her floor and crouched down to us. She had an estimate. She explained it in one breath. An emergency exam. Bloodwork. Fluids under the skin or through a line in his leg. Something for the nausea. Overnight in the hospital, maybe two nights. It was $1,900 to $3,600, depending.

I took the pen. Jo did not say anything. She looked at the clipboard in my hands.

I turned back to the first page and read it. The exam. The panel. The line in his leg. The overnight, and the second night, priced separately. The deposit, half. It was a page and a half. The woman in scrubs waited with her hands on her knees. Jo waited beside me. When I had read the last line I signed it and handed it back.

“Thank you,” I said to the woman. “Please tell me when I can see him.”

She went back through the door.

Jo had her chin on her knees.

“Why Pancake?” she said.

“I asked Abby that when she was seven. She said he looked like one.”

Jo turned her head on her knees and looked at me.

“I did not see it,” I said. “I have looked for eleven years.”

Her mouth moved at one corner. I had not seen it do that since the ridge. I looked at it as long as I could without her seeing me look, and my pulse went up under my shirt, and I could not have given a reading for it.

“He went in up to his chest at Carmel,” she said. “I've never seen a dog so happy to be cold.”

“He had not been on a beach since Abby was twelve.”

“You told me.”

“I know. I wanted to say it again.”

She looked back at the door.

We sat. The program on the television ended and another began, with the same kitchen. At 2:51 a man came in with a cat in a carrier under a blanket and stood at the counter talking low. At 2:58 the man went through the door. Jo did not look at her phone. I did not look at mine. Twice she pulled the sleeves of the fleece down over her hands and then pushed them back up. The second time I saw her knuckles. They were white.

***

At 3:04 in the morning the doctor came out. Her name on her coat was Ramos. She was perhaps thirty-five, and she had a mark across the bridge of her nose from a mask. She sat down in the nearest chair, so that she was not standing over two people on her floor.

“He's resting,” she said. “He's comfortable now. We gave him something for the nausea, and he's got fluids going.”

“What is it?”

“Most likely it's what we call old dog vestibular disease. It's the balance system. The inner ear and the nerve, mostly. It comes on all at once. To him the room is spinning. That's why the eyes, and the tilt, and why he's sick. It looks a lot worse than it is.”

“Is it his brain?”

“I don't think so. I can't rule everything out tonight without imaging, and if he doesn't start to improve we'll talk about that. But this is what it looks like. I see a few a month. Older dogs, big dogs. Goldens.”

“What is the probability that he recovers?”

She looked at me. She had been on since six the evening before, probably. Her eyes went to Jo, and back.

“Good,” she said. “Most of them are up in a couple of days. He may keep a little tilt to his head. Some of them do.”

“That is not a number.”

“It's three in the morning,” Dr. Ramos said. “Good is the number.”

Jo made a sound beside me. It was very short.

“May I see him?” I said.

***

They had him in a room in the back on a padded mat on the floor, with a blanket over his back half and a line taped into his right front leg. A bag of fluid hung from a hook over him. His head was still tipped to the left. His eyes were still going, slower than they had been in my bedroom.

When he heard me he tried to lift his head. It went the wrong way, and he put it down.

I got down on the floor by him and put my hand on his neck. He leaned it into my hand.

Jo stopped in the doorway.

She had her hands in the sleeves of my fleece.

Then she came in and got down on the floor on the other side of him, by his head. She took one hand out of the sleeve and put it on him, flat, behind his ears, in the place he likes. Nobody had shown her that place.

Pancake turned his head into her hand. He could not hold it straight, so he leaned it there and let her take the weight of it.

She did not move her hand.

“Stay down,” she said to him, in the voice she uses on a crew. “Let them do it.”

He breathed out through his nose.

She bent over him. When she did the collar of the fleece fell away from the back of her neck, and I saw a chain there, a thin one. Whatever was on it was inside the fleece, under her chin, against her. I did not know what it was. I had never seen her wear anything at her neck. I looked at the chain on the back of her neck in that light, and my hands went cold, and I wanted to put my mouth on the place where it lay.

The tech came in to check the line. She said we could stay a few minutes more, and then he needed to sleep, and so did we.

Jo took her hand off him. She looked at it. There was dog hair all over her palm and the inside of her fingers, cream colored. She did not wipe it on anything.

“You should call Abby,” she said. “When it's light.”

“Yes.”

She got up.

I stayed down with him. The tech was showing me the clip on the line and how the pump counted, and I was listening, because I wanted to know. It took perhaps two minutes. When I looked up, the doorway was empty.

I went out through the door with the keypad. The lobby was empty. The man with the cat was gone. On the television a woman was holding up a cake.

Through the glass doors I saw her truck at the far end of the lot, under the light. Her father's name was on the door. She backed out and turned toward Woodside Road without stopping, and I watched her taillights until the building took them.

I had not said thank you to her.

I went back to the desk and gave them my card, and my number, and my own name, and paid the deposit.

***

At 4:08 that morning I let myself into my house. The rug in my bedroom was where I had left it. I rolled it up and carried it down to the garage myself, and I washed the floor under it with a towel and a bowl of water and dish soap, on my knees. Then I took my shirt off and put it in the trash, because I did not know what else to do with it.

I did not sleep. I sat in the study in the chair across from the desk he sleeps under.

At 5:00, still dark, I put my shoes on and walked down the drive to the gate by myself. The fog was in the tops of the redwoods and dripping off them onto the gravel. There was no moon.

At the gate I stopped at the post. I did not know what else to do there. I stood beside it for six minutes. Then I walked back up and climbed the three steps and went in.

At six in the morning I called the hospital. A man answered at the front desk. He found the chart.

“He held his head up at five-forty,” he said. “Still tipped, but he held it. He ate a little chicken and rice for us about five-fifty. Dr. Ramos is happy with him.”

I sat down on the stairs.

“Thank you,” I said.

“Oh. And someone else called about him,” he said. “Right around ten to six. A woman. She asked if he'd eaten anything yet. She didn't leave a name. I hope that's all right. I told her he was eating.”

“Yes,” I said. “That is all right.”

On Saturday she had made four sandwiches.

She had not called me.

I sat there with the phone against my knee until my hand stopped.

***

At 6:14 that morning my phone lit up with Dawn Pruitt's name.

There was music behind her, the kind with a beat and nothing else, and somewhere a weight going down onto a rack.

“Cal.”

“Good morning.”

“I'm on a bike at my gym,” she said. “My trainer went for water. You have till he gets back.”

“Did you play it?”

“Yes.”

“How many times?”

“I lost the count.”

She did not say anything to that for a moment. I heard her breathing, harder than on Saturday.

“I'm closing your file,” she said.

I was sitting on my own stairs with no shirt on. I had slept forty-six minutes since Monday morning. The sentence took longer to arrive than most sentences do.

“Why?”

“I don't have anybody to send you.”

“I did not ask you for anybody.”

“I know. That's why.” A machine beeped. “Seven women in eight years. I called every one of them at the end.”

“What did they say?”

“I've got it in a folder.” She breathed. “I'm done adding to it.”

“Dawn.”

“What.”

“Pancake was in the hospital last night.”

The breathing stopped. She had stopped pedaling.

“Is he alive?”

“Yes. He held his head up at 5:40. He has a disease of the inner ear. The room spins. The doctor says it looks worse than it is.”

“Who drove him?”

“I did.”

“Who'd you call?”

“Jo.”

Dawn did not say anything.

“She came in thirty-five minutes,” I said. “From San Bruno. She sat on the floor with me. She asked what he ate.”

“What else did she ask?”

“Where he sleeps. Whether the stairs were all right. Why he is named Pancake.”

“What else?”

“Nothing. Nothing else.”

“Did you thank her?”

“No. She left while I was learning the pump.”

Dawn breathed out. I heard her drink something out of a bottle, which I had never heard her do. She drinks coffee.

“At ten to six she called the hospital,” I said. “She asked whether he had eaten. She did not leave her name.”

I put my hand on the step beside me. The wood was cold.

“Why did you send her?” I said.

I had wanted to ask her since the bar. I had never asked.

“How many did I send you before her?” Dawn said.

“Seven.”

“What did they ask you on the first night?”

“About the company. About Remy. One of them asked about the house on Maui.”

“Which one of them paid for her own beer?”

“None of them.”

“Which one made you lunch?”

“None of them,” I said.

“What did they do?” Dawn said.

“They learned my schedule. My dog's name. Abby's.”

“And you?”

“I sent them things.”

“Which of you was more in love?”

“They were.”

“If it was you on that floor tonight,” Dawn said, “who would Abby call?”

“Janice.”

“Janice leaves on the twenty-eighth.”

I looked at the door to the study, where he sleeps under the desk.

“Jo,” I said.

She did not say anything.

“Have you told her?” she said.

“Yes,” I said. “Twice. On a rock at Castle Rock, and across the hood of her truck.”

“Has she said it back?”

“No.”

“Good,” Dawn said.

I looked up the stairs at the door of my bedroom. It was open, and I could see the place on the floor where the rug had been.

“That is not the answer I expected,” I said.

“When my marriage ended I paid a woman in San Mateo a hundred and eighty dollars an hour to tell me why.” I heard her shift. “Third week, she told me.”

“What did she tell you?”

“That two kinds of marriage last. They love each other the same. Or he's gone on her. Out of his mind. More than she is.”

“And the third kind?”

“She loves him more.” She drank. “That one doesn't make it.”

“Why not?”

“Because a man that far gone will do the work. He'll give things up.”

“And if she is the one?”

“Then she does all of it.” There was a clank behind her, somebody setting something down. “She'd have done it anyway. I hated her for saying it. I paid her for two more months so I could keep hating her.”

I sat on the step.

I had sent Jo five hundred and twenty-one thousand dollars from my desk. It took less than a minute.

She had sat on a hospital floor at two in the morning in my fleece and put her hand on my dog.

I knew which one I was. I had not done the work.

Behind Dawn, a man's voice said her last name.

“Pruitt. Phone or bike.”

“Phone,” Dawn said, not to me.

“You tell every one of them what to do,” the man said. He was close to her phone now. “What do you do?”

She did not answer him. I listened to her not answer him for four seconds.

“Which one were you?” I said.

She did not answer me either.

I heard her breathe once, the way my mother did on the voicemail before *Calvin*. Then the machine started again behind her, and the beat, and she said, “Drive your daughter, Cal,” and she was gone.

At 6:56 that morning I pulled into Stephanie's driveway in Menlo Park in a clean shirt. Abby was on the front step with one bag and her phone, and her mother was standing behind her in the door with her arms folded, in a sweater, with her sleeves turned back twice.

Abby came down the walk and got in and put the bag between her feet. She looked at the back seat, at the lamp belted in, and at the empty place on the seat where the dog rides.

“Where's Pancake?”

I told her. The nails on the floor, and the stairs, and the hospital, and the word the doctor had used, and the chicken and rice at ten to six. She listened to all of it with her hands flat on her knees.

“Is he okay?”

“He held his head up at 5:40.”

She looked out the windshield at her mother in the door. Stephanie had not moved.

“Why didn't you call me?”

“Jo said to call you when it was light. I wanted to tell you in the car.”

Abby turned her head and looked at me.

“Who did you call?” she said.

“Jo.”

“Did she come?”

“Yes.”

Abby looked at me a little longer. Then she reached down into the bag between her feet and took out the roll of green tape and held it in her lap, with both hands, the whole way down 280 to 101.
