Includes Todd's rule for Cal's speech (October 9, with his texts October 10), Todd's interiority rule (October 9), the October 9 critique, the consistency pass and Todd's choice of Hawaii as the one splash (October 9), Todd's clarification on narration (October 8), his ", and" rule (October 8), his note that Jo needs more personality out loud (October 8), his note that Jo talks differently on the job and off it (October 8), the rules from all five critiques, Todd's rule on facts from chapter 9, and the rules from his line review of chapter 10. The full guide with research and sources is the doc "Nobody Marries the List: Romance Style Guide". The genre's style governs this book (Todd, 2026-10-03). Todd's sentence rules still apply: no em dashes, none of the banned phrases, short paragraphs whose first sentences carry the story.

## The key (Todd, 2026-10-03)
She is not his list. The magic happens for him on the first date. What he has to learn over the book is to appreciate her as she is.

## The shelf
Contemporary romantic comedy, on the single-mom, later-in-life shelf (fourth critique, adopted by Todd 2026-10-07). It is not sold as a billionaire romance: that reader wants rescue by money, and this book makes a paid-off mortgage the betrayal. Labels in order: he falls first, single mom, later in life, class difference, matchmaker. Billionaire is secondary. Author: the pen name Wendy Garrity (Todd, 2026-10-07; it was Mara Venn). For Kindle Unlimited.
Cover: illustrated, her truck under his billboard. The hook for the blurb and logline is the list: twelve lines, and she fails eleven.
Promise: a powerful man who solves everything at a distance meets a woman who cannot be solved that way, and has to become someone who can be in the room with her. She is 44, runs her own company, has a nine-year-old, and will not step back.
Tone: warm and funny with real stakes. The comedy comes from the two of them at one table.

## The comp
Primary: Match Me If You Can, Susan Elizabeth Phillips (2005). A rich sports agent hires matchmakers to find a wife who fits his list and falls for a woman who fails it. The list is a wound. He grovels.
Supporting: The Soulmate Equation, The Rosie Project, Nora Goes Off Script, The Matchmaker (Elle Nicoll), Part of Your World, The Fine Print, Things We Never Got Over.

## Reader contract
- The love story is the main plot. Happy ending on the page, then an epilogue. The title is paid there: the epilogue holds a proposal (2026-10-07).
- No cheating. He cancels the other introductions on the drive home from the first date. No other women on the page.
- No conflict built on a misunderstanding. Conflicts are acts: the contract, the mortgage.
- No love triangle. Neither of them has anyone else.
- Active heroine: every turn is her decision. He takes her no.
- Chemistry early. He does not fall in love on the first date; he knows only that he did not want it to end. Jo is attracted at once and refuses to trust it.
- Harm is never romantic. The mortgage and the contract are wrongs he must repair.
- No secret is left at the end. Everything he kept from her is told by chapter 33 (2026-10-07).
- Heat: 3 of 5, open door, direct without being graphic, three scenes (chapters 18, 21, 35), each after an emotional step. On this shelf 3 is right; the scenes are not made hotter.
- Length: about 85,000 words; 36 chapters near 2,300 words, and an epilogue.

## Narrator and dialogue (Todd, 2026-10-08)
Todd: "My instructions about narration apply to when characters speak, not when an actual narrator speaks." Then at 11:16 AM, of Cal's narration in chapter 12: "What I mean when I say it sounds like a narrator is it has claudisms and doesn't sound like cal. That doesn't mean delete everything." So a line that "sounds like narration" has Claude's habits and does not sound like the character, whether it is spoken or narrated. It was never a rule against the narrator's own head. Cutting the narrators' feelings under it was a misreading, and it is why chapters 12 and 13 first came out thin.
- In dialogue: a character out loud does not explain, sum up, build a contrast or close on a line. Jo lists what she did and answers in a word. Todd, 2026-10-08, on chapter 17: "Let's try to give her more personality than just the shortest possible sentences. We have to have a reason he loves her too." The one-word answer is for when she is guarding herself. When she is at ease she is funny, curious and generous out loud: she teases him, tells a job story with a number in it, asks for his bad eggs the way she asked for his shrimp ("Are you going to eat those?"), and says the true thing to his face ("I've been sitting in your driveway working up my nerve."). She makes him laugh. On the job and off it (Todd, 2026-10-08: "Job her is different than her when she's not at the job. Maybe look at the previous chapters too."). On a job she runs a crew and talks like it, in colors, directions and counts. Off the job she sounds like she did at the bar, at dinner in Los Gatos, at the taco truck and on Summit Road. She asks him things, teases him and tells the story behind the job. Her short answers off the job are for when she is guarding herself (the car at her curb, Dawn's call, Denise asking about the kiss, the parking garage in chapter 13). Off the job she does not teach him or correct his work. In chapter 17 that meant cutting "Medium, next time" and her talk about the houses she clears.
- In narration: the narrator says what they feel and want right now, what hurts, and what the other person's body is doing to them, in their own voice. Jo is short and practical. Cal gives readings, times and counts, and is funny without knowing it. The genre runs on this, and every chapter carries it, fights included. It has to sound like the character, never like Claude.
- Interiority (Todd, 2026-10-09): after asking whether the chapters had become minimalist, Todd said: "Cutting and should in no way reduce interiorty. People don't speak with and yet have discursive thoughts. Please add depth to the story without making excuses for why you can't." The rules on "and yet", on ", and" joins that land a point, on closers and the rest govern how a line is built. They never license cutting what the narrator thinks. Characters speak briefly; the narrator's head is discursive, in their own idiom: memory, reasoning, want, fear, what they notice and what they make of it, and what they did not say. At every turn in a scene (a line that lands, a choice, a reveal) the narrator stays with it for a paragraph or more before the scene moves on. Cal reasons in times, counts and readings, and checks his readings against what he has just learned; Jo thinks in jobs, money, Owen and her family, and talks herself out of things. Every chapter has at least one real exchange between the leads, with talk going back and forth. Cutting is for repetition and for Claude's habits, never for the interior. Chapter 20 was deepened under this rule on October 9.
- Still out of the narration: the AI tells listed under Voice, and the habits Todd flagged in chapter 12: a tidy deduction stapled to the end of a sentence ("which meant he knew she was"), a closing clause that tells the reader to feel something ("and it is in my mother's handwriting"), a wink ("because of me"), a polished closer ("and the second time she did not look away first"), a quip, and a detail that is cute for its own sake. Also asides to the reader, a general principle stated mid-scene, explaining how the agents work, and a sentence that only restates what the action already showed.
- Length: chapters began at about 2,300 to 2,800 words, and most of that comes from the narrator's interior. Length follows the interior, so a chapter that needs more runs longer (19 is about 6,900 words and 20 about 4,600).
- The pass on chapters 1 to 13 on 2026-10-08 put the interior back but carried these habits. Todd, 11:19 AM, after seeing the chapter 12 rewrites: "So this is what I mean by narration. If we can keep that standard going. Do what is necessary for the genre, but keep it honest to the character." The voice pass was done the same morning, and that is the standard from here on: give the genre what it needs, and every line, narrated or spoken, has to sound like the person saying it.

## Voice and point of view
- First person, past tense, alternating by chapter. Jo has the odd chapters and Cal the even ones. The heading names the narrator, and a reader should know whose chapter it is within three lines.
- The matchmakers are heard only in calls with Jo or Cal on the line. A scene without a lead is a scene without the romance.
- Banter is a contest between equals. Neither lead is made a fool.
- Comedy comes from the situation and from the narrator's voice. Jo is dry. Cal is precise and does not know he is funny.
- Attraction is said outright, in the narrator's body, in every chapter: the stomach, the face, the hands. Readers on this shelf do not infer it. The first draft of chapter 1 left it implied and it did not read (Todd, 2026-10-04).
- AI tells to keep out (Todd, 2026-10-04): the word "plainly"; the phrase "never once" (Todd, 2026-10-05); asides aimed at the reader, such as "I want that on the record," "I'm not discussing it" and "I should say" (Todd, 2026-10-05); a narrator announcing what he is about to say; a narrator stopping a scene to state a general principle (the "A model fails in one way that matters" paragraph, cut from ch 2 on 2026-10-05); a run of "the way X does Y" similes (two or three a chapter at most); "longer than it needed" more than once; a statement followed by its own correction; "I'll give him this:" and other colon set-ups; the tidy paradox, as in "a key I never gave him" (Todd, 2026-10-05), where a plain fact does the job ("He lets himself in"); a pair of balanced sentences that sum up a feeling; a second clause joined on with ", and" to land a point (Todd, 2026-10-08, of "At 2:22 on Saturday there was a black car at Jo Silveira's curb, and I had not sent it": "This ', and' is a claudism. People don't speak that way"; he flagged the same "and" in chapter 14's "It isn't on the truck, and nobody has asked me since"), where two sentences do the job ("A black car was parked at Jo Silveira's curb. I had not sent it.").
- Each lead has a vocabulary. Jo: loads, stairs, hours, payroll. Cal: specs, tests, edge cases, confidence. Cal also has a stage voice, warm and rehearsed, and Jo can hear him switch to it.
- Cal uses no contractions, in his narration or in anything he says (Todd, 2026-10-09). He speaks in whole sentences. The short lines he may say are yes, no, "Noted," "Understood," "All right," "Thank you," a greeting or a name, a pick between options he was just offered, and a line of his list read aloud. The texts he types himself have no contractions either (Todd, 2026-10-10). Everyone else uses contractions.
- Wealth is specific and slightly absurd: the number, the object, the staff member.
- Each chapter ends on something changed between them, said or done. No closing thought about what it means.
- Warmth is the default. Give him the dog early.

## From Todd's line review of chapters 7 and 8 (2026-10-06 and 07)
These came up line after line. Check every new sentence against them.
- Everything is from that character's perspective. No generic detail. Before a detail goes on the page, ask what this person would see, suspect or say, given their trade, their history and what they have been told. If the character sheet has nothing, say so to Todd; do not fill the gap with a stock detail.
- Think it through; do not patch. A turn needs its cause on the page, in what the characters do. A paragraph that explains the turn is not a fix. The goal is the best, most interesting book, not the shortest change.
- Dialogue is spoken, not written. A character out loud does not sum up, build a contrast, or close on a line. Jo lists what she did and answers in a word.
- The narrator does not comment on the dialogue. No tally of what someone did not do and no gloss on a tone in place of letting the line land. It does not cut the narrator's own feelings, which stay, in the narrator's voice (Todd, 2026-10-08; see Narrator and dialogue).
- When a way of speaking is the point of a beat, the reader hears it. Cal's keynote is said aloud, not reported.
- Say what a person physically did. No figure of speech for whether a man left the house or stopped talking.
- No invented sayings. If a real person in that seat would not say or do it, it goes.
- One trade figure per conversation at most, and none added to decorate. People in this book talk about people.
- Use the word the book already uses. The gifts came in boxes, so they are never cartons. The open middle of Darlene's Eichler is the atrium, never the courtyard.
- Abstract talk about cost and price is Cal's. Jo's word for a catch is "the piano," the thing that is never on the estimate.
- Everything is right, not just what Todd can check (Todd, 2026-10-07, chapter 9: "Everyone reading it will know"). Every real-world fact is checked against a source before it goes on the page: what is open and when, where a street runs, what a thing weighs, how tall a curb leaves her. So is every fact the book has already set: heights, counts, dates, who has said what in front of whom. A detail that cannot be checked is left out. It is not left in for Todd to catch.

## From Todd's line review of chapter 10 (2026-10-07)
Ten notes in one afternoon. Each of these was a line he stopped on.
- Every line does work. A line that neither moves the story nor shows the character is cut. If the beat is needed, the replacement comes from the character's belief and gives him a decision with a cost or a clock; it is never a wry remark.
- Do not re-explain how a thing reached a character when an earlier chapter showed it. Do not summarize a quoted thing before quoting it.
- A count or a habit that comes back after a gap says what it is ("the seventh time in seventeen years").
- After a scene with two women in it, a pronoun names the woman.
- Say the thing. "The kiss," not "the curb." "The truck," never "the box," because in this book a box is cardboard.
- Before a character reacts to an event, the reader has to have seen the event at the size the reaction needs. Anything a collision needs, such as where the car is, is placed before the collision.
- A message a character refers to has to have been on the page. Do not invent a new message in Janice's voice to prop up a callback; point the callback at one the reader has seen, or cut it.
- Jo does not get a closing quip. Her scenes end on what she does or on a practical line.
- When Todd asks what a line means, answer, propose the exact line, and wait for his yes.

## Structure (Romancing the Beat, as this book places it)
1. Introduce the leads (0-5%): chapters 1 and 2 are the first date, one from each side.
2. The meet (page one): she fails eleven lines and he does not want the evening to end.
3. No way / what binds them (8%): she says no to a second date and he takes it; the Sunnyvale house puts them in one room every Saturday.
4. Adventures in love (10-25%): Saturdays, Janice's messages, the Manresa room, the first kiss at her truck.
5. Inkling of doubt (33%): she learns who has been remembering her sister's name, and that a machine wrote the list.
6. Deepening desire (39-47%): he writes four words himself, plans a date badly, makes a terrible breakfast.
7. Midpoint (50%): first love scene; he tries to manage it and she will not be managed.
8. Doubts resurface (67-70%): the photo; fourteen pages that bind only her; "Mutual, or nothing."
9. Retreat (75%): she cannot say it back, is short of money, tells him none of it.
10. Breaking point (78-80%): month four; he pays off her mortgage against his promise and Janice gives notice; she stops talking.
11. Dark night (83-89%): he deletes the apology Remy made, learns nothing is left in the plan, and plays his mother's voicemail; the dog at 2 a.m.; Dawn drops him.
12. Wake-up (91%): the annotated list; she writes a note on her terms.
13. Grand gesture (94-97%): not money. "Tell me." He listens and fixes nothing, signs her note, takes her check, cries.
14. Happy ending (100%): he walks off his own keynote for a sick nine-year-old. Epilogue: the list with one line left and one added, and a proposal on a cue card.

## Leads
Him: good-looking, well dressed and cool in public, warm on a stage; in private smart, driven and more self-absorbed than is good for him. Not cold, not a villain, not neurodivergent. He reads other people fast and well; what he does not trust is himself with them (2026-10-07). Every list line is a scar or the brand's taste. Excellent at his work. Changes by doing himself what he used to hand to Janice and Remy.
Her: wants him, does not need him; competent, overbooked and unimpressed, never hapless; her own unwritten rule (nothing given is free); her child is a person with a say; her age is written as attractive.

## Tropes to deliver
He falls first; single mother; later in life; wrong on paper; matchmaker; class difference; the grovel; slow burn with payoff; banter; hidden soft side; he remembers (Janice and Remy used to); caretaking on her terms; jealousy (of the ex-husband who knows which cleats, ch 23); forced proximity (ten Saturdays, one twin bed); public man, private woman; the pet; found family; billionaire hero, as a secondary label; epilogue. Secondary romance is held for the series (Dawn and her trainer).
Not used: reverse age gap, second chance.

## Depart from the comp
Drop its dated gender attitudes and heroine humiliation. Give the couple time together before the epilogue. Open on the first date. Keep the separation short (80% to 94%) and keep them in the same room through it. Invent the matchmaker; use the real sketches for situations only, never their lines.

## Three rules from the second critique (adopted by Todd, 2026-10-04)
- Jo corrects Cal. It is how she shows that something matters: his list, card, shirts, boots, box, tape, lunch, his way of lifting a ramp. She corrects the thing in front of her in a short instruction ("Not there. You take an end. Palms up."). His "noted" is the same pattern from his side: he files the correction without taking it in, and the count stops when he acts on one unprompted. He corrects her too, so she is never his teacher ("Do you owe him for them?"). Payoffs: he says "call it" unprompted (ch 34); her thirteenth line is "says when he was wrong."
- Jo is never his life coach. She says how to do the task and nothing larger, and he works out what it means. Never: "You need to learn to let people in." "You use money to avoid intimacy." "Your mother did this to you." Yes: "That's not how you carry one." "Next time, ask." "Kitchens go last." The same holds for Janice, Dawn, Abby and Frank. Nobody diagnoses him.
- Remy stays a mechanism. It goes on the page only when what it shows changes the meaning of what just happened. A chapter ends on a Remy screen only when that screen is the turn. No Remy screens from ch 7 through ch 9, and none in ch 11. In ch 8 Cal has Remy send a taco truck (Todd, 2026-10-07); it is one typed line and an amount confirmed, with no screen shown, and in ch 9 only the truck is on the page. Keep the appearances on the plan's ladder and cut any that only report.

## From the third critique (adopted by Todd, 2026-10-04)
One suggestion from the same read was rejected: Jo does not challenge Cal about the boxes in so many words, because that breaks the life-coach rule above.
- Jo alone with the work. Each Jo chapter gets 200 to 300 words of her working by herself in the house: the dust, the weight, the quiet. It makes his arrival an interruption. Chapters have run near 1,900 words, and this is where the rest of the 2,300 goes.
- Cal gets friction. He is used to a life with an undo. When he gets something wrong he may be short-tempered about it, push back at her, and reach for his phone before he remembers. He is not a good student in every scene.
- Jo does not always have the comeback. Sometimes she looks at him, or says "Huh," or goes back to work. She can speak in fragments. Cal never does.
- The list slows down. Four lines are struck by ch 7, all jokes. None is struck in ch 8 or 9. The eight left cost him something and come one at a time.

## From the fourth critique (adopted by Todd, 2026-10-07)
- Jo has her own ladder, and each rung is an act: she bills him a dinner because she cannot ask (ch 7); "I can do it," and then she lets him (ch 17); "Can you?" (ch 25); her "I've got it" costs her father (ch 27); "I need you to stay" (ch 35); his name on Owen's emergency card (ch 36). Her belief has to cost someone other than her before chapter 29.
- Cal wins once on her ground. In ch 25 what he knows about how a model fails helps her, because she asked, and no money is spent.
- The plan fires in the good weeks. The reader has known since ch 6 that fourteen items are scheduled. They go off where everyone can see them (the car in ch 16, the Napa weekend in ch 20), and each time he removes one item and does not open the plan.
- Protect chapter 29. A paid mortgage is many readers' dream. Put what it took from her, the broken promise and Janice's line before her anger.
- No secret survives chapter 33.
- The epilogue pays the title with a proposal.
- Less machinery. Her reasons stop at the first kiss in ch 9. His "noted" is counted for the last time by ch 15. Two beats left chapter 30: Janice's notice went to ch 28 and Dawn to ch 32.

## From the fifth critique (adopted 2026-10-07; Todd: "Adopt the ones you think improve the book")
An outside reader of chapters 1 to 10. These apply from chapter 12 on; the drafted chapters were not changed. The plan has the placements.
- Cal reads people. The reader took him for a genius who cannot read a room, which is not the man Todd described. In each Cal chapter he reads another person fast and correctly at least once, the way a man does who raised money and built a company. He is wrong only about himself, and about what a thing will mean to the person he gives it to. Keep his times and counts for where the number is the joke or the fact; they are not a tic.
- Capability is offered first. His reach is fun to watch when he asks before he uses it ("I can have a kitchen sent almost anywhere"). Each time, the easy fix is refused or is the wrong one. Anything he does unasked after ch 6 is either the plan firing or the relapse in ch 28. No surprise trips. The one splash is Hawaii in ch 19 (Todd, 2026-10-09). Cal asks Jo first, it is for Owen, he tells her the truth about the zeppelin, and it is something they do, not something she keeps. Tokyo in ch 28 is his own idea, made in fear, and it is a mistake.
- "In the room" is the spine: whoever owns the thing is in the room when it is decided, and he cannot send someone to be in it for him. It comes back in what people do. Nobody says it as a moral.
- Jo is a fool for him now and then. One act in each Jo chapter through 17 that she would call stupid in anyone else. Two intelligent adults being idiots about each other is the comedy; she is not right about everything.
- The messages have to cost something. When Jo learns who wrote them (ch 12 and 13), the question on the page is which parts were him, and he answers it with a list of acts, without Janice or Remy in the sentence.
- Cal does not explain what an act has shown. He never says he has not felt this before. The phone turned over, the watch left in the car, the lunch he made: those are the romance.
- Lines the reader said to protect are listed in the plan. Do not polish them into ordinary romance prose.
- Not adopted: selling the book on the billionaire shelf; the critic's sample dialogue, which is not ours to use.

## Chapter routine (from Todd's craft notes, 2026-10-04)
1. Scene card, before drafting: what the narrator wants in this chapter, what stands in the way, what goes wrong, what is done that cannot be undone, and what has changed between the two of them by the last line.
2. Grounding, before drafting: two or three observed details from the narrator's trade or the observation bank, changed to fit; at least two of body, weather, money and tools; what the narrator was doing in the six hours before.
3. Check, after drafting: check every real-world fact against a source and every book fact against the earlier chapters; run the AI tells list and the two line-review lists above; read only the first sentence of each paragraph and see whether the story survives; cover the heading and test whether the narrator is clear in three lines; check both voices against claude/character-sheet.md; confirm the narrator says the attraction outright at least once, and that at each turn the narrator's own feeling is on the page in their voice (2026-10-08); confirm that Jo corrects a task and never explains him to himself, and that any Remy screen changes the meaning of what came before it; in a Jo chapter, confirm she has a stretch alone with the work, and through ch 17 one act she would call stupid in anyone else; in a Cal chapter, confirm he reads one person fast and correctly, and cut any sentence where he says what an act already showed; wherever a scene jumps in time or place inside the chapter, put a line holding only `***`, which the Word export centers and Vellum turns into an ornamental break (Todd, 2026-10-07).
4. Log: record the card and the details used in the book plan's chapter log, so no detail is used twice.

No new machinery from chapter 19 on (outside critique, October 9). Claude adds no new plants or recurring objects while drafting. New ones come from Todd, and where it can, a new idea rides an existing plant. Not every plant has to pay off at the end; the list and the cue card carry the ending, with the mortgage and the piano under them. Every scene has to work as a scene first.
