# Nobody Marries the List: Romance Style Guide (working copy)

Last updated: Wednesday, October 7, 2026, 11:50 AM PDT. Includes the rules from all four critiques and Todd's rule on facts from chapter 9. The full guide with research and sources is the doc "Nobody Marries the List: Romance Style Guide". The genre's style governs this book (Todd, 2026-10-03). Todd's sentence rules still apply: no em dashes, none of the banned phrases, short paragraphs whose first sentences carry the story.

## The key (Todd, 2026-10-03)
She is not his list. The magic happens for him on the first date. What he has to learn over the book is to appreciate her as she is.

## The shelf
Contemporary romantic comedy, on the single-mom, later-in-life shelf (fourth critique, adopted by Todd 2026-10-07). It is not sold as a billionaire romance: that reader wants rescue by money, and this book makes a paid-off mortgage the betrayal. Labels in order: he falls first, single mom, later in life, class difference, matchmaker. Billionaire is secondary. Author: the pen name Wendy Garrity (Todd, 2026-10-07; it was Mara Venn). For Kindle Unlimited.
Cover: illustrated, her truck under his billboard. The hook for the blurb and logline is the list: twelve lines, and she fails eleven.
Promise: a powerful man who solves everything at a distance meets a woman who cannot be solved that way, and has to become someone who can be in the room with her. She is 44, runs her own company, has a nine-year-old, and will not step back.
Tone: warm and funny with real stakes. The comedy comes from the two of them at one table.

## The comp
Primary: Match Me If You Can, Susan Elizabeth Phillips (2005). A rich sports agent hires matchmakers to find a wife who fits his list and falls for a woman who fails it. The list is a wound. He grovels.
Supporting: The Soulmate Equation, The Rosie Project, Nora Goes Off Script, The Matchmaker (Elle Nicoll), Part of Your World, The Fine Print, Things We Never Got Over.

## Reader contract
- The love story is the main plot. Happy ending on the page, then an epilogue. The title is paid there: the epilogue holds a proposal (2026-10-07).
- No cheating. He cancels the other introductions on the drive home from the first date. No other women on the page.
- No conflict built on a misunderstanding. Conflicts are acts: the contract, the mortgage.
- No real love triangle. Kevin and the trainer are foils.
- Active heroine: every turn is her decision. He takes her no.
- Chemistry early. He does not fall in love on the first date; he knows only that he did not want it to end. Jo is attracted at once and refuses to trust it.
- Harm is never romantic. The mortgage and the contract are wrongs he must repair.
- No secret is left at the end. Everything he kept from her is told by chapter 33 (2026-10-07).
- Heat: 3 of 5, open door, direct without being graphic, three scenes (chapters 18, 21, 35), each after an emotional step. On this shelf 3 is right; the scenes are not made hotter.
- Length: about 85,000 words; 36 chapters near 2,300 words, and an epilogue.

## Voice and point of view
- First person, past tense, alternating by chapter. Jo has the odd chapters and Cal the even ones. The heading names the narrator, and a reader should know whose chapter it is within three lines.
- The matchmakers are heard only in calls with Jo or Cal on the line. A scene without a lead is a scene without the romance.
- Banter is a contest between equals. Neither lead is made a fool.
- Comedy comes from the situation and from the narrator's voice. Jo is dry. Cal is precise and does not know he is funny.
- Attraction is said outright, in the narrator's body, in every chapter: the stomach, the face, the hands. Readers on this shelf do not infer it. The first draft of chapter 1 left it implied and it did not read (Todd, 2026-10-04).
- AI tells to keep out (Todd, 2026-10-04): the word "plainly"; the phrase "never once" (Todd, 2026-10-05); asides aimed at the reader, such as "I want that on the record," "I'm not discussing it" and "I should say" (Todd, 2026-10-05); a narrator announcing what he is about to say; a narrator stopping a scene to state a general principle (the "A model fails in one way that matters" paragraph, cut from ch 2 on 2026-10-05); a run of "the way X does Y" similes (two or three a chapter at most); "longer than it needed" more than once; a statement followed by its own correction; "I'll give him this:" and other colon set-ups; the tidy paradox, as in "a key I never gave him" (Todd, 2026-10-05), where a plain fact does the job ("He lets himself in"); a pair of balanced sentences that sum up a feeling.
- Each lead has a vocabulary. Jo: loads, straps, stairs, hours, payroll. Cal: specs, tests, edge cases, confidence. Cal also has a stage voice, warm and rehearsed, and Jo can hear him switch to it.
- Wealth is specific and slightly absurd: the number, the object, the staff member.
- Each chapter ends on something changed between them, said or done. No closing thought about what it means.
- Warmth is the default. Give him the dog early.

## From Todd's line review of chapters 7 and 8 (2026-10-06 and 07)
These came up line after line. Check every new sentence against them.
- Everything is from that character's perspective. No generic detail. Before a detail goes on the page, ask what this person would see, suspect or say, given their trade, their history and what they have been told. If the character sheet has nothing, say so to Todd; do not fill the gap with a stock detail.
- Think it through; do not patch. A turn needs its cause on the page, in what the characters do. A paragraph that explains the turn is not a fix. The goal is the best, most interesting book, not the shortest change.
- Dialogue is spoken, not written. A character out loud does not sum up, build a contrast, or close on a line. Jo lists what she did and answers in a word.
- The narrator does not comment on the dialogue: no tally of what someone did not do, no gloss on a tone, no summary of the narrator's own state. Report what is seen and what the hands do.
- When a way of speaking is the point of a beat, the reader hears it. Cal's keynote is said aloud, not reported.
- Say what a person physically did. No figure of speech for whether a man left the house or stopped talking.
- No invented sayings. If a real person in that seat would not say or do it, it goes.
- One trade figure per conversation at most, and none added to decorate. People in this book talk about people.
- Use the word the book already uses. The gifts came in boxes, so they are never cartons.
- Abstract talk about cost and price is Cal's. Jo's word for a catch is "the piano," the thing that is never on the estimate.
- Everything is right, not just what Todd can check (Todd, 2026-10-07, chapter 9: "Everyone reading it will know"). Every real-world fact is checked against a source before it goes on the page: what is open and when, where a street runs, what a thing weighs, how tall a curb leaves her. So is every fact the book has already set: heights, counts, dates, who has said what in front of whom. A detail that cannot be checked is left out. It is not left in for Todd to catch.

## Structure (Romancing the Beat, as this book places it)
1. Introduce the leads (0-5%): chapters 1 and 2 are the first date, one from each side.
2. The meet (page one): she fails eleven lines and he does not want the evening to end.
3. No way / what binds them (8%): she says no to a second date and he takes it; the Sunnyvale house puts them in one room every Saturday.
4. Adventures in love (10-25%): Saturdays, Janice's messages, the Manresa room, the first kiss at her truck.
5. Inkling of doubt (33%): she learns who has been remembering her sister's name, and that a machine wrote the list.
6. Deepening desire (39-47%): he writes four words himself, plans a date badly, makes a terrible breakfast.
7. Midpoint (50%): first love scene; he tries to manage it and she will not be managed.
8. Doubts resurface (67-70%): the photo; fourteen pages that bind only her; "Mutual, or nothing."
9. Retreat (75%): she cannot say it back, is short of money, tells him none of it.
10. Breaking point (78-80%): month four; he pays off her mortgage against his promise and Janice gives notice; she stops talking.
11. Dark night (83-89%): he pauses the plan and plays his mother's voicemail; the dog at 2 a.m.; Dawn drops him.
12. Wake-up (91%): the annotated list; she writes a note on her terms.
13. Grand gesture (94-97%): not money. "Tell me." He listens and fixes nothing, signs her note, takes her check, cries.
14. Happy ending (100%): he walks off his own keynote for a sick nine-year-old. Epilogue: the list with one line left and one added, and a proposal on a cue card.

## Leads
Him: good-looking, well dressed and cool in public, warm on a stage; in private smart, driven and more self-absorbed than is good for him. Not cold, not a villain, not neurodivergent. Every list line is a scar or the brand's taste. Excellent at his work. Changes by doing himself what he used to hand to Janice and Remy.
Her: wants him, does not need him; competent, overbooked and unimpressed, never hapless; her own unwritten rule (nothing given is free); her child is a person with a say; her age is written as attractive.

## Tropes to deliver
He falls first; single mother; later in life; wrong on paper; matchmaker; class difference; the grovel; slow burn with payoff; banter; hidden soft side; he remembers (Janice and Remy used to); caretaking on her terms; jealousy (Kevin at the handoff); forced proximity (ten Saturdays, one twin bed); public man, private woman; the pet; found family; billionaire hero, as a secondary label; epilogue. Secondary romance is held for the series (Dawn and her trainer).
Not used: reverse age gap, second chance.

## Depart from the comp
Drop its dated gender attitudes and heroine humiliation. Give the couple time together before the epilogue. Open on the first date. Keep the separation short (80% to 94%) and keep them in the same room through it. Invent the matchmaker; use the real sketches for situations only, never their lines.

## Three rules from the second critique (adopted by Todd, 2026-10-04)
- Jo corrects Cal. It is how she shows that something matters: his list, card, shirts, boots, box, tape, lunch, his way of lifting a ramp. She corrects the thing in front of her in a short instruction ("Not there. You take an end. Palms up."). His "noted" is the same pattern from his side: he files the correction without taking it in, and the count stops when he acts on one unprompted. He corrects her too, so she is never his teacher ("Do you owe him for them?"). Payoffs: he says "call it" unprompted (ch 34); her thirteenth line is "says when he was wrong."
- Jo is never his life coach. She says how to do the task and nothing larger, and he works out what it means. Never: "You need to learn to let people in." "You use money to avoid intimacy." "Your mother did this to you." Yes: "That's not how you carry one." "Next time, ask." "Kitchens go last." The same holds for Janice, Dawn, Abby and Frank. Nobody diagnoses him.
- Remy stays a mechanism. It goes on the page only when what it shows changes the meaning of what just happened. A chapter ends on a Remy screen only when that screen is the turn. No Remy screens from ch 7 through ch 9. Keep the appearances on the plan's ladder and cut any that only report.

## From the third critique (adopted by Todd, 2026-10-04)
One suggestion from the same read was rejected: Jo does not challenge Cal about the boxes in so many words, because that breaks the life-coach rule above.
- Jo alone with the work. Each Jo chapter gets 200 to 300 words of her working by herself in the house: the dust, the weight, the quiet. It makes his arrival an interruption. Chapters have run near 1,900 words, and this is where the rest of the 2,300 goes.
- Cal gets friction. He is used to a life with an undo. When he gets something wrong he may be short-tempered about it, push back at her, and reach for his phone before he remembers. He is not a good student in every scene.
- Jo does not always have the comeback. Sometimes she looks at him, or says "Huh," or goes back to work. She can speak in fragments. Cal never does.
- The list slows down. Four lines are struck by ch 7, all jokes. None is struck in ch 8 or 9. The eight left cost him something and come one at a time.

## From the fourth critique (adopted by Todd, 2026-10-07)
- Jo has her own ladder, and each rung is an act: she bills him a dinner because she cannot ask (ch 7); "I can do it," and then she lets him (ch 17); "Can you?" (ch 25); her "I've got it" costs her father (ch 27); "I need you to stay" (ch 35); his name on Owen's emergency card (ch 36). Her belief has to cost someone other than her before chapter 29.
- Cal wins once on her ground. In ch 25 what he knows about how a model fails helps her, because she asked, and no money is spent.
- The plan fires in the good weeks. The reader has known since ch 6 that fourteen items are scheduled. They go off where everyone can see them (the car in ch 16, the Napa weekend in ch 20), and each time he removes one item and does not open the plan.
- Protect chapter 29. A paid mortgage is many readers' dream. Put what it took from her, the broken promise and Janice's line before her anger.
- No secret survives chapter 33.
- The epilogue pays the title with a proposal.
- Less machinery. Her reasons stop at the first kiss in ch 9. His "noted" is counted for the last time by ch 15. Chapter 30 gives Janice's notice to ch 28 and Dawn to ch 32.

## Chapter routine (from Todd's craft notes, 2026-10-04)
1. Scene card, before drafting: what the narrator wants in this chapter, what stands in the way, what goes wrong, what is done that cannot be undone, and what has changed between the two of them by the last line.
2. Grounding, before drafting: two or three observed details from the narrator's trade or the observation bank, changed to fit; at least two of body, weather, money and tools; what the narrator was doing in the six hours before.
3. Check, after drafting: check every real-world fact against a source and every book fact against the earlier chapters; run the AI tells list and the line-review list above; read only the first sentence of each paragraph and see whether the story survives; cover the heading and test whether the narrator is clear in three lines; check both voices against claude/character-sheet.md; confirm the narrator says the attraction outright at least once; confirm that Jo corrects a task and never explains him to himself, and that any Remy screen changes the meaning of what came before it; in a Jo chapter, confirm she has a stretch alone with the work.
4. Log: record the card and the details used in the book plan's chapter log, so no detail is used twice.

## Places, as each narrator sees them (starting notes; "new" means not yet on the page)
- The Pied Piper, Palace Hotel. Jo: a sixteen-foot painting of a piper walking children out of town, and a valet who wants $110 for her truck. Cal: a test condition, the same table, the same order, forty-five minutes.
- Her yard, South San Francisco (new). Jo: three trucks nose out with her father's name on the doors, an office phone Remy answers, the one place she is never the guest. Cal: a place where everybody has a job and he does not. He first sees it in ch 6, when he brings the sandwich; the ch 3 estimate is at the curb of his mother's house, not here.
- Darlene's house, an old Eichler on West Remington Drive, Sunnyvale (new). The street name is "West Remington Drive" the first time it comes up in a chapter and plain "Remington" after that; never "West Remington" or "Remington Drive" (Todd, 2026-10-05: be consistent). Jo: glass walls, no attic and no crawl space, so fewer places for money, and she can still feel where it is; kept ready for an inspection that never came; an oven in a heat wave. Cal: a house he paid off and cannot walk through, 22 minutes from his own; he stops where the kitchen floor starts.
- His house, Woodside (new). Jo: staff who try to take her bag, too many rooms with nothing left out on a counter. Cal: quiet; the dog on the gravel at five; the refrigerator where Abby leaves things for him to find.
- Her house, San Bruno (new). Jo: paid for a month at a time; the mortgage statement on the counter with her plan in pen; planes overhead some days. Cal: a kitchen where somebody actually cooks; he does not know where to stand.
- Her truck. Jo: her office; a clean shirt behind the seat; a rack that makes valets nervous. Cal: her father's name on the door.

## Names
Never Priya. Pick each name from the character's birth year and home ground, prefer plain to pretty, do not match a name to a job, and check the avoid list in the book plan before any name goes on the page. The pen name, Wendy Garrity, is Todd's choice and sits outside this rule, which covers characters.

## Decisions, settled
Point of view (first person, past, alternating); the assistant (Janice writes his words, Remy keeps his schedule); how she gets a first date (the matchmaker sends her on purpose); heat (3 of 5); ages (he is 41, she is 44); the dog lives; the twelve lines are in the book plan; the shelf (romantic comedy; billionaire secondary); the pen name (Wendy Garrity).
