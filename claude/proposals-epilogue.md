## What changed and why

- **One night, start to finish.** It all happens Friday, May 7, 2027, the same night as the first date, on Jo's way to the table and at it. There's one flashback, and it comes while she's waiting in the empty house.
- **One question carries it.** In 31 Stephanie said "If there's a ring, it comes with this." Jo said "If he does, he can bring it himself." The ring in the soap dish brings that back. His pulse at 94 sharpens it. The painting makes it a sign. When he puts Stephanie's envelope on the table, he's brought it himself, and then he asks. (The old draft misquoted Stephanie as "the next thing he paid for would be a ring." She never said that.)
- **Each piece sets up a later one.**
  - Carol's fifty-four years become Jo's arithmetic of mornings at the question. (Mrs. Hollis was already fifty-one, so the number changed.)
  - The pencil runs the whole way: an inch and a half, the box of new ones, "five and a half inches in May," the prenup line, YES, and the last line.
  - The thirteenth line shows what Cal brings, and that's why Owen already knows.
- **The ring's logic is fixed.** Jo used to leave a found ring in a realtor's lockbox. Now Carol comes and signs for it, which is Jo's rule.
- **The billboard.** Jo drives under CALL YOUR MOTHER at Oyster Point with him on her speaker. That's where "Thank you, Mom" now goes, so it has a reason to come up.
- **The ending pays off line twelve.** After "In pencil?" she writes the twelfth line on the back of his old card, *SAYS WHAT SHE WANTS*, and doesn't strike it. It's the only line on the list that turned out true, and she just did it. Then she gives him what's left of the pencil.
- **Cut:**
  - October 7 and the step
  - his mother's clock on the nightstand
  - March, with Denise as partner
  - April, with the recipe tin and the massa sovada
  - the man with the hinge
  - the printout's details (the magnet, the blue ink, the dates)
- **Kept as Todd set them:** the Oak and the loving tree, who owns it, the form, the Nut Tree, the shrimp arc, the card, and everything after "Then ask."

## The draft

## Epilogue

**Jo**

The ring was in the soap dish by the kitchen sink. That's where they always are.

The family had been through the house in March and taken what they wanted. The daughter had gone through the jewelry box on the dresser twice. Nobody looks in the soap dish. A woman takes her ring off at the sink to do the dishes. She puts it back on when she's done. One night she doesn't.

It was a plain gold band, worn thin on the palm side. The house was a ranch in San Mateo. My crew had taken the last load to the yard after lunch. I'd stayed to walk it by myself, every door and every drawer.

There was nobody to see it but me. I set it on the counter, took a picture and texted it to the daughter, Carol.

She wrote back before I'd put the phone down.

*Oh my God*

*Where*

*Soap dish,* I wrote.

*Don't leave. Ten minutes.*

I wrote the ring on the found sheet. The pencil was down to an inch and a half. I'd been sharpening it with my box knife since the night I met him. That was a year ago tonight. He'd asked for the same table at the Oak, at eight. He said he'd booked forty-five minutes.

Every line I'd ever struck on his list had come out of that pencil. The list was on my fridge, the printout he'd mailed me in August. Eleven of the twelve had a line through them. He'd told me why eleven was there the afternoon we stayed home with Owen. He'd wanted a wife who could leave when he left, so nobody would be at home waiting on him to call.

Twelve was *Says what she wants*. I'd left that one alone.

Under it, in my printing, there was a thirteenth.

*SAYS WHEN HE WAS WRONG.*

I wrote it in February, the week after Owen turned ten. While Owen was at his dad's, Cal went into the dog app Owen wrote himself and made it faster. Owen came home and opened it. He shut it again. Then he went to his room and shut the door.

That night Cal sat down on the hall floor with his back against the wall outside Owen's door. I was at the sink. He talked to the door.

"I changed your app without asking you," he said. "That was wrong. I have put it back the way you wrote it."

For a while the door stayed shut. Then it opened. Owen stood there in his socks with the phone.

"Show me how you made it fast," he said.

Cal got up off the floor and went in. I dried my hands. Then I went to the fridge and wrote the thirteenth line under the twelfth.

Carol let herself in the back without knocking, the way you do at your mother's. She signed the found sheet on the counter under my line. Then she held the ring up to the window over the sink.

"Fifty-four years she wore that," she said. "She never took it off except for dishes."

She put it on her thumb to get it home. I locked up after her and left the keys in the lockbox for the realtor.

***

On the way to the freeway I stopped at the hardware store for a box of pencils, the flat red ones. In line I called Cal.

"I'm at the hardware store," I said. "You need anything?"

"I do not need anything."

I hadn't thought he would. I didn't have anything to ask him. I was in a store.

I stayed on anyway. So did he. Behind him somebody laughed. A glass went down on a table.

"You're there already," I said.

"I am at the table."

"It's at eight."

"I came at seven."

"What's your heart doing?" I said.

"My watch says ninety-four."

"What's it say on a stage?"

"It says sixty-one."

I knew his ears had gone red. They still do, with me. I stood in line with my pencils and tried to think what a man does for an hour at a table that gets him to ninety-four.

Last August Stephanie had put an envelope on my desk. *If there's a ring,* she'd said, *it comes with this.* An hour ago I'd had a ring in my hand, out of a dead woman's soap dish.

"What are you buying?" he said.

"Pencils."

"How long is the one you have?"

"Inch and a half."

"It was five and a half inches in May."

"You measured my pencil."

"You had me measure you."

I paid and went out to the truck with the phone at my ear. He was still on it. I put him on the speaker and got on 101.

He stayed on all the way up. Neither of us had anything to say. I could hear the bar behind him. He could hear the noise my truck still makes on hills.

At Oyster Point I went under his billboard. CALL YOUR MOTHER, forty-eight feet wide. The rest of him was on my speaker, saying nothing.

On the eighth of October, a year to the day, he'd played his mother's message on my couch. When she said *Calvin*, he said, "Thank you, Mom," into the space she'd left him.

***

I put the truck on Minna Street. The curb's a loading zone until six. It was after. The clean shirt stayed in its bag behind the seat. I went in with San Mateo down the front of me.

The oak was still behind the bar.

I stopped inside the door. One tree on a gold hill, with the long limb going out low and setting its elbow down in the grass. A year ago I'd slowed down on my way to being late and wanted to put my hand flat on it. Since July I'd had my hand on the real one.

It was his tree. It had been in the room the first night, when it got decided. Mrs. Hollis and her husband put their hands on the real one the night he proposed.

My mother believed in signs. I never have. I stood there long enough that the hostess asked me if I had a reservation.

Cal was at the corner table with his phone at his ear. He stood up when he saw me.

"I'm here," I said into mine, ten feet from him, and hung up.

He put his phone face down on the table. There was no shrimp. There were three small plates, oysters on ice, clams in a black pan and fries in a paper cone, with a bite gone out of each.

"You started without me," I said.

"I have been here an hour." The menu was open beside the plates. "I ordered three things I have never had."

"And?"

"Not the oysters."

"The fries," I said.

"The fries." He pushed the cone across to me. "I left you most of them."

He had on the gray shirt. The gray was coming in over his ears. I wanted my hands in it.

The bartender was there before my chair stopped moving. I asked for whatever was on draft. He said it would go on Mr. Brennan's tab.

"Thanks," I said.

There was a twenty in my purse. It stayed there. Cal looked at my purse. Then he looked at me.

"That's your tree," I said.

He looked over at it, like he'd forgotten it was there.

"It is the loving tree," he said.

"Who painted it?"

"Mrs. Hollis's grandmother, in 1919. It hung in the front hall. Mrs. Hollis left it with the house. She said it went with the tree."

"So what's it doing here?"

"This place needed something for that wall the week it opened. It was the only thing I had that was long enough."

"Who owns this place, Cal?"

He moved his glass a quarter inch.

"I do."

"You never said."

"I own a number of restaurants. I have only been inside this one. I did not think about it until you stopped in the door."

"You named it."

"There was a form. It needed a name by Friday. I looked out my kitchen window."

"Why not the Loving Tree?"

"It was too close to the old Nut Tree."

I laughed into my beer. "You thought about it."

"For about a minute."

He had Stephanie's envelope on the seat beside him. He put it on the table between us. Abby's green tape was still across the flap, with DAD on it in her printing.

I'd told Stephanie that if he ever asked me anything, he could bring it himself. I looked at it a while before I opened it.

I turned to the last page. My printing still ran off the end of the line and down the margin. He hadn't added a word to it. I got the pencil out of my purse and drew a line through *and what I owe on it*.

He looked at the line a long time. Then he put the forty pages back in the envelope in their order.

I kept the pencil in my hand. It wasn't steady.

He took a card out of his shirt pocket. There were two in there. The other one was the old one, gone soft at the corners. He laid the new one on the table between us, face up, and squared it to the edge with one finger.

It was his printing, in blue ink.

```
JO SILVEIRA
Silveira Moving & Estate
South S.F. Est. 1987.
Divorced. One son, 10.

ASK: her.
DON'T: say "noted."
```

The seven in 1987 went straight down. Nobody had crossed it.

He'd taken every one of my corrections. Jo, with no question mark after it. *& Estate*, where I'd put it in for him a year ago in pencil. Owen at ten. The bottom of the card was blank.

I read DON'T and laughed out loud. The couple at the next table looked over.

I read ASK again.

"Then ask," I said.

He put his hands flat on the table on either side of the card. He said it in the voice he uses in my kitchen.

"Will you marry me?"

I looked for a box. I'd been looking for one since the envelope came out. On the table there was his card, Stephanie's envelope, the fries and my beer.

Carol's mother had taken her ring off to do the dishes for fifty-four years. That's nineteen thousand seven hundred mornings she put it back on.

At the bottom of the card, where the car used to go, I printed what I wanted, in capitals, so he couldn't read it two ways.

*YES. IN MY DRIVEWAY.*

I turned it around and slid it across to him.

He read it twice. He put his thumb on it and held it there.

He stood up. I got up too.

"Come down here," I said.

He came down. I put my hands in the gray over his ears and kissed him at the corner table. He tasted like salt. The couple at the next table looked over again.

We sat down on the same side.

"Chairs off the truck," I said. "Abby squares them up. Denise brings a sheet cake. Dad sits in his chair, with his cushion. Owen times it."

"I did not bring a ring."

"Good. They catch on the straps."

"Which Saturday do you want?"

"Whichever one the sixteen-footer's free."

At a quarter to nine his phone buzzed face down on the table. I had my purse in my lap before I knew I'd moved. He turned it over.

```
OWEN · 8:45 PM
Did you ask her yet
```

I put the purse back on the floor.

"Owen knows?" I said.

"I asked him on Sunday, before Kevin's."

"What'd he say?"

"He asked whether Pancake comes too."

Cal lifts him up my front step every time, front end and then back.

"Then we need a ramp," I said.

"I will ask your father."

He typed his answer with one thumb. I read it over his arm.

*Yes. She wrote it down.*

Owen answered before he'd put it down.

*In pencil?*

I still had the pencil in my hand. There was about one more line left in it.

I reached into his shirt pocket for the old card and turned it over. Eleven lines in my capitals, eleven lines through them. Under the last one I printed the twelfth.

*SAYS WHAT SHE WANTS.*

I didn't put a line through it. I put the card back in his pocket, with what was left of the pencil.
