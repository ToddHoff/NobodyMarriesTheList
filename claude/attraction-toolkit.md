# Showing attraction: the toolkit

Written October 24, 2026, about 5:45 PM, after Todd's note on chapter 9: "A reaction by her is good, not just the same hot. Do some research on different ways to indicate attraction in a romance book of this type. I have a feeling this issue will come up again."

This goes with `claude/attraction-details.md`. That file says *what* each of them can't stop noticing (her hands; how he owns himself). This one lists *ways* to show the pull, so options don't fall back on a blush.

## The rule

Attraction shows through specific, involuntary attention to this one person, not through the narrator's own blush or stomach. The swap test still applies: put another attractive person in the scene, and the beat should stop working. The research agrees. Close noticing is itself the tension, a small imperfection signals desire better than praise, and what a character notices reveals the noticer (Writers in the Storm; Sherry Thomas).

## What the book already leans on

| Face or cheeks warm, hot or red | 13 | 7 |
| Heart going or pounding | 7 | 5 |
| Stomach | 4 | 2 |
| Neck hot or red | 3 | 2 |
| Hands not steady | 3 | 3 |
| Pulse (mostly the watch's heart-rate taps, a device) | 0 | 14 |

Budget from here:

- **Cal's red ears are his signature.** The epilogue pays it off ("His ears had gone red. They still do, with me."). Keep it for moments he truly can't hide, not as the default reaction.
- **Face warm, neck hot and stomach are retired as defaults.** Use at most one body reaction per scene, and never the same one as the last time.
- **The watch is Cal's device, not a body reaction.** Use it sparingly, and only when Remy's report says something Cal won't. Todd, October 25: "You are using the watch too much to indicate interest." Do not reach for a tap or buzz to show he wants her; show it in what he does or notices.

## Fifteen other ways, with this book's versions

Where the book already does a cue well, the example is quoted with its chapter. Those are the house style.

1. **The expert slips.** Each of them is very good at something. Attraction makes that skill fail for a second. *Jo:* she backed the sixteen-footer into a fender "because I was looking at him in my mirror" (13); "I lost count of the boxes twice and had to go by the tiers." (15); "read the same line on it twice" (7). *Cal:* loses his place in the four-minute pitch; misses a watch tap; does arithmetic wrong.
2. **Attention narrows.** The room drops away, shown with something concrete she would normally catch: a voice she misses, a question asked twice, a car alarm she doesn't hear. Jo hears everything on a job, so this lands hard for her.
3. **Displacement.** The hands or eyes go to work instead of to him: the clipboard under her arm (7), the tortillas, retaping a box that doesn't need it. Cal squares the knife because the card is in the car (8). Make it something from their trade, never generic fidgeting.
4. **Measuring the distance she won't admit to.** "On a bench that short, his knee was twelve inches from mine. I could have told you that without looking. I wasn't looking." (5). Jo measures for a living; she can't stop measuring him.
5. **The touch not taken.** Wanting it, then holding back. "I wanted to put my hand flat on his back… I kept it on the clipboard." (5); "I wanted to take it off with my thumb." (8). Restraint builds more tension than contact (Writers in the Storm). Todd, October 24: this one "is used a lot." Ration it.
6. **Caught looking, and what each does next.** "He'd been watching my mouth for the three." (7); "I did not look away… I let her see that too." (8). Vary who looks away. Cal holding the look is growth; Jo holding it is a decision.
7. **Speech changes.** Jo always has a comeback, so having none says a lot. Cal's sentences shorten, the presenting voice drops, or he says one true thing too plainly. Losing the thread mid-sentence is the classic tell (Writers Helping Writers).
8. **Replay and keeping.** Rereading the message "a third time" (5); the card riding in a shirt pocket "every day since the bar" (4). Keeping reminders of the person is one of the strongest cues (Novlr). Let these objects pile up and change.
9. **Building the case against him.** Resistance shows as fault-finding and rationalizing. Jo builds a case against Vili's read and finds the case was against herself (7). She hunts for his piano and likes the guess "with an end date" (7). Use it when she's fighting it.
10. **Two feelings at once.** Pair the want with irritation, wariness or a grudge, so the reader can't predict the next beat (Maass's microtension). "Somebody had thought that through. I didn't want to like him for it." (3). This is the most reliable way to keep a beat from going soft.
11. **Witnesses.** Others see what she won't say. Vili: "You laughed… Manny thought somebody got hurt." (7); "The yard would have it by Monday." (7). Denise, Owen, Frank and Janice can all carry this. It's useful in Cal's chapters, where Janice sees what he doesn't.
12. **Self-consciousness.** The closet, with the dress taken off once and put back on (9). Checking her hands for tape gum. Cal moving the card shirt to shirt, "which I would rather she had not learned" (4). It shows the person cares how they're seen.
13. **Borrowing the other's words or habits.** Mirroring is a long-term cue. Cal says Jo's "Everybody hides things" as his own (34). Use it more in the back half: Jo squaring something, Cal asking "green or blue."
14. **The specific compliment.** A compliment only this man would give this woman. "I did not say anything about it at your door. Nothing I had was good enough." (9). No "beautiful."
15. **Inventing reasons to be near.** The three lunches, the bakery in Little Portugal, calling to ask what Vili eats (7). Finding pretexts is a basic attraction behavior (One Stop for Writers). It works best when the other one sees through it.

## Match the cue to where they are

The usual progression runs: presence, eye contact, talk, first touch, sustained touch, kiss, hand to face, and on to the sex scenes (Storytelling DB, "12 levels of intimacy"). Before the kiss in 9, lean on 1 to 7, 9, 10 and 12. After it, use 8, 11, 13 and 15, plus touch that has a history (her palm, his ears).

## Picking options

- Draw from at least two different numbers above, and none from the last two pages.
- Tie the cue to their trade or habits. Jo has tape, counts, the clipboard, the truck, estimates and the crew. Cal has the card, squaring, the watch, the pitch voice, Janice and Remy.
- The cue should reveal the noticer as well as the noticed.
- Don't reuse a bit from the last few pages (Todd, 5:12 PM: "we can't use the same bit everytime").
