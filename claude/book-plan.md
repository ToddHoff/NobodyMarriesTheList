## Premise
Cal Brennan, an AI-software billionaire, asks his own product to read his history and describe his perfect wife. He agrees with all twelve lines and hands the list to a matchmaker. The matchmaker sends him Jo Silveira, 44, a mover's daughter who clears dead people's houses and fails eleven lines. The magic happens for him on the first date. The book is him learning to appreciate her as she is, which for this man means loving her without managing, buying or delegating.

Logline (leads with the list, 2026-10-07): when the woman a matchmaker sends him fails eleven of the twelve lines on his list and he still cannot end the date, a man who pays other people to do his loving has to show up himself, ten Saturdays in his dead mother's house with her in the room, or lose her and his daughter the way he lost his mother.

## Version 3 rules: a romance on every page
- The question for Cal: can a man who has outsourced every act of love learn to show up himself? His product remembers, Janice writes, the matchmaker finds, money solves.
- The question for Jo: can a woman who has made herself impossible to owe let someone carry something for her?
- The engine is unchanged (is he his history?). His record is how the reader measures whether he has learned to show up.
- The list is hidden structure. No parts, no chapter headings, no line owed per meeting. Lines come out when they would in life; they start funny and end painful.
- Opens on the first date. They share the page in 35 of 36 chapters; the one apart (33) is about him.
- He does not fall in love on the first date; he knows only that he did not want it to end. Jo is attracted at once and refuses to trust it.
- He takes her no. She turns down a second date and he accepts it, offers to take the job to another company, and does not ask again (ch 3, sharpened 2026-10-04 after an outside critique). She reopens it herself in ch 7. The Sunnyvale job is her idea, raised on the first date, so he meets her at the house as a client she invited.
- No other women on the page. He cancels the other introductions on the drive home.
- The mortgage breaks a promise: in chapter 17 she makes him promise never to pay for anything of hers without asking. He does it anyway, out of fear, as month four closes in.
- The black moment is about being known. He thought the money proved he loved her; she takes it as proof he never knew her.
- Her silence is self-protection. She does not trust herself to speak without giving something up, and she knows nothing about his mother's silences. His growth is learning her silence is not his to solve.
- The breakup keeps them in one room: her father's name is on the contract, so she finishes the job.
- Chapters end on a change between them (a decision, a touch, a refusal, a promise), not a cliffhanger.
- The last chapter proves the change: a problem he cannot delegate or buy, handled himself.
- The middle is fun. Every chapter from 4 to 24 has a laugh that comes from the two of them: boots, cue card, forty gray shirts, horseradish, dog rankings, wrong lunch, terrible breakfast, parking ticket, missing wallet, whale boat.
- Two absences, and her share (third critique, 2026-10-04): Kevin withdraws when things get hard and Cal takes over. Jo's part is that she never asks. The ending needs both changes: he stays without fixing (ch 34) and she asks (ch 35). When she stops talking in ch 29 she is doing to Cal what Kevin did to her.
- Abby has her own life. She sees through him now and then; she is not his therapist or his redemption.
- Side characters need a lead in the room. Backstory is told to the other lead or it waits. Attraction in every chapter, said outright in the narrator's body and never left to be inferred (Todd on chapter 1: it was not clear until it was said). Jo's "reasons not to" are a running joke, not a rule (staff, forty shirts, his face on a billboard). The agent is always running and is on the page only when it changes something.
- What they say: Cal goes "What's the ask?" (ch 1) → "What do you need?" → "What do you want?" → "Tell me." (ch 34); his two middle lines still need chapters. Jo goes "I've got it." (ch 1) → "I can do it." (ch 17) → "Can you?" (ch 25) → "I need you to stay." (ch 35).
- Jo's ladder (fourth critique, 2026-10-07). She changes on the page before chapter 35, one rung at a time, and each rung is an act. Ch 7: she cannot ask, so she bills him a dinner. Ch 17: "I can do it," and then she lets him make the breakfast. Ch 25: "Can you?", the first thing she asks him for that is not a bill. Ch 27: "I've got it" costs somebody else, her father. Ch 35: "I need you to stay." Ch 36: her name for him on Owen's emergency card. She is never his instructor; she is on her own ladder.
- Cal wins once on her ground (2026-10-07). In her world he gets everything wrong, which is the comedy. Once, in ch 25, the thing he knows cold helps her, she asks for it, and no money is involved. It makes ch 28 worse.
- The plan fires where the reader can see it (2026-10-07). The reader has known since ch 6 that fourteen items are scheduled. In the good weeks they go off: the car in ch 16, the month-two weekend in ch 20. Each time he removes the one item and does not open the plan. The count only comes down.
- No secret survives chapter 33 (2026-10-07). His letter tells her she was filed as relationship twelve, that he approved a plan unread, and that it had a goodbye already written.
- The title is paid in the epilogue (2026-10-07). The cue card is a proposal.
- Counts end (2026-10-07). Her reasons stop at the first kiss in ch 9. His "noted" is counted for the last time by ch 15 and comes back once, on the epilogue card.
- What each stretch answers: ch 1-9 is there something between them; 10-15 can she trust who is speaking to her; 16-24 can their lives fit together; 25-29 can he stop using money as love; 30-35 can she accept being loved without owing; 36 can they have trouble without turning into their parents.
- Milestones: meet ch 1; almost-kiss ch 7 (19%); first kiss ch 9 (25%); first love scene ch 18 (50%); second ch 21 (58%); he says it ch 26 (72%); black moment ch 29 (80%); she speaks ch 34 (94%); she says it ch 35 (97%); third love scene ch 35.
- First person, past tense, alternating Jo (odd chapters) and Cal (even). About 85,000 words, 36 chapters near 2,300 words, an epilogue. Heat: open door, middle.

## The engine: the man and his record (canon)
Dramatic question: is Cal his history, or can he do something it did not predict? The first date is an anomaly ("no prior instances") and he wants to be that man again. Every wrong move is the default his agent offers; he only has to approve. His pattern has a date: eleven relationships, four months on average, each ending within two weeks of a large gift. He cannot change by deciding; the agent records only what he does. Remy is used the way people use these tools now (Todd, 2026-10-04): Cal talks to it, reads it on a screen, and has it make songs, videos and drafts. What it makes is polished and could be for anyone. It can sound warm, and it is never wise: it reports, predicts and produces, and has no view on what is good.
Remy never stops running (Todd, 2026-10-04): it is a long-running agent. It works for Cal around the clock, keeps a plan going for months, and comes back to him only when it needs a yes; he stopped reading what he approves years ago.
- The plan: built from his eleven relationships. Messages on a schedule, a car, a weekend in Napa in month two (where the other eleven went), a large gift at month four. With Jo it starts run twelve. Janice writes the words; Remy keeps the schedule.
- It asks and he says yes. Remy never acts against him; it proposes or carries on, and he agrees or fails to stop it. Every wrong move is still his.
- Doing nothing has to be chosen: a running plan must be stopped, and Remy tells him what stopping cancels.
- It does not watch Jo. It works from what is his (calendar, accounts, what he tells it) and what anyone can see (her booking page, her reviews).
- It delivers the promise: it does what the big agents say they will do, runs for months, works across everything he owns, and gets it right. No magic: it schedules, books, sends, pays, builds and makes things, and never understands any of it.
- The second list: what Remy may do for him in his private life. He takes it back item by item and hands one back at the worst moment. Ch 2 the end of an evening (car and exit call at minute 45; he turns the phone face down) → ch 14 his words to Jo → ch 16 planning a date → ch 25 fixing things for her (stops the review cleanup, asks first, she says no) → ch 28 he hands one back (gives Remy her loan number for the month-four gift) → ch 30 the plan ("Pause everything for Jo Silveira": what is left of the fourteen, the last a goodbye already drafted) and his mother's voice (plays the voicemail) → ch 34 his family (for Abby, Jo and Owen, Remy does nothing unless he asks; it still runs the company) → ch 36 the proof (the school calls and nothing is queued).
Ladder: ch 2 the car and exit call at minute 45, then the log of the anomaly → he turns the phone face down, cancels introductions and has Remy book the job she offered. Ch 5-10 Janice's messages as approval requests → he approves nightly. Ch 6 "Relationship 12. Average duration: 4.1 months," and an offer to start the plan → he says yes without reading it. Ch 16 a car he did not order is at her curb → he removes that one item ("No cars for Jo Silveira." "Removed. Thirteen items remain. Review?" "Later."). Ch 20 month two, the usual weekend in Napa, hold expires Friday → Abby sees it; he cancels that one too, twelve remain, and he still does not open the plan. Ch 25 an offer to remove junk reviews and a contract pre-filled from the last three → asks her about the first and takes her no; approves the second unread. Ch 26 how the eleven ended → closes the screen. Ch 28 month four, the gift, and what he did in 2012 → does it again with the loan number from her counter; Janice refuses, he confirms it himself. Ch 30 a new list that is Jo → closes without saving. Ch 30-33 "Unanswered: 9 days." → sends nothing, shows up Saturdays. Ch 34 "No precedent found," the agent's last words.

**Why Remy won (Todd, 2026-10-04).** Remy is the fulfillment of what the big companies' agents promise: always on, runs for months, works across everything a person owns, and gets things right. Their agents have no face and no taste, and nobody loves them. Remy delivered the promise and Cal made it cool, the way Steve Jobs made a computer something people wanted to be seen with. No real company or person is named in the book.
- Cal is the brand: good-looking, well dressed, at ease in front of four thousand people, known by one gray shirt.
- He sold a feeling: he never says "agent" on a stage; he sells what the time is for. Remy's best-known ad is three words, "Call your mother" (confirmed by Todd). The pitch behind it: Remy does the rest, so you can show up for your people.
- He is the one user who got his own product backwards: he let Remy do the people too and gave the time to work, and his mother's last voicemail is unplayed. The book ends with him using Remy the way his ad says to.
- He used it on the way up: investors, press, hires and the women in the photographs. The first four lines of the list are the brand's taste.
- The gap is the book: he can hold a hall and cannot hold a table for two. Jo is the first person to see the cue card.
- Jo is not sold: attracted at once, and distrusts it for that reason. She hears him switch to the stage voice and calls it presenting.
- Cal has two voices: the private one (specs, numbers, "Noted.") and the stage one (warm, fluent, rehearsed).
- Proposed by Claude, not yet confirmed: two reasons Remy could deliver what the others could not. It had ten years of people's history before the models arrived, so it remembers where the big agents forget; and it asks first, which is why people trust it with everything.

## The first date (chapters 1 and 2)
Jo has driven under his face on a billboard on 101 for years; it says "Call your mother." She walks in knowing three more things: he is worth eleven billion dollars, he has a list, and she is not on it. Twenty minutes late, work boots, clean shirt from the truck; she cannot win so she does not audition. The man from the billboard is reading Janice's cue card under the table; she corrects it in pen. She asks for one line; he gives height; she stands up to be measured. Why she was late: a widow, a recliner, $3,100 in a dead man's coat. His last introduction asked if he would rehome his dog; Jo is afraid of dogs since a bite at 26 and would never ask that; he ended that date over it, which is how the reader knows what the dog is to him. The casserole dish; she says the house needs clearing and to call her office, which is a job and not a date. Abby got in off the waitlist; his eyes fill; he blames the horseradish; she passes a napkin and asks what Abby wants to build. At minute 45 Remy does what it does on every date: the car is outside and his phone rings with a reason to leave. Jo asks if that gets him out of all of them; every one so far, he says, and turns the phone face down. Three hours; Remy tries once more, tells Janice, and Janice phones the bar. She leaves first; she would like to stay, and that is why she goes. His agent: two missed calls, no prior instances. He does not think the word love; he knows he did not want the evening to end. From the car he cancels the other introductions and tells Remy to book the job she offered. Next morning (ch 3) she turns down a second date and he accepts that; her office scheduler shows an estimate booked at 11:52 the night before, and at 7 a.m. he is on the sidewalk in front of his mother's house in Sunnyvale, with his back to it. He cannot go in, she will not walk a house without its owner, and she prices the job from the curb. The book ends in the same bar a year later.

## Voice rules
AI tells to keep out of the prose (Todd, 2026-10-04): the word "plainly" and a narrator announcing what he is about to say; a run of "the way X does Y" similes (two or three a chapter at most); "longer than it needed" more than once; a statement followed by its own correction ("The bar went quiet. It hadn't."); "I'll give him this:" and other colon set-ups; a pair of balanced sentences that sum up a feeling.
Three-line test for whose chapter it is. Jo's vocabulary: loads, straps, stairs, hours, payroll. Cal's: specs, tests, edge cases, confidence. Nobody answers the question asked. One trade detail per scene. Nobody states the theme, the agent least of all: it can talk, sing and flatter, and it never understands. Janice's messages and Cal's own must be distinguishable. Children and old people are never cute. Chapters end on something said or done. No em dashes or the banned phrases.
