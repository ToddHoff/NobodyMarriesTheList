## The book

- **Title:** Nobody Marries the List. **Pen name:** Wendy Garrity. The Neopulp mark goes on the cover and the copyright page.
- **Shelf:** a romantic comedy for the single-mom, later-in-life shelf, not the billionaire shelf. The billionaire reader wants rescue by money, and this book's black moment makes a paid-off mortgage the betrayal.
- **Form:** first person, past tense, alternating Jo (odd chapters) and Cal (even chapters). About 85,000 words: 36 chapters near 2,300 words each, and an epilogue in Jo's voice.
- **Heat:** 3 of 5. Open door, on the page, direct without being graphic. Three love scenes, in chapters 18, 21 and 35.
- **The genre's style governs** (Todd). The book reads like a romance novel on every page. Sentence rules are in claude/romance-style-guide.md; voices are in claude/character-sheet.md.
- **Kindle Unlimited:** the ebook is exclusive to Amazon for each 90-day term. The book opens a series of interconnected standalones around the matchmaker's office.

**Premise.** Cal Brennan, an AI-software billionaire, asks his own product to read his history and describe his perfect wife. He agrees with all twelve lines and hands the list to a matchmaker. The matchmaker sends him Jo Silveira, a 44-year-old mover's daughter who clears dead people's houses for a living and fails eleven of the lines. The magic happens for him on the first date. The book is him learning to love her as she is, which for this man means loving her without managing, buying or delegating, and he has to learn it in a house he cannot walk into alone.

**Logline.** When the woman a matchmaker sends him fails eleven of the twelve lines on his list and he still cannot end the date, a man who pays other people to do his loving has to show up himself, ten Saturdays in his dead mother's house with her in the room, or lose her and his daughter the way he lost his mother.

## What the book is about

- **Cal's question:** can a man who has outsourced every act of love learn to show up himself? His product remembers, Janice writes, the matchmaker finds, and money solves.
- **Jo's question:** can a woman who has made herself impossible to owe let someone carry something for her?
- **The engine:** is Cal his history, or can he do something it did not predict? His record, kept by his own agent, is how the reader measures whether he has learned to show up.
- **The black moment is about being known.** He thought the money proved he loved her. She takes it as proof he never knew her.
- **The last chapter proves the change:** a problem he cannot delegate or buy, handled himself.

## Story rules

**The romance**
- They share the page in 35 of 36 chapters. The one where they are apart is 33, and it is about him.
- He does not fall in love on the first date. He knows only that he did not want it to end. Jo is attracted at once and refuses to trust it.
- He takes her no. She turns down a second date (ch 3) and he accepts it, offers to take the job to another company, and does not ask again. She reopens it herself in ch 7.
- No other women on the page. He cancels the other introductions on the drive home from the first date.
- Attraction in every chapter, said outright in the narrator's body and never left to be inferred (Todd, on the first draft of chapter 1). It changes as they do, without announcing its stages: first she is physically wrong for his list; then she distracts him; then her competence draws him; then her fear and her nerve undo him; at the end he loves the exact things that once disqualified her.
- Every chapter ends on a change between the two of them (a decision, a touch, a refusal, a promise), on something said or done. No cliffhangers, and no closing thought about what it all means.
- The middle is fun. Every chapter from 4 to 24 has a laugh that comes from the two of them: the boots, the cue card, forty gray shirts, the horseradish, the dog rankings, the wrong lunch, the terrible breakfast, the parking ticket, the missing wallet, a billionaire airsick in his own airship.
- Backstory is told to the other lead, or it waits. A side character's scene needs a lead in the room.
- The title is paid in the epilogue: the cue card is a proposal.
- No new machinery from chapter 19 on (outside critique, October 9). Claude adds no new plants or recurring objects while drafting. New ones come from Todd, and where it can, a new idea rides an existing plant. Not every plant has to pay off at the end; the list and the cue card carry the ending, with the mortgage and the piano under them. Every scene has to work as a scene first.

**Cal**
- He is not a bad man and not neurodivergent (Todd). He is smart, focused, driven and more self-absorbed than is good for him. He reads other people fast and well, which is how he raised money and hired; what he does not trust is himself with them, so he assigns it. Each Cal chapter from 12 on has at least one quick, correct read of another person.
- He does not say he has never felt this before when an act already shows it. The acts are the romance: the phone turned over, the card left in the console, the shrimp, the door, the lunch.
- His reach is a pleasure to watch when he asks before he uses it, and each time the easy fix is refused or turns out wrong: the cruise (6, unasked, and it cannot be fixed), the taco truck (8 and 9, asked, and right), the cool house (21, asked and refused), a paid crew for Frank's boat (23, asked and refused), the review cleanup (25, asked and refused). Hawaii (19) is the one splash she says yes to, because it is for Owen and he asked her first. In ch 12 Jo watches his office run on one sentence from him and is impressed against her will. After chapter 6, anything he does for her unasked is either Remy's plan firing (16, 20) or the relapse (28).
- He wins once on her ground (ch 25): the thing he knows cold helps her, she asks for it, and no money is involved. It makes chapter 28 worse.
- He never says sorry without a fix attached, until the end. He has never ended a relationship out loud; see Remy and the plan.

**Jo**
- She is exact and never cruel, in the fight (ch 13) and in the black moment (ch 29). Nothing about his looks, his mother or his daughter. He does not get a clever comeback.
- Her word for the catch she keeps waiting for is "the piano," the thing that is never on the estimate. She never says "the bill" (Todd).
- She is a fool for him once in each Jo chapter through 17, something she would call stupid in anyone else. Ch 11 has two (she checks her hair under his billboard; she kisses a client in a hall with her son thirty feet away). Ch 13 has two (she laughs out loud alone in his garage at "Noted. That is five"; she slides down behind her wheel when his car comes down the ramp). Ch 15 has one (she sends Vili in the other truck so Cal rides beside her). Ch 17 has one (at 2:10 in the morning she stands at his door with her hand up to knock and a sentence ready, and goes back to the guest room).
- Her "reasons not to" were a running joke (staff, forty shirts, his face on a billboard). They ended at eight, at the first kiss in ch 9, and are not counted again.

**The shape of the ending**
- The mortgage breaks a promise. In ch 17 she makes him promise never to pay for anything of hers without asking. In ch 28 he does it anyway, out of fear, as month four closes in.
- Two absences, and her share. Kevin withdraws when things get hard; Cal takes over. Jo's part is that she never asks. The ending needs both changes: he stays without fixing (ch 34) and she asks (ch 35). When she stops talking in ch 29 she is doing to Cal what Kevin did to her.
- Her silence is self-protection. She does not trust herself to speak without giving something up, and she knows nothing about his mother's silences. His growth is learning that her silence is not his to solve.
- The breakup keeps them in one room: her father's name is on the contract, so she finishes the job.
- No secret survives chapter 33. His letter tells her she was filed as relationship twelve, that he approved a plan unread, that the car and Napa were that plan, and that the plan stopped at the month-four gift because none of the other eleven lasted longer.
- **How dark it gets** (Todd, October 8). The black moment is as dark as the book goes: love in the wrong currency, a broken promise, and something taken that was hers. Remy is cold; Cal is careless, never cruel. Nothing in the plan was written to end things with Jo, and none of the other eleven was ended by a message.
- Abby has her own life. She sees through him now and then; she is not his therapist and not his redemption. The verdict that he was "a really good account manager" comes from Abby, never from Jo.

**The counts**
- His "noted" was counted for the last time in chapter 15, at seven. It comes back once, on the epilogue card.
- Lines struck from the list: six after chapter 14, seven after chapter 21 (see His list). He owes her half a linguiça sandwich and no lines.
- Janice's pauses: she has paused before answering him eight times in seventeen years. Chapter 2 counts from four to six, the seventh is in ch 10 and the eighth in ch 12. From chapter 16 on there are no running counts on the page except the lines Remy says itself ("Thirteen items remain," "Unanswered: 9 days," "No precedent found"); Cal still notices in numbers when the number is the joke or the fact. The reader should forget the machinery for twenty pages at a time, so that "No precedent found" lands.

## The cast

### Jo Silveira, 44

Owner of Silveira Moving & Estate in South San Francisco: eleven employees, three trucks (one is the sixteen-footer she keeps back for estimates and small jobs), and her father's name on the doors. She lives in San Bruno. She is pretty and has stopped thinking about it: dark hair tied back for work, brown eyes, a wide mouth, sun on her skin, no makeup. Cal says so outright; in her own chapters it shows in how men look at her. On a job the carpenter's pencil lives behind her ear. Divorced three years; custody of Owen week on, week off. Two years at Skyline College, business, before the trucks needed her (ch 14).

- **Wants:** to make payroll, get Owen through fourth grade, and own her house outright by 55 on her own spreadsheet.
- **Under it:** someone to take the heavy end without sending a bill.
- **Wound:** the winter she was eight (1990), her father hurt his knee on a job, and the one truck sat until spring. The church and the neighbors fed the family. A man from the parish paid the truck note for three months, and everybody at church knew who. Her job was to wash the casserole dishes and take them back. Each had its owner's name on a strip of masking tape on the bottom. She wrote every name down, her first found sheet, and she has never told anyone about the list. When Frank came back he carried everything himself. Her rule comes from that winter: everything anybody gives you has a name on the bottom. Kevin's parents were the second time. They gave the down payment and never let them forget it, every Sunday for eleven years. In 2023 she refinanced at seven percent to buy Kevin out and paid his parents back with interest nobody asked for, the way she took back the dishes washed. (The winter is Claude's, adopted by Todd on October 8, after "That can't be Jo's. She was that way before. Why?") The marriage ended in Kevin's silences, and in her never asking him for anything; in eleven years she asked for one thing, a signature.
- **Rule:** nothing given is free.
- **Knows:** families fight over the casserole dish, never the silver. Cash is in the freezer, the Bible and the coat pockets. The low man calls the lift (her father's rule, not the trade's). A job done too fast gets a worse tip. Every found item is called out loud, seen by a second person, written on the found sheet and signed for.
- **Quirk:** she takes nothing home from a job. Her purse holds a tape measure, green tape and a carpenter's pencil.
- **Talks:** short statements, logistics verbs, practical questions as affection. She says "I've got it" and never "I need" until chapter 35. When a subject has to close, she asks a work question.
- **Afraid of dogs:** bitten on a job at 26, eleven stitches in the back of her left calf. She says "I'm not good with dogs" and makes room for this one anyway.
- **Secret:** she broke her own rule once. A recipe tin from a stranger's kitchen sits in her cupboard because it looked like her mother's, which she threw out by mistake.
- **Confidante:** her sister Denise.

### Cal Brennan, 41

Founder of Recall Technologies, whose agent, Remy, runs the work and the errands of forty million paying users. In public he is good-looking, well dressed and cool, the face of his product, and he used all of it on the way up. In private he is smart, focused, driven and more self-absorbed than is good for him; keeping track of people was the first thing he stopped doing, which is why he built Remy and why he pays a person to remember for him. Raised in Sunnyvale by Darlene, in an old Eichler on West Remington Drive; she worked the line at Libby's cannery until it closed in 1985, the year he was born, and then the cafeteria at the middle school across the street for thirty years, where she handed him his tray. Married at 22, a father at 23, divorced at 27. Lives in Woodside. One daughter, Abby. One dog, Pancake.

- **Wants:** a wife in place before Abby leaves, so the house is not empty.
- **Under it:** to be forgiven by Abby without having to ask, and to be again the man he was for three hours on the first date.
- **Wound:** when his mother was hurt she stopped speaking, sometimes for nine days. What ended it was a report card. He would leave it on the kitchen table, she would read it and sign it, and she would start talking again. (She asked what he got on every test and read every report card before she signed it, ch 14; she had left school at sixteen and hid it.) So he learned that the right number gets someone to speak to you. His times to the minute, his counts, specs and tests, the forty shirts, the list and Remy all start there. He has never told anyone about the report cards either. (The report cards are Claude's, adopted by Todd on October 8.) He learned to answer feelings with deliverables. He paid for everything and attended nothing. He has never told anyone about the nine days.
- **Rule:** if it matters, assign it to someone good.
- **Knows:** how a model fails: fluent, confident and wrong. He trusts a spec and a test.
- **Quirk:** he tests and returns things until he finds the one he loves, then buys forty: thirty-one shirts tried, forty of the gray one bought (numbered one to forty, by hand, in ch 14). He is doing this with women and has not noticed.
- **Talks:** whole sentences, never fragments, and no contractions (Voice rules); numbers and readings; "What is the ask?" and "Noted." He has a second voice for the stage, warm, fluent and rehearsed, and when he is nervous with Jo he slips into it; she calls it presenting. His watch taps when his pulse is high.
- **Secret:** his mother's last voicemail, unplayed. He has read only Remy's summary of it.

### Why Remy won

Remy is what the big companies' agents promise: always on, runs for months, works across everything a person owns, and gets things right. Their agents have no face and no taste, and nobody loves them. Remy delivered the promise and Cal made it cool, the way Steve Jobs made a computer something people wanted to be seen with. No real company or person is named in the book.

- **Cal is the brand.** At ease in front of four thousand people, known by one gray shirt.
- **He sold a feeling.** He never says "agent" on a stage; he sells what the time is for. Remy's best-known ad is three words, "Call your mother" (Todd), on a billboard over 101 that Jo drives under. The pitch: Remy does the rest, so you can show up for your people.
- **He got his own product backwards.** He let Remy do the people too and gave the time to work, and his mother's last voicemail is unplayed. The book ends with him using Remy the way his ad says to.
- **He used it on the way up:** investors, press, hires and the women in the photographs. The first four lines of the list are the brand's taste.
- **The gap is the book.** He can hold a hall and cannot hold a table for two. Jo is the first person to see the cue card.
- Claude's addition, not confirmed: two reasons Remy could deliver what the others could not. It had ten years of people's history before the models arrived, so it remembers where the big agents forget; and it asks first, which is why people trust it with everything.

## Remy and the plan

Remy is a long-running agent. It works for Cal around the clock, keeps a plan going for months, and comes back to him only when it needs a yes; he stopped reading what he approves in 2019 (three hundred approvals a day). Cal talks to it, reads it on a screen, and has it make songs, videos and drafts. What it makes is polished and could be for anyone. It can sound warm, and it is never wise: it reports, predicts and produces, and has no view on what is good. Cal calls it "it"; users say "he" or "she"; Janice says "Remy thinks"; Jo says "your robot."

- **It asks, and he says yes.** Remy never acts against him. It proposes, or carries on with what he approved, and he agrees or fails to stop it. Every wrong move is still his.
- **Doing nothing has to be chosen.** A running plan has to be stopped, and Remy tells him what stopping cancels.
- **It does not watch Jo.** It works from what is his (calendar, accounts, what he tells it) and what anyone can see (her booking page, her reviews). Her office runs on the cheapest Remy plan, which Denise set up; his Remy booked the first estimate with hers.
- **It delivers the promise and has no magic.** It schedules, books, sends, pays, builds and makes things, and never understands any of it. In the good weeks (17 to 24) it is off the page except for the sleep report (18), Owen's song (19) and the Napa card (20).
- **Four settings:** do it; do it if he has said yes before; ask first; leave it to him. Money always comes back to him to confirm. He walks the people he loves down them: in ch 16 he sets everything for Jo to ask first, and in ch 34 he sets Abby, Jo and Owen to leave it to him.
- **His record.** The first date is an anomaly ("no prior instances"), and he wants to be that man again. Every wrong move after it is the default his agent offers. He cannot change by deciding, because the agent records only what he does.

**The plan for relationship twelve.** Remy built it from his eleven relationships, which lasted 4.1 months on average. It offers it in chapter 6 and he says yes without reading it.
- It holds fourteen items: the set pieces of a courtship as his history ran it, among them a car at her curb, the month-two weekend in Napa (where the other eleven went) and a large gift at month four. The count only comes down: fourteen (ch 6), thirteen when he removes the car (ch 16), twelve when he cancels Napa (ch 20). From ch 16 nothing in the plan reaches her without his yes: the items between come to him one at a time as questions, and he turns each down without opening the plan. He will not open it because it would show him his history laid out, the 4.1 months, and he does not want to read what it predicts. The month-four gift is the last item (ch 28), and that one he says yes to.
- **The plan ends at the gift.** There is no item after it, because none of the eleven lasted to a month five. Remy never drafted a goodbye, and nothing in the plan was written to end things with Jo (Todd, October 8).
- **The nightly messages are not one of the fourteen items.** They are Janice's standing job. Remy keeps a list of the people she writes to for him (forty-one names); Jo's went on it May 16, and he takes it off on June 17 (ch 14).
- **How the other eleven ended.** Nobody ended them out loud. Each ended within two weeks of a large gift: the woman drifted off, and he let her go quiet, the way he took his mother's silences. That is why "Doesn't go quiet" is the true line, why Janice says he always gives a present before the end, and why Jo's nine days of silence in chapter 31 are the test. This time he keeps showing up.

## Voice rules

The style guide (claude/romance-style-guide.md) has the full sentence rules, including Todd's October 8 rule on narration. These are the rules for keeping people on the page.
- **The three-line test.** Cover the chapter heading; a reader should know whose chapter it is within three lines.
- **Each lead has a vocabulary.** Jo thinks in loads, stairs, hours and payroll. Cal thinks in specs, tests, edge cases and confidence. Neither borrows the other's words until late; the first time Cal says "call it" unprompted is a beat. Strap imagery is limited to Vili's one speech in ch 7 and the lines already on the page (Todd).
- **Each has a sentence they cannot say.** Jo never says "I need." Cal never says sorry without a fix attached. Each says it once, near the end.
- **Cal speaks in whole sentences, with no contractions** (Todd). His narration has no contractions either. The short lines he may say are yes, no, "Noted," "Understood," "All right," "Thank you," a greeting or a name, a pick between options he was just offered, and a line of his list read aloud. The texts he types himself have no contractions either. Every other character uses contractions.
- **Nobody answers the question asked.** People deflect, mishear and change the subject. Kevin answers every question with a schedule.
- **Every scene has a third person who wants something.** Vili wants his Saturday. Owen wants the dog. Janice wants the thermostat on an even number.
- **One trade detail per scene,** used and never explained.
- **Nobody states the theme,** the agent least of all: it can talk, sing and flatter, and it never understands.
- **The texts have two authors.** Janice names the flowers and remembers the sister, with one semicolon and a practical last line. Cal writes four words with a number in them. The reader can tell them apart before Jo can. No new Janice messages after chapter 13.
- **The calls always include a lead.** Dawn asks questions. Kelsey says "to be fair" and gives a number.
- **Children and old people are never cute.** Owen negotiates. Frank judges. Both are usually right.
- **Chapters end on something said or done.**
- **AI tells to keep out** (Todd): "plainly" and a narrator announcing what he is about to say; a run of "the way X does Y" similes (two or three a chapter at most); "longer than it needed" more than once; a statement followed by its own correction; "I'll give him this:" and other colon set-ups; a pair of balanced sentences that sum up a feeling; no em dashes.
- **Lines an outside reader asked to protect,** all on the page: "It is not a penalty. It is arithmetic." "I've been short my whole life. I own six ladders." "Somebody decided I did. I have never had one." "This is a table." "I had nothing in fourteen years to file that under." "It was a kind thing. I'm not telling you it wasn't. Next time, ask." "I wanted to find out what I would say." "You know how a load sounds when it's wrong." "It's strapped, boss."

## Kindle Unlimited and positioning

- **What a read is worth.** Kindle Unlimited pays by pages read. The rate was [$0.004888 a page in May 2026 and averaged $0.00445 in 2025](https://authorsgame.com/price-and-royalties/kindle-unlimited-kenp-page-read-economics); plan at $0.004, which puts an 80,000-word novel at $1.60 to $2.20 for a full read. One book rarely pays back; the series is the business case.
- **The 15% line.** Readers who pass 15% finish at much higher rates. The book opens on the first date, and it is complete by the end of chapter 2.
- **Series.** Interconnected standalones are [the most popular series structure in romance](https://fractalenigma.com/best-romance-series-kindle-unlimited-binge-2026/): the matchmaker's office, one client and one list per book. Dawn and Danny, Kelsey, and Frank (a widower on his first date in decades) are set up for their own books.
- **Back matter.** The first chapter of the next book after the epilogue, and a bonus scene for newsletter sign-ups.
- **Lead with Jo.** A 44-year-old woman with her own company and a child is the reader this book is for.
- **Cal on the cover copy.** Good-looking, well dressed and cool in public, warm on a stage; not cold, not a villain, not neurodivergent. Famous from keynotes, magazine covers and a billboard on 101, with no reporters, paparazzi or security in the book.
- **Cover:** illustrated, her truck under his billboard. The blurb and the first page show the cue card. The logline and blurb lead with the list: twelve lines, and she fails eleven. The black moment in one sentence, for pitches: he paid off the mortgage she had spent years planning to pay off herself. The blurb makes three promises: she fails eleven of his twelve lines; he falls first and keeps coming back; the gesture he thinks will prove his love is the act that nearly ends it (critique, October 9).
- **Keywords, in order:** he falls first, single mom, later in life, class difference, matchmaker; billionaire is secondary. **Avoid:** reverse age gap (three years is not what those readers want) and second chance (it means reunited exes).
- **Love scenes carry the arc:** managing in the first, present without controlling in the last.
