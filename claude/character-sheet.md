## How to use this sheet

Check a draft against the voice lists here before anything else. Each lead also has the fields your craft notes ask for: a visible want and a hidden one that pull against each other, a wound kept off the page, a flaw that looks like a virtue, a rule they never explain, a piece of working knowledge, a secret, and how they act under pressure.

Most of it comes from the book plan and the two drafted chapters. Anything marked (new) is my proposal to fill a gap, and you can strike it.

## Jo Silveira, 44

Jo owns Silveira Moving & Estate in South San Francisco: eleven employees, three trucks, her father's name on the doors since 1987. She has been divorced three years, has Owen every other week, and lives in San Bruno. She had Owen at 35 ("I kept meaning to get to it"). She is three years older than Cal, on purpose. She narrates the odd chapters.

### How Jo sounds

- Short statements with numbers, sizes, hours and dollars in them.
- She measures by eye and says the measurement. The painting is sixteen feet.
- Her comparisons come from the job: a truck topping a hill, a couch through a doorway, what a customer hears when the truck is full. One at a time, and never to decorate.
- Affection comes out as a practical question. "What does she want to build?"
- She does not console. She says the true thing and gives the person something to do. She corrects what is in front of her and never explains a person to himself.
- She keeps count: his slips against the cue card, her reasons not to like him and which column each goes in. The reasons stop at the first kiss in chapter 9, at eight.
- She says "I've got it." She never says "I need" until chapter 35.
- She cannot ask for a thing, so she charges for it. The dinner in chapter 7 is a bill: "it costs you two lines."
- Her word for a catch is "the piano." She does not talk about cost or price in the abstract; that is Cal's register, and she is the one who sends the bills.
- She closes a subject with a work question ("Kitchen." "Thirty-six more." Whether the four-wheel dolly is on the truck).
- Out loud she lists what she did and answers in a word. She does not sum up, build a contrast or finish on a line.
- Her jokes are facts said flat. "I own six ladders."
- She does not always answer. Sometimes she looks at him, or says "Huh," and goes back to work.
- She never describes her own looks. Other people's reactions do it.

Lines to tune by:

- "I had a widow and a recliner. Neither one would move."
- "Back to who? It was hers."
- "He was there first. You go stand where the dog isn't."
- "It's my father's. I pay for the paint."
- "I go through houses for a living, Vili. I figured I could go through one week."

## Cal Brennan, 41

Cal founded Recall Technologies, whose agent, Remy, runs the work and errands of forty million paying users. He is worth eleven billion dollars. His mother, Darlene, raised him in Sunnyvale, in an old Eichler on West Remington Drive, near Bernardo. He married at 22, was a father at 23 and divorced at 27; at 23 he had a company of four people and thought he was behind. He lives in Woodside and narrates the even chapters.

### How Cal sounds in his own head

- Complete sentences, with times to the minute, counts and odds. He is never vague about when or how a thing started.
- He reports feelings as readings. His watch tells him his pulse. He notes that he did not want her to go.
- He reports what he sees and what his hands do. He does not tally what someone did not do, gloss a tone, or sum up his own state.
- He uses product words on people: test condition, variable, specified. "It had never loaded." "I had nothing in fourteen years to file that under." He does not use her trade words; he has booked an estimate, so that one he has.
- He counts what other people do not: Janice's pauses, the minute a date asked about his dog.
- He is funny and does not know it. The reader laughs and he does not.
- He admits what he does not know in four words. "I don't know. They were chosen."
- He does not think the word love before chapter 26.
- Out loud he says "What's the ask?" and "Noted." He has never said sorry without a fix attached. He is literal about instructions: told "next time, ask," he asks the next time.

### The stage voice

- Warmer, lower and slower. He asks a stranger her father's name and gives her a sentence to take home.
- He slips into it when he is nervous with Jo, and she tells him he is presenting. The reader hears it in his own words, aloud; it is never summarized.
- "That's a room. This is a table. I'm good at rooms."
- "We don't sell software. We sell an hour."

Lines to tune by:

- "It isn't a penalty. It's arithmetic."
- "At minute thirty-one."
- "It's the horseradish."
- "Eleven of the twelve are wrong. When is she free?"
- "On Tuesday you said next time, ask. This was next time."

## Remy

Remy is the always-on agent the big companies promised, and it never understands anything it does. Treat it as a voice with strict limits.

On a screen:

- A header with its name and the time, then short lines of counts and times.
- It ends on a question he can answer with one word. "Confirm?"
- "Tonight: scheduled 45 min. Actual 3 hr 05 min." "Prior instances: none."

Out loud:

- A pleasant voice. They tested forty.
- One short sentence at a time. "Done." "Booked." "All three?"
- It tells him what it did and what happens next, and stops.

Who calls it what: Cal says "it," always, because he built it and knows what it is. His users say "he" or "she" in about equal numbers (the woman at the bar says "she"). Janice says "Remy thinks." Jo says "your robot" and "that thing."

What it never does:

- It never says what is good, wise or right.
- It never says "I think" or "I feel."
- It never acts against him. It proposes, or carries on with what he approved, and he agrees or fails to stop it.
- It never reads Jo's mail or tracks her. It works from what is his and what anyone can see.
- It never moves money without him confirming.

Its four settings for anything it might do: do it, do it if he has said yes before, ask first, leave it to him. The book ends with Abby, Jo and Owen set to the last one.

What it makes is polished and could be for anyone: a song about a dog, a birthday video, an apology in his own voice.

The plan he approved unread in chapter 6 has fourteen items, and the count only comes down. A car he did not order comes for her in chapter 16 ("Removed. Thirteen items remain. Review?" "Later."). The usual month-two weekend in Napa comes up in chapter 20 and he cancels it; twelve remain. He pauses what is left in chapter 30.

Lines it says over the book: "Relationship 12. Average duration: 4.1 months." "Unanswered: 9 days." "No precedent found."

## Darlene and Pancake

Darlene Brennan is never on the page alive, so her house has to speak for her. She died on October 8 last year, at 71. She worked the line at Libby's cannery in Sunnyvale until it closed in 1985, and then for thirty years the cafeteria line at the middle school across the street, where she handed Cal his tray while he was a student, and she hid that she left school at sixteen.

What is in her house:

- Picture frames that still hold the stock photos they were sold with.
- A sugar bowl with pebbles under the sugar, so it always looked full.
- Every gift he ever sent, unopened, with the receipts.
- 156 envelopes, each holding $612 and marked "Calvin, house."
- Three boxes that came after she died, kept by the neighbor.

When she was hurt she stopped speaking, sometimes for nine days. Her last voicemail is on his phone.

Pancake is his dog: a cream golden retriever (Todd, 2026-10-05), eleven years old, sixty-four pounds, named by Abby when she was seven. He can no longer manage the last three steps, so Cal carries him. He inspects the same fence post every morning. He leans on the one person in a room who would rather he didn't. He lives.

Cal loves this dog and shows it only in what he does: the walk at five, carrying him up the steps and telling no one, letting him ride with his chin on his shoulder, watering him from his hands. He ended the one date that asked him to give the dog up. Jo is not a woman who dislikes dogs. She was bitten at 26 and is afraid of them, says "I'm not good with dogs," and makes room for this one anyway (Todd, 2026-10-05).
