## How to use this sheet

Check a draft against the voice lists here before anything else. Each lead also has the fields your craft notes ask for: a visible want and a hidden one that pull against each other, a wound kept off the page, a flaw that looks like a virtue, a rule they never explain, a piece of working knowledge, a secret, and how they act under pressure.

Most of it comes from the book plan and the drafted chapters (1 to 18). Anything marked (new) is my proposal to fill a gap, and you can strike it.

### How Jo sounds, added

- She cannot ask for a thing, so she charges for it. The dinner in chapter 7 is a bill: "it costs you two lines."
- Her word for a catch is "the piano," the thing that is never on the estimate. She does not talk about cost or price in the abstract; that is Cal's register.
- She closes a subject with a work question ("Kitchen." "Thirty-six more." Whether the four-wheel dolly is on the truck).
- Out loud she lists what she did and answers in a word. She does not sum up, build a contrast or finish on a line.
- That short answer is for when she is guarding herself. When she is at ease she has more to say. It is the reason he loves her (Todd, October 8: "Let's try to give her more personality than just the shortest possible sentences. We have to have a reason he loves her too."). She teases him ("So he's got his pick of five."; "Even in Woodside."), tells a job story with a number in it when he asks (the twenty-six cans of paint), jokes at her own expense (Owen says she would put him on the found sheet if he sat still long enough), asks for his eggs the way she asked for his shrimp ("Are you going to eat those?"), and says the true thing to his face ("I've been sitting in your driveway working up my nerve."). She makes him laugh.
- On the job she runs a crew. She sounds like it, in colors, directions and counts (Todd, October 8: "Job her is different than her when she's not at the job. Maybe look at the previous chapters too."). Off the job she sounds like she did at the bar, at dinner in Los Gatos, at the taco truck and on Summit Road ("Denise bet me twenty dollars it was Napa."; the twenty-six cans of paint, "In case"). Her short answers off the job are for when she is guarding herself.
- One trade comparison at a time, and never to decorate.
- Her reasons stop at the first kiss in chapter 9, at eight.

### How Cal sounds, added

- He is never vague about when or how a thing started.
- He reports what he sees and what his hands do. He does not tally what someone did not do, gloss a tone, or sum up his own state.
- He does not use her trade words. He has booked an estimate, so that one he has.
- He is literal about instructions: told "next time, ask," he asks the next time.

## Jo Silveira, 44

Jo owns Silveira Moving & Estate in South San Francisco: eleven employees, three trucks, her father's name on the doors since 1987. She has been divorced three years, has Owen every other week, and lives in San Bruno. She had Owen at 35 ("I kept meaning to get to it"). She is three years older than Cal, on purpose. She narrates the odd chapters.

**Core: the woman who can carry anything, and leans on nothing that can go** (Todd, October 10; deepened October 16, and at 10:31 PM: "She saw all those women who's husband died or got a divorce and she saw how they didn't have a career."). She is so good at handling things alone that nobody has ever carried anything for her. She has moved the same woman her whole working life, and the wife tells the mover: the one who stayed home, raised the kids, kept the house and helped him get where he was going, and who, when he leaves for someone younger, is past fifty with no pay stubs and no credit in her own name. Jo hears it and never sums it up (29, October 17): at seven from the Millbrae wife venting to Frank while Jo wraps glasses, and in April from a San Carlos wife on her own stairs ("Nineteen years I kept his books." "I said, from when?"), who gets her father's "Yes, ma'am." She ends up in a one-bedroom by the freeway or her sister's back room, with what won't fit in a storage unit she pays for by the month, and the kids have Christmas at his place. (No paycheck and no credit, never no money: a California wife gets half the community property.) It started at seven, on her first job with her father, a divorce in Millbrae: the husband sat in his car at the curb all morning, the son, about sixteen, carried his own boxes out to the trunk, and the wife put her hand on everything that was hers and told Frank all of it (the hygienist, twenty-six; "He told me I didn't need to work. Fifteen years I didn't need to work."; no pay stub since Nixon; every card in his name; Foster City, towels; Scott going with him). In the emptied front room Jo lay on her back in the square where the couch had been, and it was the most peaceful she had ever felt; when she came out, the car was gone. That afternoon the wife's things went into her sister's back bedroom and garage in Daly City, the boy's trophies on top, and on the way home Frank said, "What you hear in a house stays in the house." Her mother hadn't had a paycheck since Jo was born, which is why the parish fed them the winter Frank's knee went. Her rule: lean on nothing that can go, and nobody puts her name on anything but her (ch 13). Nobody can move her out of a house she has paid off herself; she was going to have it at 55 and then lie down. She walks every house she empties last, by herself. What she hears in a house stays in the house (Frank's rule, 29). She respects those women; what she fears is their position. Seeded in 13 (Dawn sent her some of the wives, back when she practiced law) and 25 (the review: the bed made up, the wall under the window); on the page in her own head in 29, where line seven and line eleven of his list turn out to be those wives; told to Cal in 34.

### How Jo sounds

- Short statements with numbers, sizes, hours and dollars in them.
- She measures by eye and says the measurement. The painting is sixteen feet.
- Her comparisons come from the job: a truck topping a hill, a couch through a doorway, what a customer hears when the truck is full.
- Affection comes out as a practical question. "What does she want to build?"
- She does not console. She says the true thing and gives the person something to do. She corrects what is in front of her and never explains a person to himself.
- She keeps count: his slips against the cue card, her reasons not to like him and which column each goes in.
- She says "I've got it." She never says "I need" until chapter 35.
- Her jokes are facts said flat. "I own six ladders." She does not always answer. Sometimes she looks at him, or says "Huh," and goes back to work.
- She never describes her own looks. Other people's reactions do it.

### Cal's engine (October 17)

Built on the belief-engine method at Todd's request (8:33 AM: "We have this for Jo, I'm not sure we do for cal"; 8:50 AM: "Ok, I can go with that."), from what Cal has already done on the page.

- **Core belief:** me showing up is not enough; the right thing, delivered, is.
- **Where it came from:** the report cards. A good one on the kitchen table ended his mother's nine-day silences. What reached her was what he brought, never him.
- **How it has paid off:** Recall, forty million paying users and eleven billion dollars; his mother's house paid off in 2012; everything he sends arrives, on time and correct.
- **Coping mechanism:** his rule, *if it matters, assign it to someone good*: Janice, Remy, a matchmaker, the counselor Janice booked.
- **Counter-belief:** you come yourself, empty-handed, and you stay when there is nothing to fix. Jo holds it; she carries the heavy end herself and pays for her own beer. Darlene held it from the other side: she opened none of his thirty-four boxes, put $612 in a shoebox every month, and left a last voicemail, 47 seconds, that Remy summarized as "asks you to call when you have a minute. Not urgent."
- **What it costs its holders:** Jo comes herself to everything, so nobody has ever carried anything for her. Darlene waited for a call.
- **On an ordinary day:** the standing order that sent his mother boxes three times a year; Janice and Remy handling Abby, who calls him an account manager; each of the eleven women given a large gift, and each sending a thank-you that his office answered; for Jo, Janice's messages, the car, the airship and the mortgage.
- **Decision logic:** when someone he loves is in trouble or goes quiet, he sends the result instead of himself, even when it breaks a promise and costs him the person.
- **Second want:** a wife before Abby leaves, against staying the man who can make anything work. The two collide at the keynote in 36.
- **The sentence he cannot say:** "I am sorry," with nothing to fix after it.
- **Line twelve, "Says what she wants"** (Todd, October 18, replacing "Doesn't go quiet": "It needs to be something else that's deeper"): what nobody in his life has given him and what he cannot build. His mother never said it in her nine days; she said it at last on the voicemail he did not play for ten months ("You, Calvin. Not Janice."). Stephanie said it once ("lonely"), and he booked a counselor. The eleven wrote thank-you notes instead. Jo cannot: at Carmel he asks "What do you want?" and she shuts her mouth on it (27); she says it in 35 ("I need you to stay"); in the epilogue the twelfth stands and she uses it. Dawn checked it for Joanna on May 4. Remy's revised list in 30 ends "Does not say what she wants." Going quiet stays the family's (Kevin, Owen, Frank, Darlene, and Jo in 29 to 31); the eleven are not part of it.
- **Pressure 1, a problem it cannot solve:** Jo will not take what he sends. The car, a truck, the transmission: "I've got it."
- **Pressure 2, people who contradict it:** Jo, who comes herself; Owen ("You came, though."); Frank, who hands him the other end of a pad.
- **Pressure 3, the belief causing the loss:** the payoff, the right thing delivered, is what makes Jo go quiet, and her silence is his mother's nine days again.
- **The kicker** (Todd, October 17: "All along Jo has been trying to get him to show the real him instead of hiding behind Remy and Janice and his money. The kicker I think is the paying off the mortgage is the real him and she rejects that whuch hirts him. She thinks its aboutnthe money amd its not."). The payoff is the real him, chosen from what he knew of her: the day off Owen told him she would take when the house was paid, the ridge and the party. Remy had three options ready and he did not open them, so it is the first month-four gift he chose himself (Remy picked the other eleven). The zeppelin, the bifana and 2012 were his own picks too. It is still the result sent in place of asking. She takes it as money, and as the present he always gives before the end, and it hurts him. She thinks he took her party for the bank; he knew it was hers (18) and moved it to August anyway. From outside it is the same gift as 2012, which is why he cannot answer Abby in 30.
- **His heart, hidden** (Todd, October 17, 9:20 AM: "I think cal has big heart. It's hard for him to show. Maybe we can give him a charity that he cares about?"): every June from 2012 to 2020, on the last day of school, he bought a cashier's check at the bank on El Camino and mailed it to the district office himself. ## Remy

Remy is the always-on agent the big companies promised, and it never understands anything it does. Treat it as a voice with strict limits.

On a screen:

- A header with its name and the time, then short lines of counts and times.
- It ends on a question he can answer with one word. "Confirm?"
- "Tonight: scheduled 45 min. Actual 3 hr 05 min." "Prior instances: none."

Out loud:

- A pleasant voice. They tested forty.
- One short sentence at a time. "Done." "Booked." "All three?"
- It tells him what it did and what happens next, and stops.

Who calls it what: Cal says "it," always, because he built it and knows what it is. His users say "he" or "she" in about equal numbers, and the woman at the bar says "she." Janice says "Remy thinks." Jo says "your robot" and "that thing."

Its name: Cal named it after his boyhood dog, Remy, whom he had named at five after his father, Jeremy (Cal, above). In public the name is "short and easy to spell," and the press thinks it is Remington Drive. The first thing it ever did was wait on hold for his mother (34).

What it never does:

- It never says what is good, wise or right.
- It never says "I think" or "I feel."
- It never acts against him. It proposes, or carries on with what he approved, and he agrees or fails to stop it.
- It never reads Jo's mail or tracks her. It works from what is his and what anyone can see.
- It never moves money without him confirming.

Its four settings for anything it might do: do it, do it if he has said yes before, ask first, leave it to him. The book ends with Abby, Jo and Owen set to the last one.

What it makes is polished and could be for anyone: a song about a dog, a birthday video, an apology in his own voice.

Lines it says over the book: "Relationship 12. Average duration: 4.1 months." "Unanswered: 9 days." "No precedent found."

## Darlene and Pancake

Darlene Brennan is never on the page alive, so her house has to speak for her. She died on October 8 last year, at 71. She worked the line at Libby's cannery in Sunnyvale until it closed in 1985, and then for thirty years the cafeteria line at the middle school across the street, where she handed Cal his tray while he was a student. She hid that she left school at sixteen. From 2012 until she retired in 2015 she was at the register on the last day of school when every lunch balance under zero was paid off; Cal does not know whether she knew it was him (ch 34).

What is in her house:

- Picture frames that still hold the stock photos they were sold with.
- A sugar bowl with pebbles under the sugar, so it always looked full.
- Every gift he ever sent, thirty-four boxes, unopened, with the receipts, stacked square by date in the closet across from the hall bathroom (ch 24).

When she was hurt she stopped speaking, sometimes for nine days. The first time was the week Jeremy left, in 1990, and she never said why he went. She let Cal name the shelter dog after him and called the dog in from the yard every night for seven years. The Mother's Day box that came after she died held a video-call tablet set up with one contact, *Office of C. Brennan* (34). Her last voicemail is on his phone.

Pancake is his dog: a cream golden retriever, eleven years old, sixty-four pounds, named by Abby when she was seven, over Cal's objection (ch 2). He is not Cal's first dog: Cal has had one since Remy died in 1997. He can no longer manage the last three steps, so Cal carries him. He inspects the same fence post every morning. He leans on the one person in a room who would rather he didn't. He lives. Cal loves this dog and shows it only in what he does: the walk at five, carrying him up the steps and telling no one, letting him ride with his chin on his shoulder, watering him from his hands. He ended the one date that asked him to give the dog up. Jo is not a woman who dislikes dogs. She was bitten at 26 and is afraid of them, says "I'm not good with dogs," and makes room for this one anyway.
